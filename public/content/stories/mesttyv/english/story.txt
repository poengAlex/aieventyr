Once there was a poor cottager who had three sons. He had no inheritance to give them and nothing to set them to, and he did not know what to do with them either; so he said they might take up whatever they liked best and travel wherever they pleased. He would gladly walk with them a bit on the road, and he did. He followed them until they came to a place where three roads met; there each took his own road, and the father bade them farewell and went home again. What became of the two eldest I have never been able to find out, but the youngest walked both far and long.

One night, as he was crossing a great forest, such wicked weather came on him: it blew and drifted so he could scarcely keep his eyes open, and before he knew a word of it he had wandered clean off the track and could find neither road nor path. But however he went, at last he saw a light far off among the trees. He thought he must try to reach it, and after a long while he did. There stood a great building, and the fire burned so bright and warm inside that he could tell they had not gone to bed yet. So he went in, and there an old crone was bustling about, tidying.

“Good evening, Mother,” said the lad.

“Good evening,” said the crone.

“Whew! It’s dreadful weather out tonight,” said the lad.

“It is that,” said the crone.

“May I lie here and have shelter for the night?” the lad asked.

“It’s no good for you to lie here,” said the crone, “for if the folk come home and find you, they’ll kill both you and me.”

“What sort of folk live here, then?” said the lad.

“Oh, robbers—riffraff like that,” said the crone. “They stole me away when I was little and have kept me as housekeeper for them,” she said.

“I think I’ll lie here all the same,” said the lad. “It must go as it will, for I’m not going back out in weather like this in the middle of the night.”

“Well, it’ll be worst for you,” said the crone.

The lad lay down in a bed that stood there, but he dared not sleep; and just as it was, the robbers came. The crone told them a stranger fellow had come who she could not get rid of.

“Did you see whether he had any money?” said the robbers.

“He—money, the poor wretch!” said the crone. “He’s got no more than the clothes on his back.”

The robbers began whispering together about what they should do with him—whether they should kill him or what else. Meanwhile the lad got up and began talking with them, asking if they needed a serving-boy, for he would not mind serving with them.

“Yes,” they said, “if you’ve a mind to it, and you’ll take up the craft we practise, you can have service here.”

“Yes, it’s all one to me what craft it is,” said the lad, “for when I left home my father gave me leave to take up whatever I liked.”

“Have you a mind to steal?” said the robbers.

“Oh yes,” said the lad; that craft couldn’t be dangerous to learn, he thought.

Now there lived a man not far from them who owned three oxen. One of them he meant to drive to town and sell, and the robbers had heard of it. So they told the lad that if he was good for stealing the ox from the man on the road, so that the man did not know it, and without doing him any harm, then he might be their serving-boy.

So the lad took a fine shoe that stood there, with a silver buckle on it, and set off. He laid the shoe in the road where the man would pass with his ox, and then went into the forest and hid under a bush. When the man came, he saw the shoe at once.

“That’s a handsome shoe,” said he. “If I had its mate, I’d take it home—then maybe I’d get my wife in a good mood for once!” For he had a wife so angry and so wicked there was never long between the times she thrashed him. But he thought he could do nothing with one shoe when he didn’t have the other, so he drove on and left it lying.

The lad took up the shoe and hurried as best he could through the wood to get ahead of the man, and then set the shoe in the road before him again. When the man came there with his ox, he was angry that he had been so stupid as to leave the mate behind and not take it with him.

“I’ll have to run back and fetch the other,” he said to himself, and tied the ox by a fence-rail. “Then I’ll have a pair of good shoes for my wife—maybe she’ll dare to be kind for once, she will.”

He went searching and searching, far and wide, but he found no shoe; at last he had to go back with just the one he had. Meanwhile the lad had taken the ox and made off with it. When the man came back and saw the ox was gone, he began to cry and carry on, for he was afraid his wife would beat him to death when she learned the ox was lost.

But then it came into his head to go home and take the second ox and drive that to town and get good money for it, and not let his wife know a word.

He did so: he went home and took the ox so the wife knew nothing of it, and drove toward town. But the robbers knew it well enough—for they had their signs out—and they told the lad that if he could take that one too, without the man knowing it and without harming him, then he should be their equal. Yes, that would be no great thing, the lad thought.

This time he took a rope and hung himself up under the arms, right in the middle of the road before the man. When the man came driving his ox and saw the “ghost” hanging there, he felt queer.

“Well, you must have brooded heavy, to hang yourself there!” he said. “Ah well, you may hang for me—I can’t blow life into you again.” And he went on with the ox. Down hopped the lad from the tree, cut through the wood, got ahead of him, and hung himself in the road before him again.

“Have you truly brooded so heavy you’ve hung yourself there, or is it only spooking me?” said the man. “Ah well, hang for me—whether you’re a ghost or what you are,” said he, and went on with the ox. The lad did just the same as the two times before: down he hopped, cut a short way through the trees, and hung himself in the road again. When the man saw it a third time, he said to himself:

“This is dreadful! Could they have brooded so heavy that they’ve hanged themselves, all three of them? No, I can’t believe it’s anything but spooking meant for me. But now I’ll know for certain: if the other two are hanging there as well, then it’s true; and if they’re not, then it’s nothing but a spectre.”

So he tied his ox and ran back to see if they truly hung there. While he went peering up into every tree, the lad hopped down, took the ox, and went off with it.

When the man came back and saw the ox was gone, it went badly with him, as you may well know: he both cried and carried on. But at last he calmed himself and thought: there’s no other counsel than to go home and take the third one, without the wife knowing it, and try to sell it all the better so I get many pennies for it. He went home, took the third ox, and set out with it so the wife knew nothing. But the robbers knew again, and they told the lad that if he could steal the third as neatly as the other two, he would be master over them all.

So the lad set off and sprang into the forest, and when the man came driving the ox, the lad began bellowing terribly—like a great ox—inside the wood. When the man heard it he was glad, for he thought he knew the voice of his big bullock and believed he would find both the stolen oxen again. So he tied the third, ran off the road, and began searching in the forest. Meanwhile the lad drove away with the third ox. When the man came back and had lost that one too, he was so wretched there was no measure to it; he wept and wailed, and he dared not go home for many days, for he feared his wife would beat him dead outright. The robbers were none too pleased either, to be forced to say the lad was master over them all.

Then they thought they would try him at something he could not manage; so one day they all went out on the road and left him alone at home.

The first thing he did, once they were well away, was to drive the oxen out onto the road so they went home again to the man they had been stolen from; and glad the farmer was, you may be sure. Then he took all the horses the robbers had, loaded on them the best he could find—gold and silver and clothes and other fine things—and told the crone to greet the robbers and thank them for him: now he was off, and they would have trouble catching him again. And with that he rode away.

After a long time he came onto the same road he had taken when he first wandered to the robbers. When he came near home, where his father lived, he put on a uniform he had found among the loot—made like a general’s—and drove into the yard like some great lord. Then he went in and asked whether he might have lodging there.

No, he could not have it at all. “How should I lend house-room to such a great gentleman as you?” said the man. “I’ve scarcely enough clothes to lie on myself, and poor enough they are.”

“You’ve always been hard,” said the lad, “and you’re hard still, since you won’t lend your own son a roof.”

“Are you my son?” said the man.

“Don’t you know me again?” said the lad.

And then he knew him well enough. “But what have you taken up, that you’ve become such a man in so short a time?” said the father.

“I’ll tell you,” said the lad. “You said I might take up whatever I liked, so I apprenticed myself to thieves and robbers. And now I’ve served out my time and become a master thief.”

Now there lived a bailiff close by the cottage where his father lived, with such a great estate and so much money he did not know the number of it; and he had a daughter too, who was fair and kind and good and clever. Her the master thief wanted, and he told his father to go to the bailiff and ask for the daughter for him.

“If he asks what trade I follow, you may say I am a master thief,” he said.

“I think you’ve gone out of your wits,” said the father, “for sensible you can’t be if you think of such madness.”

But go he must and ask for the bailiff’s daughter—there was no counsel against it, the lad said.

“I won’t go to the bailiff and speak for you,” said the father. “He’s so rich and has so much money.”

There was no help for it, the master thief thought: he should go, whether he wished or not; and if he would not go kindly, then he would make him go unkindly. The father still would not, and the lad followed him and threatened him with a great birch cudgel, so that he came crying straight in through the bailiff’s door.

“Well, my man, what ails you?” said the bailiff.

So the father told how he had had three sons who had gone off one day, and he had promised they might travel where they liked and take up what they liked too—“and now the youngest has come home, and he has threatened me into coming to you to ask for your daughter for him and to say he is a master thief,” said the man, crying and carrying on.

“Comfort yourself, my man,” said the bailiff, laughing. “And you can tell him from me that he must prove it first. If he can steal the roast from the spit in the kitchen on Sunday, while they’re all watching it, then he shall have my daughter. Tell him that!”

The father did so, and the lad thought it would be an easy matter. He got hold of three live hares, put them in a sack, and hung on himself such rags that he looked so miserable and tattered it was pitiful. Then he sneaked into the entry like a rag-boy with his sack on Sunday forenoon. The bailiff himself and all the household were in the kitchen to watch the roast.

After a while the lad let out one hare, and it shot off and flew about out in the yard.

“Look at that hare!” said the folk in the kitchen, and wanted to run out and catch it. The bailiff saw it too. “Oh, let it run,” he said. “There’s no use thinking of taking a hare on the leap.”

It was not long before the lad let out the second, and they saw it in the kitchen and thought it was the same one; they wanted to run out and catch it again, but the bailiff said it was no use. But after a while the lad let out the third as well, and it set off and ran around in the yard; they saw it from the kitchen too and thought it was the same one coming again, and then they would go out and catch it.

“That’s a queer hare, the way it scampers and stumbles,” said the bailiff. “Come, let’s see if we can lay hands on it.” Out he went, and the others with him, and the hare first and they after, as orderly as you please.

But while they were gone, the master thief took the roast and slipped off with it. Where the bailiff got himself a roast for dinner that day I do not know, but I do know he got no hare-roast, though he ran himself both sweaty and weary.

At dinner the parson came there, and when the bailiff had told him the trick the master thief had played, the parson mocked him beyond all measure.

“I can’t see how it could happen that I should be fooled by such a fellow,” said the parson.

“Just you watch yourself,” said the bailiff. “Maybe he’ll be with you before you know a word of it.”

But the parson held to his own and kept jeering at the bailiff for letting himself be led by the nose.

Toward afternoon the master thief came and wanted the bailiff’s daughter, which he had been promised.

“You must do more trials first,” said the bailiff, wheedling him. “What you did today was nothing much. Could you not play the parson a proper trick? He sits there jeering at me because I let myself be fooled by such a man.”

Yes, that would be no great thing, the master thief said.

He dressed himself up like a bird, took a great white sheet over him, broke the wings off a goose and fastened them to his back, and climbed up into a big maple that stood in the parson’s garden. When the parson came home that evening, the lad began crying: “Master Lars! Master Lars!”—for the parson was named Master Lars.

“Who is it calling me?” said the parson.

“I am an angel sent from the Lord to proclaim to you that you shall be taken up alive into the Kingdom of God for your godliness,” said the master thief. “Next Monday evening you must hold yourself ready to travel, for then I will come and fetch you in a sack, and all your gold and silver and whatever you have of this world’s vanity you shall lay together in a heap in your great room.”

Master Lars both thanked and fell on his knees before the “angel,” and the Sunday after he preached a farewell sermon and spoke of how an angel from God had come in the great maple in his garden and proclaimed that he should go alive into the Kingdom of God for his godliness’ sake. And he preached and explained it so that all in the church wept, both old and young.

On Monday the master thief came again as an angel, and the parson fell on his knees and thanked before he was stuffed into the sack; and when he was well inside, the master thief dragged him along over stock and stone.

“Oh! Ow!” cried the parson in the sack. “Where does this carry me?”

“It is the narrow road that leads to the Kingdom of Heaven,” said the master thief, and hauled him on so that he nearly beat him to death.

At last he threw him into the bailiff’s goose-house, and the geese began hissing and pinching him so he was more dead than alive.

“Ow, ow, ow! Where am I now?” said the parson.

“Now you are in purgatory, to be cleansed and refined for eternal life,” said the master thief, and went his way, and took all the gold and silver and the fine things the parson had gathered in his great room.

In the morning, when the goose-girl came to let the geese out, she heard the parson lying in the sack and moaning inside the goose-house.

“In Jesus’ name, who are you, and what ails you?” she said.

“Oh, if you are an angel from Heaven, then let me out and let me come back to earth again, for here is worse than hell; the little devils are pinching me with tongs!” said the parson.

“I’m no angel, God help us,” said the girl, and helped the parson out of the sack. “I just mind the bailiff’s geese, I do—and it’s them little devils that have pinched your reverence.”

“Oh, the master thief has done this! Oh, my gold and my silver and my fine clothes!” screamed the parson, and carried on and ran home so fast the goose-girl thought he’d gone clean out of his senses on the spot.

When the bailiff learned how it had gone with the parson—how he had been on the narrow road and in purgatory—he laughed till he was near bursting. But when the master thief came and wanted the bailiff’s daughter, which he had been promised, the bailiff wheedled him again and said:

“You must do a trial that’s better, so I can truly see what you’re worth. I have twelve horses in the stable,” he said, “and I will set twelve men on them, one on each. If you are good for stealing the horses from under them, then I’ll see what I can do.”

“Yes, that would be a way,” said the master thief. “If I get your daughter for it, then—”

“If you can do it, I’ll do my best,” said the bailiff.

So the master thief went to the shop and bought spirits in two pocket-flasks. In one he brewed a sleeping-draught, and in the other he had plain brandy; and then he hired eleven fellows to sit behind the bailiff’s barn at night. For fair words and payment he borrowed a ragged skirt and jacket from an old crone, took a staff in his hand and a pouch on his back, and when evening drew near he hobbled off toward the bailiff’s stable. When he got there, the stable-lads were watering the horses for the night and had plenty to do.

“What the devil do you want?” said one of the lads to the “crone.”

“Oh, brr! It’s so cold!” said the “crone,” shivering and trembling and making a great to-do. “So cold a poor wretch might freeze to death! Oh Lord God, may I be allowed to sit just inside the stable door?” she said.

“You may have the devil instead! Be off at once—if the bailiff sees you here we’ll catch it,” said one.

“Oh, the old broken hook,” said another, who felt sorry for her. “That hook can sit there; she surely won’t do any harm.”

The others said it should not be, but as they argued and tended the horses, she edged farther into the stable, so that in the end she sat behind the door, and after that no one paid her any mind.

When the night wore on, it grew cold for the lads to sit astride the horses.

“Brr! It’s devilish cold,” said one, flapping his arms.

“Yes, I’m shaking with it,” said another.

“If only someone had a bit of tobacco,” said a third.

One had a plug; he shared it, though it was little enough per man, and they chewed and spat. It helped a bit, but before long it was just as bad again.

“Brr!” said one, shuddering.

“Brr!” said the “crone,” chattering her teeth so they rattled. Then she took up a flask of plain brandy, and her hand shook so the liquor sloshed inside, and she drank till it went glug down her throat.

“What have you got in your flask, old woman?” said one of the stable-lads.

“Oh, it’s just a little drop of brandy, father,” she said.

“Brandy! What’s that? Let me have a sip! Let me have a sip!” all twelve shouted over one another.

“Oh, it’s so little I have,” whined the “crone,” “it won’t even wet your mouth.”

But they would have it and they must have it. So she took the flask with the sleeping-draught and put it to the mouth of the first; and then she no longer trembled but poured steady, so each got what he needed. And before the twelfth had drunk, the first was already snoring. Then the master thief threw off the beggar-rags and took one lad after another, easy as anything, and sat them straddle-legged on the stall-partitions. He called up his eleven men and drove off with the bailiff’s horses.

When the bailiff came out to look to the lads at daybreak, they began to “freshen themselves up”: they dug their spurs into the partitions so the splinters flew, and some tumbled down, and some hung and sat there like fools.

“Oh yes,” said the bailiff, “I can well see who’s been here. But you’re a sorry lot, sitting there and letting the master thief steal the horses from under your very backs!” And he had them flogged for not keeping better watch.

Later in the day the master thief came again, told how he had managed it, and wanted the bailiff’s daughter, as he had been promised. But the bailiff gave him a hundred dollars and said he must do something better still.

“Do you think you can steal the horse from under me while I’m out riding it?” said the bailiff.

“Oh, that would be a way,” said the master thief. “If I get your daughter for it.”

So the bailiff said that one day he would ride out on a great drill-field.

The master thief quickly got hold of an old worn-out mare, made a harness of withies and broom-twigs, bought a broken old cart and a great barrel, and then told an old beggar-woman he would give her ten dollars if she would lie inside the barrel and gape at the bung-hole. He would stick his finger in there—nothing bad would happen to her, she only had to ride a little—and if he took his finger out more than once, she should have ten dollars more each time. The master thief himself put on rags, blackened his face, and set on a wig and a great beard of goat’s wool so no one could know him. Then he went off to the field, where the bailiff had been riding about for some time already.

When he came there, he went so slow and so stubbornly that he scarcely moved from the spot; it crept and crept, then stood still, then crept a little again. It looked so wretched that the bailiff never thought it could be the master thief. He rode right up and asked if the fellow had seen anyone prowling about in the wood.

“No,” said the man, “he’d seen no one at all.”

“Listen now,” said the bailiff. “If you’ll ride into the wood and look well around for anyone lurking there, you may borrow my horse, and you’ll get a good drink-money for the trouble.”

“No, I can’t,” said the man, “for I’m going to a wedding with this mead-barrel I’ve fetched, and the tap fell out on the road, and now I have to walk holding my finger in the hole.”

“Ride,” said the bailiff. “I’ll watch both the horse and the barrel.”

So he would; but the fellow begged the bailiff that he must watch sharp and be quick to put his finger in the bung-hole when he took his own out.

Yes, the bailiff said he would do his best, and the master thief mounted the horse.

But time went, and it wore on and wore on, and no one came back. At last the bailiff grew tired of standing there with his finger in the bung-hole and took it out.

“Now I’m to have ten dollars more!” screamed the old woman inside the barrel.

Then he understood how the mead-barrel business had been, and he set off for home. But when he had walked a bit, someone met him leading his horse, for the master thief had already been home to him.

The next day the bailiff came to the master thief and wanted his daughter, as he had been promised. But the bailiff wheedled him again, gave him two hundred dollars, and said he must do one more trial; if he could do it, then he should have her.

The master thief said yes indeed, if only he could hear what it was.

“Do you think you can steal the sheet off our bed—and the shift off my wife?” said the bailiff.

“That’s not impossible,” said the master thief. “If I get your daughter for it, then—”

When night came, the master thief went out and cut down a thief who hung on the gallows, slung him on his back, and carried him along. Then he got hold of a great ladder and set it up to the bailiff’s chamber window. He climbed up and bobbed the dead man up and down, just as though someone stood outside the window peeping in.

“There’s the master thief, Mother!” said the bailiff, nudging his wife. “Now I’ll shoot him,” he said, and took up a rifle he had laid beside the bed.

“Oh no, you mustn’t do that,” said the lady. “You’re the one who summoned him here.”

“Yes, I’ll shoot him, Mother,” he said, and lay aiming and aiming. But sometimes the head was up so he could see a bit of it; then it was gone again. At last he got a proper aim; it cracked, and the dead man fell with a heavy thud on the ground. Down the master thief climbed as fast as he could.

“Yes, I’m the high authority,” said the bailiff, “but folk will soon have something to say, and it would be a shame if they saw this dead man. Best I go out and bury him.”

“Do as you think best, dear,” said the lady.

The bailiff rose and went down, and the moment he had gone out the door, the master thief slipped in and went straight up to the lady.

“Now, dear,” she said—for she thought it was her husband—“are you finished already?”

“Oh yes, I tucked him in a hole,” said he, “and scraped a bit of earth over him so he’s hidden, for it’s weather out there. I’ll do it better later. But let me have the sheet to wipe myself with—he was so bloody, and I’ve soiled myself all over with him.”

And he got it.

“You’ll have to let me have your shift as well,” he said, “for the sheet won’t be enough, I find.” And she gave him that too.

Then the master thief slipped out as softly as a cat and was gone with both sheet and shift. After a while the bailiff came back up, stamping and blowing with the cold.

“Well, dear,” said the lady, “did you get dried off properly? You took both the sheet and my shift away with you.”

The bailiff stopped short.

“The sheet—and your shift?” he said. “Then I’ve been fooled again!”

In the morning the master thief came and laid the sheet and the shift on the table before the bailiff, and asked for the daughter he had been promised.

The bailiff stood a long time, turning it over in his mind, but there was no way out; he had given his word, and a word was a word. So he let the master thief have his daughter, and there was a wedding that could be heard over hills and through woods.

And the master thief did not steal any more, not so far as anyone could prove; for now he had got what he wanted, and he lived well with his wife, and his father never lacked either bread or clothes for the rest of his days.
