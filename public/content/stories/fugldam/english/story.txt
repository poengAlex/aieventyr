There was once a king who had twelve daughters, and he loved them so dearly that he would always have them close about him. Yet every midday, when the king lay down for his nap, the princesses would slip out to take a walk. One day, while the king slept and the princesses were out, they vanished all at once and never came back.

Then there was great sorrow throughout the whole land, and the king grieved most of all. He sent word out through his own kingdom and into foreign ones, and had the priests proclaim it from every church and every bell in the country rung for them; but the king’s daughters were gone and stayed gone, and no one knew what had become of them. So people understood that they must have been taken into the mountain.

It was not long before this was talked of far and wide, in towns and in the countryside, across many districts and even into foreign lands, and so the rumor reached a king far away who had twelve sons. When they heard of the twelve princesses, they begged their father for leave to go and search for them. He was reluctant to let them go; he feared he would never see them again. But they fell to their knees and pleaded so long that at last he had to allow it.

He fitted out a ship for them and set Sir Red as their helmsman, for he was well known on the sea.

They sailed for a long, long time, and in every land they came to they went ashore to seek and ask after the princesses; but they could neither hear nor learn anything. Now only a few days remained before they would have been sailing for seven years. Then one day a fierce storm rose, such weather that they thought they would never reach land again. All hands had to work so hard that no one got a wink of sleep as long as the gale lasted.

But toward the third day the wind fell, and all at once there was a dead calm. They were so worn out by the labor and the hard weather that they dropped off at once; all of them, that is, except the youngest king’s son, who had no peace in him and could not sleep at all.

As he paced back and forth on deck, the ship came near a small island, and on the island ran a little dog, yelping and snarling at the ship as if it wanted to get aboard. The prince leaned over and called and coaxed and whistled to it; the more he did, the more it barked and snapped. He thought it a pity to leave it there to perish, for he believed it might have come from some ship that had been wrecked in the storm. Yet he did not think he could help it either, for he doubted he could lower the boat alone, and the others slept so deeply that he would not wake them for the sake of a dog.

But the sea was smooth and still. So he thought, I’ll go ashore anyway and save it. He tried to lower the boat, and it went easier than he had expected. He rowed to land and went up to the dog, but every time he reached for it, the dog sprang aside. So it went on until, before he knew what was happening, he had followed it right into a great fine castle.

There the dog changed into a lovely princess, and on a bench sat a man so huge and ugly that the prince was horrified.

“You’ve no need to be afraid,” said the man—and the prince grew even more afraid when he heard the sound of his voice. “For I know well what you want. There are twelve princes of you, and you’re searching for the twelve princesses who disappeared. I know where they are: they’re with my master. There they sit, each on her golden chair, combing and stroking him, for he has twelve heads. You’ve been sailing for seven years, but you’ll sail seven years more before you find them.

“You might stay here,” he went on, “and have my daughter—but first you must kill him. He’s a harsh lord to us, and we’re all weary of him; and when he is dead, I will be king in his place. Now try whether you can swing the sword,” said the troll.

The prince took hold of an old rusty sword that hung on the wall, but he could scarcely budge it.

“Then you must take a swallow from this bottle,” said the troll.

When he had done so, he could stir the sword; after another swallow he could lift it; and after yet another he could swing it as lightly as a baker’s peel.

“When you go back aboard,” said the troll prince, “you must hide the sword well in your bunk, so Sir Red does not see it. He may not be good for swinging it, but he’ll still turn spiteful toward you and lie in wait for your life.

“And when seven years are nearly up—within three days—things will happen just as now: a strong storm will come upon you, and when it has passed you will all grow sleepy. Then you must take the sword and row ashore. You will come to a castle, and there will stand every kind of guard—wolves and bears and lions—but you must not fear them, for they will all fall at your feet. When you go inside, you will see him sitting in a splendid chamber, decked out in finery; but he has twelve heads, and the princesses sit each on her golden chair, stroking one head apiece. You may be sure they have no liking for that work. Then you must hurry and hew off one head after another. If he wakes and sees you, he will swallow you alive.”

The prince went aboard with the sword, keeping well in mind all he had been told. The others still lay sleeping, and he hid the sword in his bunk so that neither Sir Red nor any of the rest might see it. Then the wind picked up again, and he roused the others and said it seemed a shame to sleep longer now that there was such a good breeze. No one noticed that he had been away.

When the seven years were nearly done, within three days, it went just as the troll had said. A great storm and gale came, and it lasted three days. When it had passed, all of them grew drowsy from the labor and lay down; but the youngest king’s son rowed ashore. The guards fell at his feet, and so he came to the castle.

When he entered the chamber, the great troll lay asleep, just as the troll prince had said, and the twelve princesses sat each on her chair, stroking one head apiece. The prince beckoned to the princesses to move aside. They pointed at the troll and waved him away, signaling that he should go and save himself; but he kept making signs that they should move, and then they understood that he meant to rescue them. Quietly, one by one, they slipped aside, and as quickly he struck off the troll king’s heads, one after the other, until at last the blood ran like a great brook.

When the troll was killed, he rowed back to the ship and hid the sword again. He felt he had done enough, and since he could not manage to dispose of the body alone, he wanted the others to help a little too. So he woke them and told them it was a shame for them to lie and sleep while he had found the king’s daughters and freed them from the troll.

They laughed at him and said he had surely slept as well as they, if not better, and dreamed he was such a fellow; if anyone had rescued the princesses, it was more likely one of them had done it. But the youngest prince told them how it had all happened, and when they followed him ashore and first saw the stream of blood, and then the castle and the troll and the twelve heads and the princesses, they saw that he had spoken true. Then they helped to heave the heads and the body into the sea.

All were glad, but none were gladder than the king’s daughters, who were spared from sitting all day stroking the troll. From all the gold and silver and costly things in the castle they took as much as the ship could carry, and then they all went aboard together, princes and princesses.

But when they had sailed some way out to sea, the princesses said that in their joy they had forgotten their golden crowns. They lay in a cupboard, and they dearly wished to have them. Since none of the others would go back for them, the youngest prince said, “Well, I’ve risked so much already that I can go for the crowns too—if you’ll lower the sails and wait till I return.” Yes, they would.

But when he had gone so far that they could no longer see him, Sir Red—who wanted to be the chief one and have the youngest princess for himself—said it was no use lying still and waiting, for they could be sure he would never come back. They knew, said he, that the king had given him, Sir Red, power and authority, so he could sail when he pleased; and they were to say that he had rescued the princesses, and if anyone said otherwise, he would lose his life. The princes dared do nothing but what Sir Red wished, and so they sailed away.

Meanwhile the youngest prince rowed ashore, went up into the castle, found the cupboard with the golden crowns, and tugged and hauled until he got it down into the boat. But when he came to where he could see the ship, it was gone. When he saw it nowhere on any side, he understood well enough how it had happened. To row after it was useless, and he had to turn back to land again.

He was afraid to be alone in the castle overnight, but there was no other house to be had, so he took heart, locked every door and gate, and lay down in a room where a bed stood made up. Yet he was fearful—and more fearful still after he had lain a while, when the walls and roof began to creak and crash as if the whole castle would split apart. All at once something thudded down beside the bed like a load of hay.

Then it was still again, and he heard a voice telling him not to be afraid, saying:

“I am Bird Dam,
and I will help you on.
But the first thing when you wake tomorrow,
you must go to the storehouse and fetch me four barrels of rye.
I must have that for my life until noon,
or else I can do nothing.”

When he woke, he saw a monstrous great bird, with a feather at the back of its neck as thick as a half-grown fir beam. The prince went to the storehouse and fetched four barrels of rye for Bird Dam, and when it had eaten them, it asked him to hang the cupboard with the golden crowns on one side of its neck, and take as much gold and silver as would weigh the same and hang it on the other. Then it told him to climb onto its back and hold tight to the neck-feather.

Off they went, whistling through the air, and it was not long before they flew past the ship. The prince wanted to go aboard for the sword, for he feared someone might see it, and the troll had said that must not happen. But Bird Dam said he would have to let it be.

“Sir Red won’t get sight of it,” said the bird. “But if you go aboard, he’ll be after your life, for he wants the youngest princess. Yet you may be easy about her: every night she lays a naked sword before her in the bed.”

At last they came to the troll prince, and the king’s son was welcomed so well there was no end to it. The troll prince did not know what good he might do him, because he had killed his master and made him king; he would gladly have given him his daughter and half the land and kingdom. But the prince had taken such a liking to the youngest of the twelve king’s daughters that he had no rest, and wanted to set out again and again. The troll begged him to be patient a while yet, and said there were nearly seven years of sailing before they came home.

Of the princess he said the same as Bird Dam. “You can be sure about her; she lays a naked sword before her in the bed. And if you don’t believe me,” said the troll, “you can go aboard when they sail past here, and see for yourself and fetch the sword—since I must have it back anyway.”

When they sailed by, there had again been foul weather, and when the prince came aboard, they were all asleep. Each princess lay with her prince, but the youngest lay alone with a naked sword laid before her in the bed, and on the floor in front of it lay Sir Red. The prince took the sword and rowed back to land again, without anyone noticing he had been aboard.

Still the prince could not be easy, and he often wanted to set out. And when the end of the seven years drew near, with only about three weeks left, the troll king said, “Now you may make ready to travel, since you will not stay with us. You shall have the loan of my iron boat, which goes of itself if you only say, ‘Boat, go on!’ In the boat is an iron club. You must lift that club when you see the ship straight ahead of you, and they will get such a fair wind that they forget to look for you. When you come alongside the ship, lift the club again, and there will be such a storm that they’ll have something else to do than peer after you. And when you have passed them, lift the club the third time—but you must always lay it down gently again, or the weather will be so fierce that you and they will perish. When you reach land, you need not bother about the boat any more; just shove it out and turn it, and say, ‘Boat, go home the way you came!’”

When he left, he was given so much gold and silver, so much other finery, and clothes and linen that the troll princess had sewn for him over the long time, that he was far richer than any of his brothers. He had scarcely sat down in the boat and said, “Boat, go on!” before the boat went. When he saw the ship straight before him, he lifted the club, and they got such a breeze that they forgot to look after him. When he came alongside, he lifted the iron club again, and then there rose such a storm and such weather that white foam stood all around the ship and waves broke over the deck, so they had other work than watching him. When he had passed them, he lifted the club the third time, and then they had so much to do that they had no time at all to see what sort of one he was.

He came to land long, long before the ship. When he had taken all his goods out of the boat, he shoved it out again, turned it about, and said, “Boat, go home the way you came!” and away it went.

Then he disguised himself as a sailor and went up to a wretched hut where an old crone lived. He made her believe he was a poor seaman, that he had been on a great ship that had gone down, and that he was the only one saved; and he begged her to lend him shelter for himself and the things he had rescued.

“God preserve me,” said the woman, “I can hardly give anyone house. You see how it is here: I’ve nothing to lie on myself, and still less for others.”

“That’s all the same,” said the sailor. “If I only get a roof over my head, it doesn’t matter how I lie. You can’t refuse me shelter when I’m willing to put up with what you have.”

That evening he carried his things inside, and he had scarcely gotten them in when the crone—who dearly loved to have news to run with—began asking who he was, where he came from, where he had been, where he was going, what he had with him, what errand he traveled on, and whether he had heard anything of the twelve princesses who had vanished so many years ago, and much more besides, enough to talk of and count up for a long time. But he said he was so battered and had such an aching head from the dreadful weather that he could make sense of nothing. She must let him have peace for a few days, until he had come to himself after the hard work he had endured; then she should learn all she wanted, and more.

The next day the crone began questioning and digging again, but the sailor still had such pain in his head after the storm that he could make sense of nothing. Yet now and then he let slip a word that he did know something about the king’s daughters. At once the crone ran off with what she had heard to every gossiping woman thereabout, and soon one came after another, running in to ask news of the princesses—had he seen them, were they coming soon, were they on the way, and so on. He still complained of his aching head from the weather so he could not explain everything; but he said this much: if they had not been lost in the hard weather that had been, they would arrive in a fortnight, or perhaps sooner. But he could not say for certain whether they were still alive, for he had indeed seen them, but they might well have gone to the bottom since.

One of the women rushed to the king’s manor with this and said that there was a sailor in the hut of such-and-such a crone; he had seen the princesses, and they would likely come in a fortnight, or perhaps in eight days. When the king heard that, he sent for the sailor to come and tell him himself.

“I’m not fit to appear,” said the sailor. “I have no clothes fit to come before him.” But the king’s messenger said he must come all the same; the king would and must speak with him, no matter how he looked, for no one had yet been able to bring him any word of the princesses.

So he went to the king’s manor and came before the king, who asked whether it was true that he had seen anything of the princesses.

“Yes, I have,” said the sailor, “but I cannot know if they still live, for when I saw them the weather was so fierce that we were wrecked. Yet if they still exist, they will likely arrive in a fortnight, and perhaps sooner.”

When the king heard it, he was almost beside himself with joy. And when the time drew near that the sailor had said they would come, the king went down to the shore to meet them in full splendor. There was great rejoicing throughout the land when the ship came in with the princesses and the princes and Sir Red; but none were happier than the old king, who had his daughters back again.

The eleven eldest princesses were glad and merry too; but the youngest, who was to have Sir Red, wept and was always sorrowful. The king disliked this and asked why she was not cheerful like the others. She had nothing to be sullen about now that she had escaped the troll and was to have such a man as Sir Red. But she did not dare say a word, for Sir Red had said he would take the life of anyone who told how it had truly happened.

One day, as they sat sewing their wedding finery, a man came in wearing a great sailor’s coat, with a peddler’s box on his back, and asked whether the princesses would buy finery from him for the wedding. He had so many rare and costly things, in gold and silver. Yes, that might well be.

They looked at his wares, and they looked at him, for they seemed to recognize both him and many of the things he carried.

“Whoever has so much fine stuff,” said the youngest princess, “surely has something finer still—something that would suit us even better.”

That might be, said the peddler.

But the others hushed her and told her to remember what Sir Red had threatened.

Some time later the princesses sat by the window one day, and there came the prince again in his great sailor’s coat, with the cupboard of golden crowns on his back. When he entered the great hall of the king’s manor, he opened the cupboard for the princesses. And when they recognized each her own golden crown, the youngest said, “I think it is right that the one who saved us should have the reward he has earned. And it is not Sir Red, but he who brought our crowns—he is the one who saved us.”

Then the prince threw off the sailor’s coat and stood there finer than all the others. And the old king had Sir Red put to death. Now there was true joy in the king’s house. Each took his own, and then there was a wedding such as was heard of and spoken of across twelve kingdoms.
