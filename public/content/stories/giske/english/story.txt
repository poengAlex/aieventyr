There was once a widower. He had a housekeeper named Giske, and she was eager to have him, and pestered him day in and day out that he should marry her. At last the man grew so sick of it he didn’t know what to do to be rid of her.

It was in that time between mowing and haymaking, when the hemp was ripe and they were to go and pull it. Giske always thought she was so pretty, and so capable and clever, and she pulled hemp till she grew light-headed from the strong smell and fell headlong and lay sleeping out in the hempfield. While she slept, the man came with a pair of shears and clipped off her skirt; then he smeared her over, first with tallow and then with pipe-soot, so she looked worse than the very Devil.

When Giske woke and saw how hideous she was, she didn’t know herself again. “Can that be me?” said Giske. “No, it can’t be me, for I’ve never been so frightful. It must be the Devil himself.”

But she wanted to know how it all fit together, so she went off and knocked at her master’s door and asked, “Is your Giske at home today, father?”

“Yes indeed, my Giske is at home,” said the man, for he wanted to be rid of her.

“Well then, I can’t be his Giske,” she thought, and off she went, and he was glad to be rid of her.

After she’d walked a while she came to a great forest, and there she met two thieves. “I’ll join company with them,” thought Giske. “Since I’m the Devil, it suits me well enough to travel with thieves.” But the thieves didn’t think so. When they caught sight of Giske, they took off running as fast as they could, for they thought the Evil One himself was after them and meant to have them. But it didn’t help much, because Giske was long-legged and quick on her feet, and she was on them before they knew it.

“If you’re going out stealing, I’ll go with you and help,” said Giske. “I’m well known around this district.”

When the thieves heard that, they thought it would be good company, and they weren’t afraid anymore.

They said they were going to steal a sheep, but they didn’t know where to get hold of one.

“Oh, that’s no trouble,” said Giske. “I’ve served at a farm out by the forest a long time; I could find the sheep-house in pitch dark.”

The thieves thought that was grand, and when they got there, Giske was to go into the sheep-house and send something out, and they were to take it. The sheep-house stood tight up against the wall of the main room where the farmer lay sleeping, so Giske went quite quietly and carefully into the shed; but once she was well inside she screamed out to the thieves, “Do you want a billy or a ewe? There’s plenty to take!”

“Hush, hush! Just take one that’s good and fat!” said the thieves.

“Yes, but do you want a billy or a ewe? Do you want a billy or a ewe? There’s plenty to take!” screamed Giske.

“Hush, hush!” said the thieves. “Just take one that’s good and fat—same thing whether it’s a billy or a ewe.”

“Yes, but do you want a billy or a ewe? Do you want a billy or a ewe? There’s plenty to take,” said Giske. She stuck to her point.

“Then shut your mouth and just take one that’s good and fat, whether it’s a billy or a ewe,” said the thieves.

Meanwhile the farmer woke in the room from all the racket and came out in his shirt to see what was going on. The thieves ran for their lives, and Giske took off after them so fast she knocked the farmer flat.

“Wait, lads! Wait, lads!” she screamed.

The farmer, who had seen nothing but that black creature, was so frightened he scarcely dared get up again, for he thought it was the Devil himself that had been in the sheep-house. He knew only one remedy: he went in and woke his people and sat down to read and pray, for he’d heard you could read the Devil away.

Then came the next evening. The thieves were going to steal a fat goose, and Giske was to show them the way. When they got to the goose-house, Giske was to go in and send one out, since she was well known there, and the thieves were to take it.

“Do you want a goose or a gander? There’s plenty to take!” screamed Giske when she got inside the goose-house.

“Hush, hush! Just take one that’s good and heavy,” said the thieves.

“Yes, but do you want a goose or a gander? Do you want a goose or a gander? There’s plenty to take!” screamed Giske.

“Hush, hush! Just take one that’s good and heavy—it’s the same whether it’s a goose or a gander—and keep that ugly mug of yours shut,” they said.

While Giske and the thieves were bickering about that, one of the geese began to shriek, then another, and in no time they were all shrieking at the top of their lungs. The farmer came out to see what was going on, the thieves bolted as fast as they could, and Giske flew after them so quickly the farmer thought it was the black Devil; for long-legged she was, and with no skirt to catch on anything.

“Wait a bit, lads!” screamed Giske. “You could have got what you wanted, whether it was a goose or a gander.”

But they had no time to stop, they thought; and back at the farm they’d visited, both great and small began to read and pray, for they were sure the Devil had been there.

Toward evening on the third day, the thieves and Giske were so hungry they didn’t know what to do with themselves. So they agreed they would go to the storehouse of a rich farmer who lived at the edge of the woods and steal some food. Yes, they went there; but the thieves didn’t dare venture in, so Giske was to go up into the raised storehouse and send food out, and they were to stand outside and take it.

When Giske got in, it was packed full of everything—meat and bacon and sausages and pea-bread. The thieves hissed at her and begged her just to toss out some food and remember how it had gone the last two evenings. But Giske held to her way: “Do you want meat or bacon or sausage or pea-bread?” she yelled so it rang. “You can have what you like, for there’s plenty to take, there’s plenty to take!”

The farmer on the place had woken at this bargaining and came out to see what was afoot. The thieves ran off as fast as they could. And in a moment Giske came springing too, black and hideous as she was. “Wait a bit, wait a bit, lads!” she screamed. “You can have what you want, for there’s plenty to take!” When the farmer caught sight of that ugly beast, he too thought the Devil was loose, for he’d already heard what had happened the two evenings before; and he began both to read and to pray, and so did they on every farm in the whole district, because they knew you could read the Devil away.

On Saturday evening the thieves were going out to steal themselves a fat billy again for Sunday fare, and they could do with it well enough, for they’d been starving many days; but they wouldn’t have Giske along. She did nothing but blather and make trouble with that mouth of hers, they said.

While Giske walked about waiting for them on Sunday morning, she grew terribly hungry. She hadn’t got much either these three days, so she went up into a turnip patch and pulled up some turnips and ate.

The farmer who owned the turnip patch was so uneasy, he thought he simply had to go and look at it on Sunday morning. So he put on his smock and went down toward a bog that lay below the slope where the turnip patch was. When he got there he saw something black up in the patch, tugging and tugging, and it didn’t take him long to believe it was the Devil too. He hurried home as fast as he could and said the Devil was in the turnip patch. They were nearly scared out of their wits on the farm when they heard it; but they thought it best to send for the priest, to get him to bind the Devil.

“No, that won’t do,” said the old woman. “To go to the priest today—it’s Sunday morning. He won’t come now; he’s not up so early, and even if he is, he’ll be reading over his sermon.”

“Oh, I’ll promise him a fine roast of a fattened calf, and he’ll come,” said the farmer.

He set off for the parsonage, but when he got there the priest wasn’t up yet. The maid asked the farmer to go into the sitting room meanwhile, and she went upstairs to the priest and said that this farmer was down below and wanted to speak with him. And when the priest heard it was such a good man waiting, he hauled on his trousers and came down at once in slippers and nightcap.

The farmer told him his errand: the Devil was loose up in his turnip patch, and if the priest would come along and bind him, he would send him a fine roast of a fattened calf.

The priest wasn’t unwilling, and called to the boy and told him to put the saddle on the horse while he dressed.

“No, sir, that won’t do,” said the farmer. “The Devil won’t stand around long, and you never know where you’ll catch him again once he’s slipped loose. You must come right now.”

So the priest came along just as he was, in nightcap and slippers. But when they reached the bog, it was so soft the priest couldn’t cross in his slippers. The farmer took him on his back and was to carry him over. He stepped carefully enough, onto a stump here and a tussock there; but when they were halfway out, Giske caught sight of them and thought it was the thieves coming with the billy.

“Is it fat, is it fat, is it fat?” she screamed, so it rang through the woods.

“Devil take me if I know whether it’s fat or thin,” said the farmer when he heard that. “But if you want to know, you can come and feel for yourself,” he said—and he was so frightened he flung the priest right out into the sucking bog and took to his heels, and if the priest hasn’t got out, then he’s likely still lying there.
