Once upon a time there was a widower who had a housekeeper called Giske. Giske wanted him for a husband. She told him so in the morning when she set out his porridge, she told him so in the evening when she cleared the table, and in between she told him that too. In the end the man was so sick of it that he didn’t know what to do to be rid of her.

Then came a day between the haymaking and the harvest. The hemp was ripe, and it was time to pull it. Giske always thought she was so pretty, and so handy, and so very capable, and today he was going to see it. She pulled hemp and pulled hemp until the strong smell of it went to her head, and she keeled over and lay fast asleep in the middle of the hemp field.

While she slept, the man came along with a pair of shears and cut her long skirt off at the knees. Then he smeared her all over, first with tallow and then with soot from the chimney, puffing away at his pipe the whole time, until she looked worse than the Devil himself.

When Giske woke up, she looked down at herself. Her legs were black. Her arms were black. And where had half her skirt gone? She didn’t know herself at all.

“Can this be me?” said Giske. “No, it can’t be me. I’ve never been as ugly as this. It must be the Devil himself.”

But she wanted to be sure. So she went up to the house, opened her master’s door and asked, “Is Giske at home today?”

“Oh yes, my Giske’s at home,” said the widower. He had never called her his Giske before, but he was desperate to be rid of her.

“Well then, I can’t be his Giske,” she thought, and off she went on her long legs. The widower shut the door, and the house was so quiet he could hear the clock tick.

When she had walked a good way, she came into a great forest, and there she met two thieves.

“I’ll tag along with them,” thought Giske. “Now that I’m the Devil, thieves are just the company for me.”

The thieves didn’t see it that way. The moment they caught sight of her, they ran for their lives, for they thought the Evil One had come to fetch them. They needn’t have bothered. Giske was long in the leg and quick on her feet, and she was on their heels before they knew it.

“If you’re going out stealing, I’ll come along and help,” said Giske. “I’m well known around here.”

The thieves didn’t doubt it for a moment. Company like that could come in handy, they thought, and they stopped being afraid.

They were going to steal a sheep, they said, but they didn’t know where to find one.

“Oh, that’s easy,” said Giske. “I was in service on a farm over by the forest for years. I could find that sheep shed in the pitch dark.”

The thieves thought that was grand. When they got there, it was agreed that Giske would go into the shed and send a sheep out, and they would wait outside and take it. The shed stood right up against the wall of the room where the farmer lay sleeping.

Giske crept in, quiet and careful. But once she was well inside among the sheep, she flung out her arms and shouted to the thieves:

“Do you want a ram or a ewe? There’s plenty to take!”

“Hush, hush!” said the thieves. “Just take one that’s nice and fat!”

“Yes, but do you want a ram or a ewe? Do you want a ram or a ewe? There’s plenty to take!” shouted Giske.

“Hush, will you!” said the thieves. “Just take one that’s nice and fat. It doesn’t matter if it’s a ram or a ewe.”

“Yes, but do you want a ram or a ewe? Do you want a ram or a ewe? There’s plenty to take!” said Giske. She wasn’t one to let a thing drop.

“Then shut your mouth and take one that’s nice and fat, ram or ewe!” said the thieves.

By now all the racket had woken the farmer on the other side of the wall. Out he came in his nightshirt, lantern in hand, to see what was going on. The thieves took to their heels, and Giske tore after them so fast that she bowled the farmer clean over.

“Wait, lads! Wait, lads!” she shouted.

The farmer lay where he had fallen. All he had seen was something big and black rushing past, and he was so frightened he hardly dared get up again. He was sure of it: the Devil himself had been in his sheep shed. He knew only one thing to do. He went inside, woke his whole household and sat down to read the Bible and pray, for he had heard that the Devil can’t abide the Good Book read aloud.

The next evening the thieves were going to steal a fat goose, and Giske was to show them the way. When they reached the goose house, it was Giske who went in to send one out, since she knew the place so well, and the thieves waited outside to take it.

“Do you want a goose or a gander? There’s plenty to take!” shouted Giske the moment she was in.

“Hush, hush! Just take one that’s nice and heavy,” said the thieves.

“Yes, but do you want a goose or a gander? Do you want a goose or a gander? There’s plenty to take!” shouted Giske.

“Hush, hush! Just take one that’s nice and heavy, goose or gander, it’s all the same. And shut your trap!” said the thieves.

While they stood there bickering, one of the geese began to shriek. Then another joined in, and before long the whole flock was shrieking at once. Out came the farmer to see what was going on. Off ran the thieves as fast as they could, and Giske after them, so fast that the farmer took her for the black Devil himself. For she had long legs, and now there was no skirt to hold her back.

“Wait a bit, lads!” shouted Giske. “You could have had whichever you wanted, goose or gander!”

But the thieves felt they had no time to stop. And on the farm they had just left, great and small sat up reading and praying, for they knew for certain that the Devil had been there.

On the third day, as evening came on, Giske and the thieves were so hungry they didn’t know what to do with themselves. So they agreed to go to the storehouse of a rich farmer who lived at the edge of the forest and steal themselves some food. But the thieves didn’t dare go in, so Giske was to climb up into the storehouse, which stood on posts, and hand the food down while they stood below and took it.

Inside it was packed with every good thing: meat and bacon and sausages and pea bread. The thieves hissed up at her to keep quiet. She was just to toss down some food, they said, and to remember how it had gone the last two nights.

But Giske was still Giske, whoever she thought she was. She seized a slab of bacon in one hand and a round of pea bread in the other, and bellowed so it rang through the night:

“Do you want meat or bacon or sausage or pea bread? You can have whatever you like, for there’s plenty to take, there’s plenty to take!”

The farmer woke at all this haggling and came out to see what it was. The thieves were gone before he had the door open. A moment later Giske came bounding after them, black and hideous as she was.

“Wait a bit, wait a bit, lads!” she shouted. “You can have whatever you like, for there’s plenty to take!”

When the farmer saw that ugly beast, he too believed the Devil was loose, for he had heard what had happened the two nights before. He began to read and pray, and soon they were reading and praying on every farm in the parish, for everyone knew that was how you drove the Devil out. The parish hadn’t been so devout in years.

On Saturday evening the thieves were going out to steal a fat ram for their Sunday dinner, and they needed it badly, for they had been starving for days. But this time they weren’t taking Giske. All she ever did was make a racket and ruin everything with that mouth of hers, they said.

On Sunday morning, while Giske waited for them, she grew terribly hungry. She hadn’t had much to eat in three days either. So she went up to a turnip patch in a clearing in the woods, pulled up some turnips and ate them.

The farmer who owned the turnip patch had hardly slept for all the talk of the Devil, and that Sunday morning he couldn’t settle. He had to go and see to his turnips. He pulled on his breeches and went down to the bog at the foot of the slope where the turnip patch lay. From there he saw something black up among the turnips, tugging and tugging. It didn’t take him long to decide that it was the Devil too. He hurried home as fast as his legs would carry him and told them the Devil was in the turnip patch.

They were half scared out of their wits on the farm. But in the end they agreed it would be best to send for the priest, so he could come and bind the Devil.

“No, that won’t do,” said the farmer’s wife. “You can’t go to the priest today. It’s Sunday morning. He won’t come now. He isn’t up this early, and if he is, he’ll be going over his sermon.”

“Oh, I’ll promise him a fat roast of veal,” said the farmer. “Then he’ll come.”

He set off for the parsonage. The priest wasn’t up yet, but the maid showed the farmer into the parlour to wait and went upstairs to tell her master. When the priest heard who was waiting, one of the richest farmers in the parish, he dragged on his trousers and came straight down in his slippers and nightcap.

The farmer told him his errand. The Devil was loose in his turnip patch, and if the priest would come along and bind him, there would be a fat roast of veal in it for him.

The priest was not unwilling. He called to the stable boy to saddle his horse while he got dressed.

“No, no, that won’t do,” said the farmer. “The Devil won’t hang about for long, and once he’s loose, there’s no telling where you’ll catch him again. You’ll have to come right now.”

So the priest came along just as he was, in his nightcap and slippers. But when they reached the bog, it was so soft that he couldn’t cross it in slippers. The farmer took him on his back to carry him over. It was no light load, for the priest had eaten a good many roasts of veal in his time. The farmer stepped carefully, onto a stump here and a tussock there. Halfway across, Giske spotted them from the turnip patch and thought it was the thieves coming back with the ram.

“Is he fat? Is he fat? Is he fat?” she screamed, so it rang through the woods.

“How the devil should I know whether he’s fat or thin?” said the farmer. “If you want to know, come and feel for yourself!”

And he was so frightened that he flung the priest into the middle of the bog and ran for his life. And if the priest hasn’t got out, he’s lying there still.
