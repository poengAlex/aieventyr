Once there was a poor, poor widow who had only one son. She toiled with the boy until he had been to the parson for his learning; but then she told him she could not feed him any longer, and he must go out and earn his bread.

So the boy set off into the world, and after he had walked a day or so, he met a strange man.

“Where are you going?” the man asked.

“Out into the world to try to find myself a place in service,” said the boy.

“Will you serve with me?”

“Oh yes—just as gladly with you as with anyone else,” the boy answered.

“Yes, you shall have it well with me,” said the man. “You need only keep me company and do nothing else.”

So the boy went with him, and lived well on food and drink, and had little or nothing to do; but he never saw a single other person at the man’s place.

One day the man said to him, “Now I am going away for eight days. While I’m gone you’ll be here alone, but you must not go into any of these four rooms. If you do, I’ll take your life when I come back.”

No, said the boy, he surely would not.

But when the man had been gone three or four days, the boy could not bear it any longer and went into one of the rooms. He looked around, but saw nothing except a shelf above the door where a whip of thorny briar lay.

This is surely something worth forbidding so strictly, the boy thought.

When the eight days were up, the man came home again. “You haven’t been inside any of the rooms, have you?” he asked.

“No, not at all,” said the boy.

“Yes, I’ll soon see,” said the man, and went straight into the room the boy had entered. “So—you have been in here after all,” he said. “Now you shall lose your life.”

The boy wept and begged for himself, and at last he was allowed to keep his life—but he received a sound beating. When it was over, they were the best of friends again.

After a while the man traveled away again. This time he would be gone fourteen days, but first he told the boy he must not set foot in any of the rooms he had not already been in; the one he had been in, he might enter again if he wished.

It went just as before—only now the boy held out for eight days. Then he slipped into the next room. There too he saw nothing but a shelf above the door, and on it a heavy millstone and a water flask.

This too is something to be so afraid of, he thought.

When the man returned, he asked whether the boy had been in any of the rooms. No—of course not, said the boy.

“Yes, I’ll soon see,” said the man, and when he found the boy had been there, he cried, “Now I won’t spare you any longer. Now you shall lose your life!”

But the boy wept and begged again, and again he escaped with his life—yet he was thrashed as hard as there was strength to thrash him. When he recovered, he lived just as well as before, and he and the man were the best of friends.

After some time the man was to travel again, and now he would be gone three weeks. He told the boy that if he went into the third room, there would be no thought of life left for him. When fourteen days had passed, the boy could not restrain himself; he slipped in. Inside he saw nothing at all except a trapdoor in the floor. He lifted it and looked down, and there stood a great copper cauldron bubbling and boiling below—yet he could see no fire beneath it.

It would be curious to feel whether it is hot, the boy thought, and he dipped a finger in. When he pulled it out, it was gilded all over. He scraped and washed, but the gilding would not come off, so he tied a rag around it. When the man came home and asked what was wrong with his finger, the boy said he had cut himself badly. But the man tore off the rag and saw at once what had happened. At first he meant to kill the boy; but when the boy wept and begged again, he only beat him so that he lay in bed for three days. Then the man took a jar down from the wall and anointed him with it, and the boy became perfectly well again.

After a while the man traveled away once more and would not return for a month. But he told the boy that if he went into the fourth room, he must never think to keep his life.

For two or three weeks the boy held back, but then he could not help himself; he had to go in, and he slipped inside. There stood a great black horse tied on a halter, with a trough of glowing coals by its head and a basket of hay by its tail. The boy thought it strange, and he swapped them, putting the hay basket by its head.

Then the horse said, “Since you have such a good heart that you would give me food, I will save you. If the troll comes home now and finds you, he will kill you. But now you must go up to the room directly above this one and take a suit of armor from those that hang there; and you must by all means not take one of the bright ones—take the rustiest you can find. Seek out sword and saddle in the same way.”

The boy did so, but it was heavy work for him to carry it all.

When he came back, the horse said he must strip naked and go down into the cauldron that stood boiling in the other room and bathe himself well in it.

I’ll surely be hideous then, the boy thought—but he did it all the same. And when he had bathed, he became so handsome and hearty, so red and white as milk and blood, and far stronger than before.

“Do you feel any change?” asked the horse.

“Yes,” said the boy.

“Try to lift me,” said the horse.

Oh yes—he could do that, and he swung the sword as if it were nothing at all.

“Then saddle me,” said the horse, “and put on the armor. Take with you the briar whip and the millstone and the water flask and the jar of ointment—then we ride.”

When the boy was properly seated on the horse, off they went, so fast he did not know how he got anywhere at all.

After they had ridden a while, the horse said, “I think I hear a humming. Look back—can you see anything?”

“There are many, many behind us—surely twenty,” said the boy.

“Yes, that is the troll,” said the horse. “Now he comes with his crew.”

They rode on until those behind began to draw closer.

“Now throw the briar whip over your shoulder,” said the horse, “but throw it well, and far from me!”

The boy did so, and at that moment there sprang up a huge, thick forest of thorny briars.

Then the boy rode on a long, long way, while the troll had to go home to fetch something to hack through the forest with.

After a while the horse said again, “Look back—can you see anything now?”

“Yes, a whole crowd,” said the boy, “like a great church congregation.”

“Yes, that is the troll,” said the horse. “Now he has more with him. Throw the millstone now—but throw it well, and far from me!”

The instant the boy did as the horse said, a great, great mountain of stone rose behind them. So the troll had to go home again to fetch something to cut his way through the mountain; and while the troll did that, the boy rode a good distance farther.

Then the horse asked him to look back once more, and the boy saw it swarming like an entire army; they were so bright that they shone.

“Yes,” said the horse, “that is the troll. Now he has all his folk with him. Smash the water flask behind you—but take good care you don’t spill any on me!”

The boy did so; yet somehow he spilled a drop on the horse’s loins. At once there spread a great, great body of water behind them, and because of the drops he spilled, the horse ended up standing far out in the water—still, it swam to land. When the trolls reached the water, they lay down to drink it dry, and they gulped it down until they burst.

“Now we are rid of them,” said the horse.

After they had traveled a long, long time, they came to a green clearing in a forest.

“Now take off all your armor and put on only your ragged clothes,” said the horse. “Take my saddle off and let me go, and hang everything inside this great hollow linden tree. Then make yourself a wig of spruce-lichen and go up to the king’s manor close by and ask for service. And when you need me, come here and rattle the bit, and I will come to you.”

The boy did as the horse had said, and when he put on the lichen wig he looked so wretched and pale and shaggy that no one could have known him. He came to the king’s manor and first asked to be in the kitchen and carry water and firewood for the cook.

But the cook’s girl asked, “Why do you wear that ugly wig? Take it off—I won’t have anyone so ugly in here.”

“I can’t,” said the boy. “My head isn’t quite clean.”

“You think I’ll have you near the food if you’re like that?” said the cook. “Go down to the stablemaster—you’re better suited to mucking the stalls.”

But when the stablemaster told him to take off the wig, he got the same answer, and the stablemaster would not have him either.

“You can go to the gardener,” he said. “You’re best suited to digging in the soil.”

With the gardener he was allowed to stay; but none of the other servants would sleep with him, so he had to sleep alone beneath the stairs to the summerhouse, which stood on posts with a high stairway. Under that he was given a little moss for a bed, and there he lay as best he could.

After he had been at the manor for some time, it happened one morning at sunrise that the boy had taken off the lichen wig and was standing washing himself—and then he was so handsome it was a delight to look at him.

The princess saw the handsome garden boy from her window and thought she had never seen anyone so handsome. So she asked the gardener why he lay outside under the stairs.

“Oh, none of the servants will sleep with him,” said the gardener.

“Let him come up tonight and lie by the door inside my chamber,” said the princess. “Then they won’t think themselves too good to let him sleep under a roof with them afterward.”

The gardener told the boy.

“Do you think I want to do that?” said the boy. “Then they’ll say there’s something between me and the princess.”

“Yes, you have good reason to fear that,” said the gardener, “you who are so handsome.”

“Well, well—if you command it, I suppose I must,” said the boy.

That evening as he went up the stairs, he stomped and sloshed so loudly that they had to beg him to walk softly, so the king would not hear. He came in, lay down, and at once began to snore like a sawmill. Then the princess said to her maid, “Now creep over and pull off his lichen wig,” and she did; but just as she tried to tug it free, he clutched it in both hands and said she would not have it at all. Then he lay down again and snored. The princess gave the maid another sign, and then she managed to snatch off the wig; and there lay the boy, as lovely and red and white as the princess had seen him in the morning sun. After that the boy lay every night in the princess’s room.

But it was not long before the king heard that the garden boy lay in the princess’s chamber every night, and he grew so furious he nearly had the boy put to death. He did not do it, though; instead he threw him into the prison tower, and he locked up his own daughter in her room so she was never allowed to come out, day or night. No matter how she wept and begged for herself and the boy, it was no use; the king only grew angrier.

After a time war and unrest came to the land, and the king had to arm himself against another king who meant to take the realm from him. When the boy heard it, he begged the jailer to go to the king and ask for armor and sword and leave for him to go to war. Everyone else laughed when the jailer delivered the message, and they begged the king to give the poor wretch some old junk for armor so they could have the fun of seeing him go to war. He got it—and along with it an old worn-out nag that limped on three legs and dragged the fourth after it.

So they rode out against the enemy. But they had not gone far from the manor before the boy and his nag got stuck fast in a bog. There he sat, chopping and nodding.

“Hey, will you get up? Hey, will you get up?” he said to the nag.

All the others had fine sport from it, and laughed and mocked the boy as they rode past. But no sooner were they gone than he sprang to the linden tree, put on the armor, and rattled the bit. At once the horse came and said, “If you do your best, I shall do mine!” When the boy arrived, the battle had already begun and the king was in dire straits; but in no time the boy had driven the enemy a long way back.

The king and his men wondered greatly who it might be who came and helped them; but no one came near enough to speak to him, and when the battle was over, he was gone. When they rode back, the boy was still sitting in the bog, chopping and nodding at the three-legged nag.

Then they laughed again. “Just look—there sits that fool still!” they said.

The next day when they rode out, the boy was still sitting there; again they laughed and mocked him. But no sooner had they ridden past than the boy sprang to the linden, and everything went just as it had the day before. They all wondered what strange giant had helped them, but none came near enough to talk with him, and of course no one guessed it was the boy.

When they rode home that evening and saw the boy still sitting on the nag, they laughed him to scorn again, and one shot an arrow at him and hit him in the leg. He began to scream and howl terribly, and the king tossed him his own handkerchief so he could bind it around the wound.

The third morning when they rode out, the boy sat in the bog again.

“Hey, will you get up? Hey, will you get up?” he said to the nag.

“No, no—he’ll sit there till he starves to death,” said the king’s men as they rode past, laughing so hard they nearly fell from their horses.

When they were gone, the boy sprang again to the linden and came to the battle just as it hung in the balance. That day he killed the enemy king, and the war ended at once.

When the battle was over, the king caught sight of his own handkerchief, which the strange champion had tied around his leg, and then it was easy for him to know who he was. They took him with great joy among them back to the manor, and the princess, who saw him from her window, was so glad you could never believe it.

“There comes my sweetheart too!” she said.

Then the boy took the jar of ointment and rubbed his own leg, and afterward all the wounded, and at once they were all made whole again.

So he got the princess for his wife. But when he went down to the stable to the horse on the day the wedding was to be held, it stood there so sullen, ears drooping, and would not eat at all. When the young king—for he had become king by then and had received half the realm—spoke to it and asked what ailed it, the horse said, “Now I have helped you on your way, and now I will not live any longer. Now you must take the sword and cut off my head.”

“No, I will not do it,” said the young king. “But you shall have what you wish, and stand still always.”

“If you do not do as I say, I know well how to take your life,” said the horse.

So the king had to do it. But when he raised the sword to strike, he felt so wretched that he turned his face away, because he did not want to see the blow. Yet no sooner had he cut off the head than the most splendid prince stood where the horse had stood.

“Where in all the world did you come from?” asked the king.

“It was I who was the horse,” said the prince. “I was once king in the land that king came from—the one you killed in the battle yesterday. It was he who cast a troll-skin on me and sold me to the troll. Now that he is dead, I will have my kingdom back, and you and I will be neighboring kings; but we shall never make war against each other.”

And they did not. They were friends as long as they lived, and each came often and visited the other.
