Once there was a man and his wife who meant to sow their fields, but they had no seed grain, and no money to buy any either. They owned only a single cow, and the man was to go to town with it and sell it, so they could get money for seed. But when it came to it, the wife didn’t dare let her husband go, for she was afraid he would drink away the money. So she set out herself with the cow, and took a hen along too.

Close by the town she met a butcher.

“Are you selling that cow, Mother?” he asked.

“Yes, I am,” she said.

“And what do you want for it, then?”

“I suppose I must have a mark for the cow, but the hen you must have for ten dalers,” she said.

“Yes,” said he, “the hen I’ve no use for, and you’ll surely get rid of it when you reach the town; but for the cow I’ll give you a mark.”

She sold her cow and got her mark, but there wasn’t a soul in town who would give ten dalers for a dry, mangy hen. So she went back to the butcher again and said, “I can’t get rid of the hen, Father! You’ll have to take it too, you who got the cow.”

“We can manage that,” said the butcher. He invited her to the table and gave her food, and poured her so much spirits that she got drunk and went clean out of her senses.

While she slept it off, the butcher dipped her in a tar barrel and then laid her in a heap of feathers.

When she woke up she was feathered all over, and she began to wonder: “Is it me, or isn’t it me? No, it can never be me—it must be some great strange bird. But how am I to go about finding out whether it’s me, or whether it isn’t? Yes—now I know how I can tell whether it’s me: if the calves lick me, and the dog doesn’t bark at me when I come home, then it’s me.”

The dog had barely set eyes on such a monster before it burst into a furious barking, as if thieves and tramps were loose in the yard. “No, then it surely can’t be me,” she said. When she came into the cowshed, the calves wouldn’t lick her, because they knew the stink of tar. “No, it can’t be me—it must be a strange bird,” she said. Then she crawled up onto the storehouse roof, began flapping her arms as if they were wings, and meant to take off into the air.

When her husband saw that, he came out with his rifle and aimed.

“Oh, don’t shoot, don’t shoot!” cried the wife. “It’s me.”

“If it’s you,” said the man, “then don’t stand there like a goat—come down and give an account of yourself.”

So she climbed down again, but she didn’t have a single penny—because the mark she’d gotten from the butcher she had thrown away in her drunkenness. And when the man heard that, he said, “You’re not more than once again as crazy as you’ve been,” and he grew so angry that he said he would go away from it all and never come back unless he met three other wives who were just as crazy.

He trudged off, and when he had gone some way down the road, he caught sight of a woman running in and out of a newly built room with an empty sieve. Each time she ran in, she flung her apron over the sieve as if it held something, and tipped it onto the floor.

“Why do you do that, Mother?” he asked.

“Oh, I’m only trying to carry in a bit of sunshine,” she answered. “But I don’t know how it is: when I’m outside, I have the sun in the sieve, but when I come in, I’ve thrown it away. In my old room I had sunshine enough, though I never carried in so much as a little; if only someone could get me some sun, I’d gladly give him three hundred dalers.”

“If you have an axe,” said the man, “I’ll get you sunshine.”

He got an axe and chopped window-holes, for the carpenters had forgotten them. At once the sun streamed in, and he got his three hundred dalers.

That’s one, thought the man, and went on again.

After a while he came to a house, and there was a dreadful yelling and racket. He went inside and saw a woman who was about to thrash her husband over the head with a beetle, and over his head she had pulled a shirt with no neck-hole.

“Are you going to beat your husband to death, Mother?” he asked.

“No,” she said, “I’m only trying to get a neck-hole in this shirt.”

The man screamed and carried on and said, “God help the one who has to put on a new shirt! If only someone could teach my wife to make a neck-hole in a different way, I’d gladly give him three hundred dalers.”

“That can soon be done—just bring a pair of scissors,” said the stranger. He got scissors, clipped a hole, and went on his way with his money.

That’s the second, he said to himself.

At long last he came to a farm, and he thought he would rest there awhile and went in.

“Where are you from, Father?” asked the wife of the house.

“I’m from Ringerike,” he answered.

“Oh no, no! Are you from the Kingdom of Heaven? Then you surely know Per the Second, my late husband?”

The woman had been married three times; the first and the last husbands were bad men, so she thought that only the second one had become blessed, because he had been a kind man.

“Yes, I know him well enough,” said the man who went about.

“And how is he doing now?” the woman asked.

“Oh, it’s poorly with him,” said the man from Ringerike. “He wanders from farm to farm and has neither food nor clothes on his body—and money isn’t even worth talking about.”

“God have mercy on him!” cried the woman. “He needn’t go so wretched, he who was so well provided for; here hangs a whole loft full of clothes that belonged to him, and a big chest of money stands here too. If you’ll take it with you, you shall have horse and cart to haul it; and he can have the horse too, and on the cart he can sit and drive from farm to farm, for he needn’t go on foot.”

The man from Ringerike got a whole cartload of clothes and a chest full of bright silver coins, and as much food and drink as he pleased. When he had it all, he climbed up and drove away.

That’s the third, he said to himself.

But out in the field the woman’s third husband was ploughing, and when he saw a stranger driving off from the farm with horse and tackle, he went home and asked his wife what sort of man it was who had gone off with the blue horse.

“Oh, him,” she said, “that was a man from the Kingdom of Heaven. He said things are so miserable with Per the Second, my late husband, that he goes from farm to farm there with neither clothes nor money; so I sent with him all those old clothes that hung here after him, and the old money chest with the silver in it.”

The husband understood at once how the matter stood. He saddled a horse and set off in a full gallop. It wasn’t long before he was close behind the one sitting on the cart and driving; but when the other noticed it, he drove horse and cart into a little wood, plucked a handful of horsehair from the horse’s tail, and then leapt up onto a rise, where he tied the horsehair fast to a birch, and beneath it he lay down to gape and stare up into the clouds.

“No, no, no!” he said, as if to himself when the third Per came riding up. “No, never have I seen anything so strange! No, never have I seen the like!”

Per stood looking at him a while, wondering whether he was mad, or what it meant at all. At last he asked, “What are you lying there gawking at?”

“No—never have I seen the like!” said the other. “There went one straight up to Heaven with a blue horse. Here you see the tailhair hanging still in the birch, and up there in the clouds you can see his blue horse.”

Per stared at the clouds and at the man and said, “I see nothing but the tailhair in the birch.”

“No, you can’t see it from where you stand,” said the other, “but come here and lie down, and stare straight up, and you mustn’t turn your eyes away from the clouds.”

While Per the Third lay there staring up at the sky until his eyes ran with water, the man from Ringerike took the horse, mounted it, and rode away with both it and the cartload. When the rumbling of wheels began to sound on the road, Per the Third sprang up—but he was so bewildered at the other man riding off with his horse that he didn’t even think to give chase until it was too late.

Now he was left bare and cheated; but when he came home and his wife asked what he had done with the horse, he said, “I sent it along too to Per the Second, for I didn’t think it worth it that he should sit on a cart and rattle from farm to farm in Heaven; now he can sell the cart and buy himself a proper wagon to ride in.”

“You shall have thanks for that! Never would I have believed you were such a good man,” said the wife.

When the man who had gathered the six hundred dalers and the cartload of clothes and money came home, he saw that all the fields were ploughed and sown. The first thing he asked his own wife was where she had got seed grain from.

“Oh,” she said, “I’ve always heard that whoever sows something will reap something, so I sowed the salt the north-country trader left here, and if only we get rain soon, I expect it will come up nicely.”

“Crazy you are, and crazy you’ll stay as long as you live,” said the man. “But it can be all the same now, for the others are no better than you.”
