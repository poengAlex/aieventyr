Once there was a man they called Rich Per the Pedlar, because he had travelled about with his packs of goods and gathered so much money that he had become a wealthy man. Rich Per had a daughter, and he doted on her so much that every suitor who came courting was sent away, for he thought none of them good enough for her. When things went that way with everyone, at last no one came at all, and as the years went on Per grew afraid she would never marry.

“I truly can’t understand it,” he said to his wife, “why no one comes to court our daughter, rich as she is. It would be a fine thing indeed if there were nobody who wanted her. She has money, and she’ll get more besides. I think I must go to the star-gazers and ask them whom she is to have; for here there isn’t so much as a living soul coming to the door.”

“How can star-gazers answer that?” his wife asked.

“Oh, they read everything in the stars, they do,” said Rich Per.

So he took a great deal of money with him to the star-gazers and begged them to look into the heavens and tell him what man his daughter should have. The star-gazers stared and studied, but said they could not see it. Per begged them to look harder and tell him at last; he would pay them well for it. Then they peered again and said that his daughter would have for her husband the miller’s child who had just been born in the mill that lay right below Rich Per’s farm.

Per gave the star-gazers a hundred dalers and rode home with his news. It seemed utterly unreasonable to him that his daughter should marry someone newly come into the world—and such a lowly one at that. He said as much to his wife and added, “I wonder if they’d sell me the boy, then we’ll soon be rid of him.”

“Oh, I daresay they would,” said his wife. “They’re poor folk.”

Per went down to the mill and asked the miller’s wife if she would sell him her son; she would get plenty of money for him. No, she would not. “I can’t imagine why you won’t,” said Per the Pedlar. “It’s nothing but poverty with you, and that boy won’t lighten it, I’ll warrant.” But she loved the child so much she would not part with him.

When the miller came in, Per said the same to him and promised six hundred dalers for the boy, so they could buy themselves a farm and be spared the grinding for others and the hunger when there was no water to turn the wheel. The miller liked the sound of that. He spoke with his wife, and so Rich Per got the boy. The mother wept and took on bitterly; but Per comforted her, saying the child would have it well—only they must promise never to ask after him, for he meant to send him far away to other lands to learn foreign tongues.

When Per came home with the child, he had a small chest made, so finely done it was a pleasure to look at. He sealed it tight with pitch, laid the miller’s boy inside, locked it, and set it out on the river so the current carried it off. Now I’m rid of him, thought Rich Per.

But when the chest had floated far downriver, it drifted into a millrace at another mill and shot through and down onto the mill beam so that the wheel stopped dead. The miller there went down to see what had halted his work, found the chest, and carried it up with him.

At dinner he said to his wife, “I truly wonder what can be in this chest. It came rushing down the race and onto the beam and stopped the mill for me today.”

“We’ll soon know,” said his wife. “The key’s in the lock—open it.”

When they opened it, there lay the prettiest child anyone could wish to see. They were glad and wanted to keep the boy, for they had no children of their own, and were so far along in years that they did not expect ever to have any.

After some time had passed, Per the Pedlar began again to wonder why no suitors came for his daughter, rich and moneyed as she was. But none came, so he travelled once more to the star-gazers and offered them much money if they could tell him who his daughter should marry.

“We told you already she’s to have the miller’s boy down there,” said the star-gazers.

“Yes, yes,” said Per, “but that boy has come to a bad end—he’s gone from this world. If you can tell me who my daughter is to marry, I’ll gladly give you two hundred dalers.”

The star-gazers looked again, but then they grew angry and said, “She shall have all the same the miller’s boy you set out in the river to do away with. He lives still, and he is at the mill that lies so-and-so far below.”

Per gave them two hundred dalers for the prophecy and thought hard on whether he could not still be rid of that miller’s boy.

The first thing Per did when he came home was to go down to the mill. By then the boy was big enough to have stood on the church floor, and he worked in the mill and helped along. A fine lad he had become.

“Couldn’t you let me have that boy of yours?” said Rich Per to the miller.

“No, I can’t,” said the miller. “I’ve raised him as my own, and he’s behaved well, so now I have help and use of him in the mill, for I’m growing old and frail.”

“Well, that’s how it goes with me too,” said Per, “and that’s why I’d like someone I can train up for trading. If you’ll let me have him, I’ll give you six hundred dalers, so you could buy a farm and live in peace in your old days.”

When the miller heard that, he let Rich Per have the boy.

The two of them travelled far and wide with their packs, trading as they went, until they came to an inn at the edge of a great forest. From there Per sent the boy home with a letter to his wife—for straight over the forest it was not far—and told him to say she must do what was written in the letter as soon as possible.

But in the letter it said that at once, on the spot, she must light a great bonfire and throw the miller’s boy upon it; and if she did not do it, then she herself should be burned alive.

The boy set off with the letter through the forest. Toward evening he came to a farm deep in the woods and went in. In the house he found no one, but in one room there was a made-up bed, and he lay crosswise upon it. He put the letter under his hatband and laid his hat over his face.

When the robbers came home—for twelve robbers lived at that farm—and saw the boy lying on the bed, they wondered what sort he was. One of them took the letter, broke the seal, and read it. “Hm, hm,” he said. “That’s Per the Pedlar out on the road. But now we’ll play him a trick, for it would be a shame if the old vulture got to put an end to such a young, handsome boy.”

So the robbers wrote another letter to Per’s wife and tucked it under the hatband while the boy slept. In this letter they wrote that she must at once hold a wedding for her daughter and the miller’s boy, and give them horses and cattle and tools, and set them properly on their way to the farm Per owned up under the ridge; and if it was not done by the time he came home, she would be punished for it.

The next day the robbers let the boy go, and when he came home and handed her the letter, he said he was to greet her from Per and tell her she must do what stood in the letter—this very instant.

“You must have served him like a good lad,” said Per’s wife to the miller’s boy, “since he can write like this now. For the last time you travelled, he was so angry with you he hardly knew how to get you out of the way.” She at once prepared the wedding and sent them off with horses and cattle and every kind of tool to the farm up under the ridge.

Not long after, Per came home again, and the first thing he asked was whether she had done what he wrote in the letter.

“Yes, I thought it was strange, but I dared do nothing else,” she said.

Then Per asked where their daughter was.

“You can well know where she is,” said his wife. “She’s with him, at the farm up under the ridge, just as it said in the letter.”

When Per understood how it had gone and saw the letter, he grew so furious he nearly burst apart, and at once he set off for the farm where the young couple lived.

“Well then, my boy,” he said to the miller’s lad, “it’s one thing that you’ve got my daughter. But if you mean to keep her, you must go to the Dragon of Deep-Ford and fetch me three feathers from the tip of his tail; for whoever has them can get whatever he wants.”

“Where am I to find him, then?” asked his son-in-law.

“That’s your affair; I don’t know,” said Rich Per.

The lad set out bravely, and after he had walked some time he came to a king’s manor. Here I’ll go in and ask, he thought, for such folk know more of the world than others, and perhaps here I can learn the way. The king asked where he came from and what errand he travelled on.

“I’m going to the Dragon of Deep-Ford to get three feathers from his tail—if only I could find him,” said the lad.

That would be luck indeed, the king said. “For I’ve never heard of anyone coming back from him. But if you should meet him, you might ask him from me why I cannot get clean water in my well. I’ve had it dug out again and again, but never can I get pure water in it.”

“Yes, I will,” said the lad.

At the king’s manor he lived well, and when he left he was given both provisions and money.

Toward evening he came to another king’s manor. When he entered the kitchen, the king came out and asked where he was from and what errand he travelled on.

“I’m going to the Dragon of Deep-Ford to get three feathers from his tail,” said the lad.

“There’ll be luck needed for that,” said the king, “for I’ve never yet heard of anyone returning from him. But if you reach him, you may ask him from me where my daughter is, who disappeared many years ago. I have searched and proclaimed for her from every church, but no one could tell me anything.”

“I will,” said the lad.

He was treated well there too, and when he went, he was given provisions and money.

When evening drew near again, he came to yet a third king’s manor. Here the queen came out into the kitchen and asked where he was from and what errand he travelled on.

“I’m going to the Dragon of Deep-Ford to get three feathers from his tail,” said the lad.

“There’ll be luck needed for that,” said the queen, “for I’ve never heard of anyone returning from him. But if you find him, you might ask him from me where I can find my golden keys, which I have lost.”

“I will,” said the lad.

After he had walked a while, he came to a great wide river. As he stood wondering how he was to cross—or whether he should follow it along—an old hunchbacked man came and asked where he was going.

“I’m bound for the Dragon of Deep-Ford—if anyone could tell me where to find him.”

“I can tell you that well enough,” said the man, “for I go here ferrying over those who are going to him. He lives just up above; once you reach the hill, you’ll see his castle. And if you get to speak with him, you might ask him from me how long I must go on here ferrying people across.”

“I will,” said the lad.

The man took him on his back and carried him over the river, and when he reached the hilltop he saw the castle and went in. The princess was alone at home.

“But dear me, do Christian folk dare come here?” she said. “There have been none here since I came, and you’d best be off again as quickly as you can. For when the dragon comes home he will smell you, and then he’ll swallow you at once—and you’ll make me miserable too.”

“No,” said the lad. “I can’t go before I’ve got three feathers from his tail.”

“You’ll never get them,” said the princess.

But the lad would not leave. He would wait for the dragon and get the tail feathers and the answers to the questions.

“Well, since you’re so set on it, I must see if I can help you,” said the princess. “Try if you can lift the sword hanging on the wall there.”

No, he could not even budge it.

“Then you must take a swallow from this bottle,” said the princess.

After he had sat a while, he tried again; then he could just barely stir it.

“You’ll have to take another swallow,” said the princess, “and then you must tell me your errand.”

He took another, and told her: one king had begged him to ask why he could not get clean water in his well; another wanted to know what had become of his daughter who had been gone for many years; a queen wanted to know where her golden keys had gone; and lastly he must ask, for the ferryman, how long he must stand below ferrying people over.

When he took hold of the sword, he could lift it, and after one more swallow he could swing it.

“If the dragon doesn’t finish you at once, you must creep under the bed,” said the princess as evening approached, “for he’ll soon be home, and there you must lie so still he doesn’t notice you. When we’ve gone to bed, I’ll ask him, but you must listen well and mark what he answers. Stay under the bed until all is quiet and the dragon is asleep again. Then creep out softly, take the sword with you, and when he rises you must be ready to strike off his head in a single blow, and at the same moment snatch the three feathers. Otherwise he’ll tear them off himself so no one can profit by them.”

A little while after the lad had crept under the bed, the dragon came home.

“I smell the bones of a Christian man!” roared the dragon.

“Oh yes,” said the princess, “a raven came flying with a man’s bone in its beak and sat on the roof. That must be what you smell.”

“Well then,” said the dragon.

The princess set out the food, and when they had eaten they lay down. After they had lain a while, the princess slept restlessly, and suddenly she cried out.

“Ah!” she said.

“What’s the matter with you?” said the dragon.

“Oh, I sleep so uneasily,” said the princess, “and I had such a strange dream.”

“What did you dream?” said the dragon.

“I thought a king came here and asked you how he should go about getting clean water in his well,” said the princess.

“He could know that himself,” said the dragon. “If he digs around the well and lifts out the old rotten log lying at the bottom, he’ll have clean water again. Now lie still and dream no more.”

After the princess had lain a bit, she grew restless again and tossed in bed, and then she cried out once more.

“Ah!”

“What is it now?” said the dragon.

“Oh, I sleep so uneasily, and I had such a strange dream,” said the princess.

“That’s a fine amount of dreaming for you,” growled the dragon. “What did you dream this time?”

“I thought a king came here and asked you what had become of his daughter, who vanished many years ago,” said the princess.

“That’s you,” said the dragon. “And him you’ll never see again. Now I tell you, let me have peace at night and don’t lie there dreaming any more, or I’ll break your ribs.”

The princess had not lain long before she grew restless again; all at once she cried out.

“Ah!”

“Now, is it that again! What is it now?” snarled the dragon. He was so wild and sleepy with it that he was near to flying into a rage.

“Oh, don’t be angry,” said the princess. “I had such a strange dream.”

“The devil take all this dreaming! What did you dream now?” said the dragon.

“I thought a queen came here and asked if you could tell her where she might find again her golden keys that she has lost.”

“She can look among the bushes where she lay that time—she knows well enough—and she’ll find them,” said the dragon. “But now leave me in peace from more dreams.”

They slept a while, but then the princess grew restless again, and suddenly she cried out.

“Ah!”

“You won’t be well till I break your neckbone,” said the dragon, and he was so furious that sparks flew from him. “What is it now again?”

“Oh, don’t be cruel to me—I can’t help it,” said the princess. “But I had such a strange dream.”

“There’s no end to your dreaming,” said the dragon. “What did you dream now?”

“I thought the ferryman down at the crossing came and asked how long he must go on standing there ferrying people over,” said the princess.

“That stupid beast will soon be free,” said the dragon. “When someone comes who wants to cross, let him throw that one out into the river and say, ‘Now you ferry over till you’re relieved!’ Then he’ll be rid of it. And now let me have peace from your dreams, or there’ll be another sort of dance.”

So the princess let him sleep in peace. And as soon as all was quiet and the miller’s lad heard the dragon snoring, he crept out. Before daylight the dragon rose, but he had scarcely set both feet on the floor before the lad struck off his head and snatched three feathers from his tail.

Then there was great joy, and the lad and the princess took as much silver and gold and money and other treasures as they could carry. And when they came to the crossing, they loaded the ferryman so thoroughly with all he had to carry over that he forgot to ask what the dragon had said, until everything and both the lad and the princess were across.

“It’s true,” said the ferryman as they were about to go. “Did you ask the dragon what I asked you to?”

“Yes,” said the lad. “He said that when someone comes who wants to cross, you must throw him into the middle of the river and say, ‘Now you ferry over till you’re relieved!’—and then you’ll be free.”

“Shame on you!” said the ferryman. “If you’d said that sooner, you could have freed me yourself.”

When they came to the first king’s manor, the queen asked if he had asked the dragon about her golden keys.

“Yes,” said the lad, and he whispered to her, “He said you should look among the bushes where you lay that time—you know well enough.”

“Hush, hush, say nothing,” said the queen, and she gave the lad a hundred dalers.

When he came to the second king’s manor, the king asked if he had spoken to the dragon about what he had begged.

“Yes,” said the lad, “and here is your daughter.”

The king was so glad that he would gladly have given the miller’s lad the princess and half the kingdom. But since the lad was already married, he got two hundred dalers and horses and a wagon, and as much gold and silver as he could carry with him.

When he came to the third king’s manor, the king came out and asked if he had asked the dragon what he had requested.

“Yes,” said the lad. “He said you must dig out the well and lift up the old rotten log lying at the bottom, and then you’ll have clean water.” And the king gave him three hundred dalers.

From there he travelled straight home, so decked out with gold and silver and so splendid that he gleamed and clinked as he went; and now he was far richer than Per the Pedlar. When Per got the feathers, he had nothing more to say against the marriage; but when he saw all the riches, he asked whether there was so much with the dragon.

“Yes,” said the lad. “There was far more than I could bring. There’s so much it would take many horse-loads, and if you want to travel there, there’s surely enough for you too.”

So Per the Pedlar wanted to go. His son-in-law told him the road so well that he needn’t ask his way; “but the horses,” he said, “you’d best leave them on this side of the river, for the old ferryman will help you over.”

Per set out and took a great deal of provisions and many horses with him; but he left them behind by the river, just as the lad had said. Then the ferryman took him on his back, and when they were partway out, he flung him into the middle of the river and said, “Now you can stand here ferrying over till you’re relieved!”

And if no one has relieved him, then Rich Per the Pedlar is still there, ferrying people across to this very day.
