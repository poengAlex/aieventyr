Once upon a time there was a man they called Rich Per the Pedlar. He had gone from farm to farm with a pack of wares on his back for so many years, and sold so much, that he had become a rich man.

Rich Per had a daughter, and he thought so highly of her that every suitor who came courting was sent packing. Not one of them was good enough for her. Word got about, and in the end nobody came at all. And as the years went by, Per began to be afraid she would never be married.

“I can’t make it out,” he said to his wife. “Why does nobody come courting our daughter, rich as she is? It’d be an odd thing if nobody wanted her. She’s got money, and she’ll get more. I’d better go to the stargazers and ask them who she’s to have. Not a mother’s son comes near the place.”

“And how can the stargazers tell you that?” asked his wife.

“Oh, they read everything in the stars,” said Rich Per.

So he took plenty of money with him to the stargazers and asked them to read in the stars what husband his daughter would get. The stargazers looked and looked, and said they couldn’t see it. Per begged them to look again, and look properly. He would pay them well. So they looked more closely, and then they said that his daughter was to marry the miller’s child who had just been born in the mill below Rich Per’s own farm.

Per gave the stargazers a hundred dalers and went home with his answer. He thought it most unreasonable that his daughter should marry somebody who had only just arrived in the world, and a miller’s brat at that.

“I wonder if they’d sell me the boy,” he said to his wife. “Then we’d soon be rid of him.”

“I’m sure they would,” said his wife. “They’re poor folk.”

So Per went down to the mill and asked the miller’s wife whether she would sell him her son. She would get a great deal of money for him.

No, she would not.

“I can’t see why not,” said Per the Pedlar. “There’s nothing in this house but want, and I doubt the boy will make it any lighter.”

But she loved the boy, and she would not part with him.

When the miller came in, Per said the same thing to him, and promised him six hundred dalers for the boy. With that they could buy a farm of their own and be done with grinding other people’s corn, and with going hungry every time the millstream ran dry. The miller liked the sound of that. He talked it over with his wife, and so Rich Per got the boy. The mother wept and wailed, but Per comforted her. The boy would be well looked after, he said. Only they must promise never to ask after him, for he meant to send him far away to foreign lands, to learn foreign languages.

When Per got home with the boy, he had a little chest made, so finely fitted and polished that it was a pleasure to look at. He sealed every seam with black pitch, laid the miller’s boy inside and locked the lid. Then he carried the chest down to the river below his farm, knelt among the reeds and pushed it out into the current. For a moment his hands stayed on the lid, which was still sticky with pitch. Then he let go. The river turned the chest round once and carried it off downstream.

Now I’m rid of him, thought Rich Per.

But it was a very good chest, and it didn’t leak.

It sailed a long way down the river, until it was drawn into the millrace of another mill. Down the chute it shot and into the waterwheel, and there it stuck, and the mill stopped dead. The miller went down to see what was the matter, found the chest and carried it up to the house.

“I’d dearly like to know what’s in this,” he said to his wife when he came in for his dinner. “It came shooting down the race into the wheel and stopped the mill on me today.”

“We’ll soon find out,” said his wife. “The key’s in the lock. Just open it.”

They opened it, and there lay the loveliest child anyone had ever seen. The miller and his wife were overjoyed. They had no children of their own, and they were too far on in years to hope for any now. So they kept him.

Years went by, and still no suitors came for Per the Pedlar’s daughter, rich as she was. Per began to wonder all over again what the matter could be, and in the end he went back to the stargazers and offered them a great deal of money if they would tell him who his daughter was going to marry.

“We’ve told you already,” said the stargazers. “She’s to have the miller’s boy down there.”

“Yes, yes, that’s all very well,” said Per the Pedlar, “but he met with an accident, and he’s no longer with us. Now, if you could tell me who my daughter’s to marry, I’d gladly give you two hundred dalers.”

The stargazers looked at the stars again, and this time they grew angry.

“She’ll have the miller’s boy all the same,” they said. “The one you put in the river to be rid of him. He’s alive, and he’s at the mill so many miles down the river.”

Per gave them two hundred dalers for the prophecy, and all the way home he was thinking how he might still be rid of that miller’s boy.

The first thing he did when he got home was to go down the river to that mill. The boy was big by now, old enough to have been confirmed, and he was working in the mill alongside the old man. A fine, handsome lad he had grown into.

“Couldn’t you let me have that boy?” said Per the Pedlar to the miller.

“No, I could not,” said the miller. “I’ve raised him as my own, and he’s turned out well. Now he’s a real help to me in the mill, and I need it, for I’m getting old and frail.”

“So am I,” said Per the Pedlar, “and that’s just why I’d like someone I could train up in trade. Let me have him, and I’ll give you six hundred dalers. Then you can buy yourself a farm and live in peace and quiet in your old age.”

When the miller heard that, he let Per the Pedlar have the boy.

The two of them travelled far and wide, peddling and trading, until they came to an inn at the edge of a great forest. From there Per sent the lad on ahead to his own house, with a letter for his wife. Straight through the forest it wasn’t far, he said, and the lad was to tell her to do what the letter said, as quickly as ever she could.

And this is what the letter said: she was to light a great bonfire that very moment and throw the miller’s lad on it. And if she didn’t, she would be burned alive herself.

The lad set off through the forest with the letter. Towards evening he came to a farm deep in the woods, and went in. There was nobody in the house, but in one of the rooms stood a bed all made up, and he lay down across it. He tucked the letter under his hatband, laid his hat over his face and went to sleep.

Twelve robbers lived on that farm. When they came home and saw a strange lad lying on the bed, they wondered who he could be, and one of them took the letter, broke the seal and read it.

“Hm, hm,” he said. “So Per the Pedlar’s up to his tricks. Well, we’ll play him a trick of our own. It’d be a sin and a shame if the old skinflint got to make an end of a fine young lad like this.”

So the robbers wrote another letter to Per’s wife and fastened it under the lad’s hatband while he slept. This one said that she was to hold a wedding at once for their daughter and the miller’s lad, and give them horses and cattle and tools, and set them up properly on the farm Per owned up under the ridge. And if all that wasn’t done by the time he came home, she would pay for it.

The next day the robbers let the lad go. When he got there, he gave the letter to Per’s wife and said that Per sent his greetings, and she was to do what it said, and look sharp about it.

“You must have served him very well,” said the pedlar’s wife to the miller’s lad, “if he can write like this now. When the two of you set off, he was so angry with you he didn’t know how to be rid of you.”

She got the wedding ready that same day. Per’s daughter took one look at the miller’s lad and made no objection. Then the young couple were sent off to the farm up under the ridge, with horses and cattle and every kind of tool they could need.

Not long after, Per the Pedlar came home, and the first thing he asked was whether she had done what he’d written in the letter.

“Yes,” said his wife. “I did think it was strange, but I didn’t dare do otherwise.”

“And where’s our daughter?” asked Per.

“Well, you ought to know where she is,” said his wife. “She’s with him, up at the farm under the ridge, just like it said in the letter.”

When Per the Pedlar found out how things stood and saw the letter, he was so furious he nearly burst. He set off at once for the young people’s farm.

The young couple were out in the yard when he came. The new horses stood in harness by the stable, the cattle were out, and the tools were stacked against the wall, just as the letter had ordered. The sky hung low and grey over the ridge. Per’s daughter came and stood close beside her husband. Per stopped in front of them, stiff with rage, and pointed at the lad.

“It’s all very well, my boy, that you’ve got my daughter,” he said. “But if you want to keep her, you’ll have to go to the Dragon of Deep-Ford and fetch me three feathers from his tail. Whoever has those can get anything he wants.”

The lad met his eye. “And where would I find him?”

“That’s your business,” said Rich Per. “I don’t know.”

The lad set off undaunted, and when he had walked a good while, he came to a king’s manor. I’ll go in here and ask, he thought. Great folk know more of the world than the rest of us, and maybe they can tell me the way.

The King asked him where he came from and what his errand was.

“I’m going to the Dragon of Deep-Ford to get three feathers from his tail,” said the lad, “if only I can find him.”

“That’ll take luck,” said the King. “I’ve never heard of anybody coming back from him. But if you do find him, you might ask him from me why I can’t get clean water in my well. I’ve had it dug out time after time, and the water’s never clean.”

“I’ll do that,” said the lad.

He was well looked after at the King’s manor, and when he left, he was given food for the road and money besides.

Towards evening he came to another king’s manor. When he went into the kitchen, the King came out and asked him where he came from and what his errand was.

“I’m going to the Dragon of Deep-Ford to get three feathers from his tail,” said the lad.

“That’ll take luck,” said the King, “for I’ve never yet heard of anybody coming back from him. But if you get there, you might ask him from me where my daughter is. She vanished many years ago. I’ve searched high and low, and had it cried from every church in the land, but nobody can tell me a thing.”

“I’ll do that,” said the lad.

He was well looked after there too, and when he left, he was given food for the road and money besides.

When evening came round again, he reached yet another king’s manor. Here it was the Queen who came out into the kitchen and asked him where he came from and what his errand was.

“I’m going to the Dragon of Deep-Ford to get three feathers from his tail,” said the lad.

“That’ll take luck,” said the Queen, “for I’ve never heard of anybody coming back from him. But if you find him, you might ask him from me where I’m to find my golden keys. I’ve lost them.”

“I’ll do that,” said the lad.

When he had walked a while longer, he came to a great wide river. While he stood there wondering how to get across, or whether he ought to follow the bank instead, an old man came along with a back as crooked as a hook, and asked him where he was going.

“I’m going to the Dragon of Deep-Ford,” said the lad, “if anybody could tell me where to find him.”

“I can tell you that,” said the old man, “for I’m the one who carries across everybody who’s going to him. He lives just up there. Once you’re up the hill, you’ll see his castle. And if you get to speak to him, you might ask him from me how long I’m to go on carrying people across.”

“I’ll do that,” said the lad.

The old man took him on his back and waded across the river with him. When the lad got to the top of the hill, he saw the castle, and he went in.

The princess was alone at home. A fire burned on the hearth, and the shadows lay deep in the corners.

“Mercy on us!” she said. “Do Christian folk dare come here? There’s not been one since I came. You’d better be off again as fast as you can, for when the dragon comes home he’ll smell you, and then he’ll swallow you on the spot. And you’ll bring me to grief as well.”

“No,” said the lad. “I can’t go until I’ve got three feathers from his tail.”

“You’ll never get them,” said the princess.

But the lad wouldn’t go. He meant to wait for the dragon and get the feathers from his tail and the answers to his questions.

“Well, since you’re so stubborn about it, I’d better see if I can help you,” said the princess. “Try if you can lift that sword on the wall there.”

It was a huge old sword, and the lad couldn’t so much as shift it.

“Then you’d better take a swig from this bottle,” said the princess.

He sat a while, and then he tried again. Now he could just shift it.

“You’d better have another swig,” said the princess, “and then you can tell me your errand.”

He took another swig, and then he told her. There was a king who wanted to know why he couldn’t get clean water in his well, and another king who wanted to know what had become of his daughter, who had vanished many years ago. When he got to that one, the princess turned her face to the fire and said nothing. And there was a queen who wanted to know where her golden keys had got to. Last of all, there was the ferryman, who wanted to know how long he must go on carrying people across.

When the lad took hold of the sword now, he could lift it, and when he’d had one more swig, he could swing it.

By then it was getting on towards evening.

“If the dragon isn’t to finish you off on the spot, you’ll have to crawl under the bed,” said the princess, and she pointed to the great bed that stood made up against the wall. “He’ll be home soon. And there you must lie so still that he doesn’t notice you. When we’ve gone to bed, I’ll ask him your questions. Listen hard and remember every word he says. Stay under the bed until everything’s quiet and the dragon’s asleep again. Then creep out softly and take the sword with you. When he gets up, you must cut off his head at one stroke, and snatch the three feathers the same moment. Otherwise he’ll tear them out himself, so nobody can have any good of them.”

The lad crawled under the bed. He hadn’t been lying there long when the dragon came home.

“I smell Christian man’s bones in here!” said the dragon.

“Oh, that,” said the princess. “A raven came flying by with a man’s bone in its beak and sat down on the roof. That’ll be what you can smell.”

“I see,” said the dragon.

The princess put the food on the table, and when they had eaten, they went to bed. But when they had been lying there a while, the princess began to toss and turn, and all at once she started up.

“Ow!” she said.

“What’s the matter with you?” said the dragon.

“Oh, I’m sleeping so badly,” said the princess, “and I had such a strange dream.”

“What did you dream, then?” said the dragon.

“I dreamed a king came here and asked you what he should do to get clean water in his well,” said the princess.

“Oh, he could have worked that out for himself,” said the dragon. “If he digs round the well and pulls up the old rotten log that’s lying at the bottom, he’ll have clean water again. Now lie still and don’t dream any more.”

When the princess had lain quiet a little while, she grew restless and began to thrash about in the bed, and then she started up again.

“Ow!”

“What’s going on now?” said the dragon.

“Oh, I’m sleeping so badly, and I had such a strange dream,” said the princess.

“There’s a terrible lot of dreaming in you,” said the dragon. “What did you dream this time?”

“I dreamed a king came here and asked you what had become of his daughter, who vanished many years ago,” said the princess.

“That’s you,” said the dragon. “But he’ll never set eyes on you again. Now I’m asking you to let me have my night’s sleep and stop lying there dreaming, or I’ll break your ribs.”

The princess hadn’t lain there long before she grew restless again, and all of a sudden she started up.

“Ow!”

“What, again! What is it now?” said the dragon. By this time he was so wild and so short of sleep that he was ready to fly into a thousand pieces.

“Oh, you mustn’t be angry,” said the princess, “but I had such a strange dream.”

“The devil take all this dreaming!” said the dragon. “What did you dream now?”

“I dreamed a queen came here and asked if you could tell her where she’d find her golden keys that she’s lost.”

“Oh, she can look in the bushes where she lay that time—she knows the time I mean—and she’ll find them,” said the dragon. “But now leave me in peace, and no more dreams.”

They slept for a while. But then the princess grew restless again, and all of a sudden she started up.

“Ow!”

“You won’t be right till I’ve broken your neck,” said the dragon. He was so furious by now that sparks flew off him. “What is it this time?”

“Oh, you mustn’t be cross with me, I can’t help it,” said the princess. “But I had such a strange dream.”

“There’s no end to this dreaming,” said the dragon. “What did you dream now?”

“I dreamed the ferryman down at the crossing came and asked how long he must go on carrying people across,” said the princess.

“The stupid beast! He can be rid of that soon enough,” said the dragon. “The next time somebody comes who wants to cross, he just throws him in the river and says, ‘Now you carry them across, till someone takes your place!’ But now let me have some peace from your dreams, or you’ll be dancing to a different tune.”

So the princess let him sleep in peace. As soon as all was quiet, and the miller’s lad could hear nothing but the dragon snoring, he crept out. Before it was light, the dragon got up. But he had barely got both feet on the floor before the lad cut off his head and snatched three feathers from his tail.

Then there was great rejoicing. The lad and the princess took as much silver and gold and money and other precious things as they could carry. And when they came down to the crossing, they gave the ferryman so much to carry across, load after load, that his head was in a whirl, and he forgot to ask what the dragon had said until every last bit of it was across, and the lad and the princess too.

“Oh, that reminds me,” he said, as they turned to go. “Did you ask the dragon what I asked you to?”

“I did,” said the lad. “He said that when somebody comes who wants to cross, you’re to throw him into the middle of the river and say, ‘Now you carry them across, till someone takes your place!’ Then you’ll be free.”

“Curse you!” said the ferryman. “If you’d told me that before, you could have taken my place yourself.”

When they came to the first king’s manor, the Queen wanted to know whether he had asked the dragon about her golden keys.

“Yes,” said the lad, and he leaned close and whispered in her ear, “he said you should look in the bushes where you lay that time. You know the time he means.”

“Hush, hush! Not a word!” said the Queen, and she gave the lad a hundred dalers.

When he came to the second king’s manor, the King asked whether he had remembered to speak to the dragon for him.

“I did,” said the lad, “and here’s your daughter.”

The King was so overjoyed that he would have given the miller’s lad the princess and half the kingdom on the spot. But since the lad was married already, he got two hundred dalers instead, and horses and a carriage, and as much gold and silver as he could take with him.

When he came to the third king’s manor, the King came out and asked whether he had put his question to the dragon.

“I did,” said the lad. “He said you should dig out the well and pull up the old rotten log that’s lying at the bottom, and then you’ll have clean water.” And the King gave him three hundred dalers.

From there the lad went straight home, and he was so decked out in gold and silver, and so splendid, that he shone and glittered from a long way off. Now he was a great deal richer than Per the Pedlar. When Per got the feathers, he had nothing more to say against the marriage. But when he saw all the riches, he asked whether there was really so much at the dragon’s.

“Yes,” said the lad, “and a great deal more than I could bring. There’s enough for many a horse-load, and if you’d like to go there, I’m sure there’s plenty for you too.”

Per the Pedlar would like that very much. So his son-in-law told him the way so clearly that he wouldn’t need to ask anybody.

“But the horses,” he said, “you’d best leave on this side of the river. The old ferryman will carry you over, never fear.”

So Per set off, with a great load of provisions and a great many horses, and he left the horses at the river, just as the lad had said. Then the ferryman took him on his back and waded out. When they were well out in midstream, he heaved Per off his back and flung him into the river. In went Rich Per with a great splash, and his fine travelling clothes and his heavy pack of provisions dragged at him.

“Now you can carry them across,” said the ferryman, “till someone takes your place!”

And if nobody has taken his place, Rich Per the Pedlar is still there, carrying people across that river to this very day.
