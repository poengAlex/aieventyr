There was once a man everyone called Rich Per the Trader, because he’d spent half his life on the road buying cheap, selling dear, and stacking money until he owned more property than he could keep track of. Per had one daughter, and he guarded her like an investment. Every time someone came around with flowers and good manners, Per found a reason they weren’t good enough. After a while word got out, and the visits stopped. Then the years started to run, and Per began to worry she’d end up alone.

“I don’t get it,” he said one evening to his wife. “She’s set for life. She’ll inherit everything. You’d think people would line up. But there isn’t a single serious guy at the door anymore. I’m going into town to those star people and ask what’s going on.”

“The star people?” his wife said. “How would they know who she’ll marry?”

“They read everything,” Per said. “That’s what they claim. And I’m willing to pay for certainty.”

So Per took a thick envelope and drove to a couple of astrologers who rented a sleek little office with candles in the window and a waiting room full of nervous people. He asked them to look at the charts and tell him who his daughter would marry. They tapped on tablets, checked dates, frowned, and said they couldn’t see it.

Per leaned in and promised them more money if they’d “look properly.” They looked again, and finally one of them said, almost reluctantly, that Per’s daughter would marry the miller’s baby—born that very week at the little mill down by the river, just below Per’s own place.

Per drove home with the prediction burning like an insult in his chest. “A miller’s newborn,” he spat to his wife. “That’s what the universe has for us? Some nobody’s baby?”

He paced, then stopped. “I wonder if they’d sell me the boy. Poor people sell things when they’re desperate.”

“They are poor,” his wife said quietly. “They can barely keep the mill running in dry seasons.”

Per went down to the mill and asked the miller’s wife if she would take money for her son. He offered enough to change their lives. She stared at him like he was sick and said no, absolutely not.

“I don’t understand why you won’t,” Per said, forcing a laugh. “You’re scraping by. That boy won’t lift you out of anything.”

But she held the baby closer and wouldn’t budge.

When the miller came in, Per tried again—this time with numbers big enough to make a man dizzy. He promised six hundred thousand kroner, enough to buy a small farm outright, enough to stop worrying about water levels and grinding contracts and another winter of debt.

The miller’s face tightened. He spoke with his wife, low and fast, and at the end of it he couldn’t look her in the eyes. Per got the boy.

The mother cried like something had been ripped out of her. Per told her the child would be fine, that he’d be sent far away to learn languages and have opportunities. But he made them swear they would never come asking, never try to find him.

Back home, Per had a carpenter build a small, beautiful box—more like a polished crate—with a heavy latch. He sealed the edges with tar, put the baby inside, locked it, and in the dark of early morning carried it down to the river. He set it on the current and watched it slide away.

“Now I’m rid of him,” Per whispered.

The box floated for a long time, bumped along stones, and finally got sucked into a side channel feeding another old mill further downstream. It jammed the wheel intake so the machinery shuddered and stopped. The man who ran that place went down to see what was wrong, found the sealed box wedged where it shouldn’t be, and hauled it up, annoyed and curious.

At lunch he set it on the kitchen table. “Something came through the channel and shut the whole operation down,” he told his wife. “Look at this. The key’s still in it.”

“Then open it,” she said.

Inside lay the most beautiful baby either of them had ever seen, blinking up at the light. They had no children and had stopped hoping for any. They didn’t call the police or ask questions. They wrapped the boy in a towel, named him, and raised him as their own.

Time passed.

Per kept waiting for someone to come for his daughter, but still nobody did. He began to feel the pressure of gossip, of birthdays, of the quiet panic that people pretend not to have. So he went back to the astrologers with another envelope and asked again.

They looked at him like he was a problem that wouldn’t go away.

“We told you,” they said. “She’s going to marry the miller’s boy.”

“Yes, yes,” Per said, waving it off. “But that boy is gone. He died. It’s finished. Tell me the real answer. I’ll pay double.”

They checked again—longer this time—and when they looked up they weren’t amused.

“She’ll still marry him,” one of them said. “The boy you put in the river is alive. He’s working at the mill a good distance downstream. You can go see for yourself.”

Per paid them anyway, because anger still believes in fate when it suits him. On the drive home he planned, coldly, how to finish what he’d started.

The very next day Per drove to the mill the astrologers had described. The boy was no longer a baby. He was a teenager with strong arms and an open face, hauling sacks, fixing belts, doing whatever needed doing. He’d grown into the kind of good-looking that makes people trust you without meaning to.

Per spoke to the miller, the one who had raised the boy.

“Couldn’t you let me have that lad?” Per said, trying for friendly. “I’m getting older. I need someone I can train in business.”

The miller shook his head. “No. He’s like my own. And he’s been good to us. I’m not giving him away.”

Per smiled and raised the offer until it was obscene: the same six hundred thousand kroner, enough for a farm, enough to retire somewhere sunny, enough to stop worrying forever.

The miller swallowed. He didn’t want to, but he did. Per got the boy again.

Per and the boy traveled around together for a while, doing deals—wholesale goods, secondhand machinery, whatever Per could flip. They ended up at a roadside inn on the edge of a huge forest. From there Per told the boy to take a letter home to Per’s wife. It wasn’t far if you cut straight through the woods.

“Tell her to do exactly what it says,” Per added. “Immediately.”

The boy tucked the letter under the band of his cap and set off.

In the letter Per had written: Light a big fire right away and throw the miller’s boy into it. If you don’t, you’ll be burned alive yourself.

The boy walked for hours under dark trees. Toward evening he came to an isolated farmhouse deep in the forest. He went in to ask for a glass of water or a place to rest, but the rooms were empty. In one bedroom the bed was made up clean. He lay down across it, exhausted, his cap pulled low over his face. The letter stayed under the cap band.

Near midnight the owners came home: twelve thieves who used the place as a base. When they saw a stranger in their bed, they surrounded him in silence. One of them lifted his cap, found the letter, broke it open, and read.

“It’s Rich Per,” the thief muttered. Then he looked at the sleeping boy and snorted. “That old crow wants to burn a kid like this? No. Let’s pay Per back.”

They wrote a new letter in Per’s voice, folded it neatly, and slid it under the cap band in place of the original. In their letter they ordered Per’s wife to hold a wedding immediately for Per’s daughter and the miller’s boy, to give them horses, livestock, tools—whatever they’d need—and to set them up on the small farm Per owned up under the ridge. If it wasn’t done before Per returned, the letter threatened consequences.

In the morning they let the boy go as if nothing had happened.

When he reached Per’s house and handed the letter over, he said, “Per told me you were to do what it says, right away.”

Per’s wife read it, blinked, and read it again. Her mouth went tight, but fear of her husband was stronger than confusion. “You must have served him well,” she said to the boy, half to herself. “Because last time you left together, he was so angry at you he could hardly think.”

She made calls, booked the church, bribed the right people to move schedules, and by evening there was a wedding: quick speeches, forced smiles, a daughter pale with shock, a young man trying to understand why his life had suddenly snapped into a new shape. Before the week was over the couple were sent up to the ridge farm with animals, equipment, and keys.

Not long after, Per came home.

The first thing he asked was, “Did you do what I wrote?”

His wife’s hands shook as she nodded. “It felt strange, but I didn’t dare do anything else.”

Per asked where their daughter was.

His wife stared at him. “Where you told me to send her. She’s up at the ridge farm—with him. Like your letter said.”

Per snatched the letter, read it, and understood. The room went hot with his rage. He stormed out and drove straight up to the ridge.

When he got there, he forced himself into a smile that didn’t reach his eyes.

“Well,” he said to the miller’s boy, “you’ve got my daughter. Fine. But if you plan to keep her, you’ll do one thing for me. You’ll go to the Dragon of Dybenfart and bring me three feathers from his tail. Whoever holds them can get whatever he wants.”

The young man stared. “Where do I even find him?”

“That’s your problem,” Per said. “Not mine.”

So the boy set off.

After a day of travel he came to a royal estate—modern security, cameras, guards, and a flag in the wind. He told the gate who he was and, because folktales still happen even in a world of keycards, he was eventually brought in.

The King asked where he was going and why.

“I’m going to the Dragon of Dybenfart,” the boy said, “to get three feathers from his tail—if I can find him.”

The King leaned back, grim. “People don’t come back from him,” he said. “But if you do meet him, ask him this: Why can’t I get clean water in my well? I’ve had it dug and cleaned again and again, but it never runs clear.”

“I’ll ask,” the boy said.

He was fed well, given money for the road, and sent on.

By evening he reached another royal residence. This King asked the same questions.

“I’m going to the Dragon of Dybenfart,” the boy said.

The King’s face softened into something like grief. “Then ask him something for me,” he said. “Where is my daughter? She vanished years ago. I’ve searched, announced rewards, gone to every police chief and every priest who’d listen. No one can tell me.”

“I’ll ask,” the boy said.

Again he was fed, given cash and supplies, and sent on.

The next evening he reached yet another grand place. This time it was the Queen who came out to the kitchen entrance, curious and wary.

“I’m going to the Dragon of Dybenfart,” the boy said, “to get three feathers from his tail.”

The Queen sighed. “No one comes back from him,” she said. “But if you do, ask him where my gold keys are. I lost them—real gold, old family keys—and it’s like they vanished into the ground.”

“I’ll ask,” the boy promised.

After that he walked until the road turned into rough track and the air began to taste like water. He came to a wide river. No bridge. No ferry dock he could see. He stood there trying to decide whether to follow the bank when a bent old man appeared, hunched and sharp-eyed, like he’d been waiting.

“Where are you headed?” the old man asked.

“To the Dragon of Dybenfart,” the boy said. “If I can find him.”

“I can take you,” the old man said. “I’m the one who ferries people over when they go to him. He lives just up there. Once you’re on the far bank and up the rise, you’ll see his place. And if you speak with him, ask him this from me: how long do I have to keep doing this—carrying people across?”

“I’ll ask,” the boy said.

The old man didn’t have a boat. He simply crouched, let the boy climb onto his back, and waded in with steady strength. The current pushed hard, but the old man didn’t stagger. He set the boy down on the far side, nodded once, and turned back.

The boy climbed the rise and saw it: a massive compound perched above a deep fjord, half mansion, half fortress, lights glinting like watchful eyes. He went in.

Inside, the place was eerily quiet. Only one person was there: a young woman, pale and tense, dressed too nicely for someone who lived like this.

“Are you insane?” she whispered. “Do you know where you are? Christians—ordinary people—don’t come here. Not since I was taken. If the Dragon comes home and smells you, he’ll swallow you whole. And if you die here, it’ll be my fault too.”

“I’m not leaving,” the boy said. “Not until I have three feathers from his tail.”

“You’ll never get them,” she said, and her voice shook because she wanted him to be wrong and feared he wasn’t.

He didn’t move.

She looked at him a long moment, then exhaled like someone making a decision against her own safety. “If you’re going to be this stubborn, I’ll try to help you. See that sword on the wall?”

It was an old ceremonial blade, absurd in a modern room of glass and steel.

“Try to lift it,” she said.

He gripped it with both hands and couldn’t even budge it.

“Then drink,” she said, and handed him a small bottle. It looked like an energy shot, but it smelled sharp as metal.

He took a mouthful. Heat spread through his chest and arms, like blood waking up.

After a while she told him to try again. This time he could move the sword—barely.

“Another sip,” she said.

He drank again, and when he lifted the blade it came free with a clean, terrifying ease.

“Now tell me what you need,” she said.

He told her everything: the King with the dirty well, the King with the missing daughter, the Queen with the lost gold keys, the bent ferryman who wanted to know how long he’d be trapped at the river. And he told her why he’d come—because his wife’s father demanded the three feathers.

As the evening darkened, the woman said, “Hide under the bed. Don’t breathe loud. Don’t move. When we lie down, I’ll ask him the questions like they’re dreams. You listen and remember every word. When he finally falls asleep properly, you crawl out with the sword. When he gets up, you have one chance: one clean strike. And take the feathers immediately—he’ll tear them out himself rather than let anyone have them.”

The boy slid under the bed. Dust and darkness. His heart hammered.

Not long after, the Dragon came home.

Even without seeing him, the boy felt the air change. Heavy. Hot. Like a storm held indoors.

The Dragon paused and sniffed. “I smell Christian bone,” he said.

The woman forced a yawn. “A crow landed on the roof earlier,” she said lightly. “It had something in its beak—maybe that’s what you smell.”

A low sound—half growl, half laugh. “Maybe.”

She served food. The Dragon ate. Then they went to bed.

After a while the woman shifted restlessly and let out a small cry. “Ow.”

“What is it now?” the Dragon rumbled.

“I’m sleeping badly,” she said. “I had the strangest dream.”

“What dream?”

“I dreamed a King came here and asked you why he can’t get clean water in his well.”

The Dragon snorted. “He could figure that out himself,” he said. “There’s an old rotten timber down at the bottom. As long as it’s there, the water won’t clear. Pull it out and the well will run clean. Now be quiet.”

She lay still for a moment, then tossed again and cried out. “Ow.”

The Dragon’s voice sharpened. “Again? What now?”

“I’m sorry,” she said. “Another dream.”

“What?”

“I dreamed a King came asking where his daughter went—she disappeared years ago.”

The Dragon’s answer came too fast, like a crack. “She’s here,” he said. “You. And he’ll never see you again. Sleep. If you keep this up, I’ll break your ribs.”

The woman went still, waited, then—carefully—did it again. “Ow.”

The Dragon flared with anger, sparks of it in his breathing. “What are you doing?”

“I can’t help it,” she whispered. “It’s just—dreams.”

“What dream now?”

“A Queen came asking where her gold keys are. The ones she lost.”

The Dragon made a sound of disgust. “Tell her to look between the bushes where she lay down that time she thinks no one knows about,” he said. “They’re still there. Now stop.”

Silence stretched. The boy under the bed felt sweat slick his back.

Then the woman shifted one last time, like she couldn’t help it, and cried, “Ow.”

The Dragon exploded into rage. “You won’t be right until I snap your neck,” he said, and the air seemed to crackle. “What now?”

The woman’s voice was small. “The ferryman by the river came asking how long he has to carry people over.”

The Dragon laughed, ugly and tired. “That idiot can be free soon,” he said. “When the next one asks to cross, he should throw him into the river and say, ‘You ferry now, until someone relieves you.’ Now let me sleep, or you’ll see what happens.”

The woman went quiet.

The Dragon’s breathing deepened into a harsh snore.

The boy crawled out from under the bed, soundless, took the sword, and waited.

Before dawn the Dragon stood up. His feet hit the floor.

The boy swung once.

The Dragon’s head fell. In the same heartbeat the boy grabbed three feathers from the tail—dark, glossy, wrong-looking in the low light. He shoved them into his pocket before the body could even twitch.

The woman stared, then began to cry—not from fear now, but from something like release. They moved fast, filling bags with cash, jewelry, and whatever valuables could be carried without slowing them down. Before the sun was fully up they were out of the compound and down to the river.

The old ferryman carried loads over and over, muttering as his back took the weight. He was so busy hauling bags and helping the woman across that he forgot his own question until they were all safely on the far bank.

Then he blinked at the boy. “You did ask him, didn’t you? What he said to me?”

“Yes,” the boy said. “He said when someone comes wanting to cross, you should throw him into the river and say, ‘You ferry now, until someone relieves you.’ Then you’ll be free.”

The old man’s face twisted with rage. “Filthy tongue,” he hissed. “If you’d told me that earlier, you’d be the one walking back wet and shaking.”

They traveled on.

At the first royal estate, the Queen demanded to know about her gold keys.

The boy leaned close and whispered what the Dragon had said: “Look between the bushes where you lay that time you remember.”

The Queen’s eyes widened with shock and fury, then she hissed, “Quiet. Not a word,” and pressed a hundred thousand kroner into his hand like payment for silence.

At the next palace, the grieving King asked about his daughter.

The boy stepped aside and brought the young woman forward.

The King made a sound that wasn’t quite a sob and wasn’t quite a laugh. He held her as if she might vanish again. He offered the boy half the kingdom in the old ceremonial way, but the boy said he was already married. So the King gave him two hundred thousand kroner, a car with a driver for the next stretch, and enough gold and silver to weigh down the trunk.

At the third estate, the King with the well listened to the answer and immediately ordered men out to the well. They dug, pulled up the rotten timber from the bottom, and within hours the water ran clear. The King was so pleased he paid the boy three hundred thousand kroner on the spot.

Then the boy went home—back to the ridge farm—so loaded with money and valuables that he looked like someone who’d won the lottery twice and robbed a museum in between. His wife stared at him, laughing and crying at once. People in the valley began to talk about how the miller’s boy had come back shining.

When Per saw the three feathers, he had no choice but to accept the marriage. But when he saw the wealth, his greed woke up so hard it drowned out everything else.

“There was that much at Dybenfart?” Per asked, trying to sound casual.

“There was more,” the boy said. “I couldn’t carry it all. It would take many horse trailers.”

Per’s eyes lit like a screen in the dark. “Then I’ll go,” he said. “Why should you get it all?”

The boy told him the way and said, “Leave your horses on this side of the river. The ferryman will get you across.”

Per packed more food than sense, drove with a convoy of vehicles, and when he reached the river he did exactly as he’d been told. He left the cars and horses behind, walked down to the bank, and called for the ferryman.

The bent old man appeared, expression unreadable, and crouched.

Per climbed onto his back.

They waded out. The current pressed hard.

Halfway across, the ferryman stopped, turned just enough, and with one violent heave threw Per into the middle of the river.

“Now you ferry,” the old man said, voice flat as stone. “Until someone relieves you.”

And if no one has relieved him yet, then Rich Per the Trader is still there, soaked to the bone, carrying strangers across that river to this day.
