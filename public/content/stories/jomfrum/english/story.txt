Far, far away in a great forest there once lived a poor couple. The wife went into labor and bore a child, a lovely little daughter; but because they were so poor, they did not know how they could manage to have the baby baptized. So one day the husband had to go out to look for godparents who could afford to give the offering themselves. He went all day, from one house to another, but everyone said they would gladly stand as godparent—only they didn’t think he had the means to pay the offering on their behalf.

As he was walking home that evening, he met a beautiful lady dressed in splendid clothes, and she looked so kindly and good. She offered to see that the child was baptized—only afterward she would keep the child. The man said he would first have to ask his wife what she thought. But when he came home and told her, she said a flat no.

The next day the man went out again, but no one would be godparent if they had to make the offering themselves, and for all he begged, it did no good. When he was on his way home again toward evening, he met the beautiful lady once more, looking as gentle as ever, and again she made the same offer. He told his wife what had happened, and she said that if he still could not find godparents for the child the next day, then they would just have to let the lady have her, since she looked so good and kind.

On the third day the man went out and still found no godparents; and when he met the lady again that evening, he promised to let her have the child if she would see it baptized and brought up in the Christian faith. So in the morning she came to where the man lived with two men in her company, took the child, and traveled with it to church, and there it was baptized. Then she took it home with her, and the little girl lived with her for several years, and her foster-mother was always good and gentle to her.

When the child had grown big enough to understand things, the foster-mother made ready to go away. “You may go through all the rooms you like,” she said to the girl, “only not into the three chambers I am showing you now.” And then she left. But the girl could not help herself; she opened one of the chamber doors just a crack, and—whisk!—a star flew out. When the foster-mother came back, she was angry with her foster-daughter and threatened to drive her away; but the child wept and begged, and at last she was allowed to stay.

After a while the foster-mother was going away again, and she once more forbade the girl to go into either of the two chambers she had not yet been in. The girl promised to be careful; but after she had been alone for some time, walking about and thinking and wondering what could be inside the other chamber, she could not keep from peeking through the door to look in, and—whisk!—the moon flew out. When the foster-mother came back and saw the moon had escaped, she was deeply distressed and said to the girl that now she could not keep her at all; now she must go away. But the girl cried so bitterly and pleaded so prettily that she was allowed to stay that time too.

After some time the foster-mother was to travel again, and then she impressed upon the girl—who was now nearly grown—that she must not, on any account, try to go into or even look into the third chamber. But after the foster-mother had been gone for a while, and the girl had been alone and bored for a long time, she thought, “Oh, how delightful it would be to have just one little look into the third chamber!” At first she thought she would not do it, for her foster-mother’s sake; but when the thought came to her again, she could not restrain herself any longer—she felt she simply had to see into that chamber. She opened the door a crack, and—whisk!—the sun flew out.

When the foster-mother came back and saw the sun had flown out, she was heartbroken, and said that now the girl could not be allowed to stay with her any longer. The foster-daughter wept and begged even more beautifully than before, but it did no good. “No,” said the mother, “now I must punish you—but you may choose: either you shall become the most beautiful of all women and be unable to speak, or you shall become the ugliest of all and be able to speak. But away from me you must go.” The girl said, “Then I would rather be beautiful.” And beautiful she became—but from that day she was mute.

When she had been sent away from her foster-mother, she walked and wandered through a great, great forest; and no matter how far she went, there never seemed to be an end to it. As evening drew near, she climbed up into a tall tree that stood right above a spring, and sat there to sleep through the night. Near by there was a castle, and early in the morning a maid came from it to fetch water from the spring for the prince’s tea. The servant girl saw the lovely face in the water and thought it was her own; so she threw down the bucket, ran home, and held her head high, saying, “If I’m that beautiful, I’m far too good to go carrying water.”

So another girl was sent for the water, but the same thing happened to her; she too came back and said she was far too beautiful and too good to go and fetch water for the prince. Then the prince went himself, for he wanted to see what this was all about. When he came to the spring, he too saw the reflection, and at once he looked up; and there he beheld the beautiful maiden sitting up in the tree. He coaxed her down and took her home with him, and he insisted on having her for queen because she was so beautiful. But his mother, who was still alive, would not hear of it. “She can’t even speak,” she said, “and she might well be some troll-creature.” But the prince would not be satisfied until he got his way.

After they had lived together for some time, she became with child, and when she was to give birth the prince had a strong guard set around her; but at the hour of delivery they all fell asleep, and when she had borne the child her foster-mother came, cut the baby in its little finger, and smeared the queen’s mouth and fingers with the blood, and said to her, “Now you shall be as sorrowful as I was when you let the star out!” And then she vanished with the child. When the guards awoke, they believed the queen had eaten her own baby, and the old queen wanted to have her burned; but the prince loved her so dearly that at last he begged her free from punishment—though it was by the narrowest margin.

The second time the young queen was to lie in childbed, twice as strong a guard was set as before. But it went just the same as the first time, only now the foster-mother said, “Now you shall be as sorrowful as I was when you let the moon out.” The queen wept and pleaded—for when the foster-mother was there, she could speak—but it helped nothing. Now the old queen was determined she must be burned, but the prince managed to beg her free that time too.

When the queen was to lie in childbed the third time, a triple guard was set around her, but it happened exactly the same: the foster-mother came while the guards slept, took the child, cut its little finger, and smeared the queen’s mouth with blood; and then she said that now the queen would be as sorrowful as she herself had been when the sun was let out.

Now there was no way at all for the prince to save her: she must be burned. But at the very moment they were leading her up onto the pyre, they saw the foster-mother coming with all three children; she led two by the hand, and carried the third in her arms. She went up to the young queen and said, “Here are your children; now you shall have them back again. I am the Virgin Mary, and as sorrowful as you have been now, so sorrowful was I when you let out the sun, the moon, and the star. Now you have suffered punishment for what you did, and from this day on you shall be able to speak again.” How glad the queen and the prince were, anyone can easily imagine, though no one can truly tell it; and from then on they were always happy, and the prince’s mother also came to love the young queen from that time forward.
