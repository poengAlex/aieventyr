Once upon a time, far, far away in a great forest, there lived a poor man and his wife. The wife gave birth to a daughter, the loveliest baby you ever saw. But a baby must be christened, and that was where the trouble began. In those days godparents were expected to lay money on the altar at a christening, and the poor man had not a penny to help them with.

So one morning he set out to find godparents. He went from farm to farm all day long. Everyone would be delighted to stand godparent. Nobody would be delighted to pay.

As he trudged home through the dusk, he met a lady on the path. She wore splendid clothes, and she looked so kind and so good that he stopped in his tracks.

“I’ll see your daughter christened,” she said. “But afterwards, she’s to live with me.”

The man said he would have to ask his wife. His wife said no. She didn’t need to think about it.

The next day he went out again, and it was the same wherever he knocked: they would gladly be godparents, as long as someone else paid. Toward evening, there was the lady on the path again, as gentle as before, making the same offer. This time, when he told his wife, she sat for a long while looking at the baby.

“If you can’t find anyone tomorrow either,” she said at last, “we’ll have to let the lady have her. She sounds so good and kind.”

In the morning she wrapped the baby in her shawl and put her into his arms. “Take her with you,” she said. “They might say no to you. They won’t say no to her.”

They did, though. Every one of them.

That evening he came back along the edge of the forest with the baby asleep in the crook of his arm. The cottage windows were already lit, and there on the path, between him and his own front door, stood the lady. She held out her hand.

He looked down at his daughter for a long time. Then he promised her to the lady, as long as she would see the child christened and brought up a Christian.

Early the next morning the lady came to the cottage with two men. They took the baby to church, and there she was christened, with the lady standing godmother and the two men as godfathers. Then the lady took the little girl home with her. The child lived with her for many years, and her foster-mother was always good and kind to her.

When the girl was old enough to understand what she was told, her foster-mother made ready for a journey.

“You may go wherever you like in the house,” she said, “except into these three rooms.” And she showed her the three doors.

Then off she went. And the girl simply could not leave them alone. She opened one of the doors, just a crack, and whoosh! out flew a star.

When her foster-mother came home, she was angry, and said the girl would have to go. But the child cried and begged, and in the end she was allowed to stay.

Some time later the foster-mother was to travel again, and this time she forbade the girl the two rooms she had not yet been in. The girl promised to be careful. But after she had wandered about alone for a while, wondering and wondering what could be behind the second door, she could not help herself. She opened it a crack to peep in, and whoosh! out flew the moon.

This time the foster-mother was not angry. She was sorrowful. “Now I can’t keep you,” she said. “Now you must go.” But the girl wept so bitterly and pleaded so sweetly that she was allowed to stay that time too.

Then the foster-mother had to go away a third time. The girl was nearly grown now, and her foster-mother told her very gravely that she must not go into the third room. She must not so much as look in.

The foster-mother was gone a long time. The girl drifted through the big, quiet house on her own until she was bored to tears, and one day she thought: “Oh, what fun it would be to have just one little look into the third room!”

No. She wouldn’t, for her foster-mother’s sake. But the thought came back, and the second time it came, she could not hold out. She had to see. She simply had to. She put her hand to the latch, opened the heavy door a crack, and whoosh! out flew the sun. Golden light poured down the corridor, so hot and bright that she reeled back with her hand still on the latch.

When her foster-mother came home and saw that the sun had flown, she was sorrowful to the bottom of her heart, and she said the girl could not stay with her any longer. The girl wept and pleaded more sweetly than ever, but it was no use.

“Now I must punish you,” said her foster-mother. “But you shall choose. Either you will be the most beautiful of all women and never speak, or you will be the ugliest of all women and keep your voice. But away from me you must go.”

“Then I’d rather be beautiful,” said the girl.

And beautiful she became. But from that day on, she could not speak a word.

She wandered off through a great, great forest, and however far she walked, there was no end to it. When evening came, she climbed a tall tree that stood right over a spring, and there she sat and slept the night.

Close by stood a castle. Early in the morning a maid came down from it to fetch water from the spring for the prince’s tea. She leaned over the water and saw a lovely face looking up at her, and she thought it was her own. So she flung down the bucket, marched home, tossed her head and said: “If I’m as pretty as that, I’m far too good to be carrying water.”

Another maid was sent, and the very same thing happened to her. She came back saying she was too pretty and too good to go fetching water for the prince.

Now there were two maids at the castle who were too good to carry water, and the prince still hadn’t had his tea.

In the end he went down to the spring himself, for he wanted to see what was going on. He, too, saw the face in the water, and at once he looked up. There in the tree sat the most beautiful maiden he had ever laid eyes on.

He stood on the bank with his arms raised and coaxed her down. Slowly she reached toward him through the branches, and she came. He took her home with him and said he would make her his queen, because she was so beautiful.

But his mother was still alive, and she would not hear of it. “She can’t even speak,” said the old queen. “For all we know, she’s half troll.”

The prince would not give in until he had her.

When they had been married a while, the young queen was expecting a child. As her time drew near, the prince set a strong guard around her. But at the very hour of the birth the guards all fell asleep, and when the baby had been born, her foster-mother came. She cut the baby’s little finger, smeared the blood around the queen’s mouth and on her fingers, and said: “Now you shall be as sorrowful as I was when you let out the star.”

Then she was gone, and the baby with her.

When the guards woke up, they believed the queen had eaten her own child. The old queen wanted her burned. But the prince loved his wife so dearly that he begged her free, though it was a near thing.

The second time the young queen was to give birth, the guard was twice as strong. It went exactly as before. There were simply more people asleep. Her foster-mother came and took the baby and said: “Now you shall be as sorrowful as I was when you let out the moon.”

The queen wept and pleaded, for when her foster-mother was there, she could speak. But it did no good. Now the old queen was determined to see her burned, but the prince begged her free that time too.

The third time, the guard was three times as strong, and it went just the same. The foster-mother came while the guards slept, took the child, cut its little finger and smeared the queen’s mouth with the blood.

“Now you shall be as sorrowful as I was when you let out the sun,” she said.

There was no saving her now. Not even the prince could do it. She was to be burned.

It was winter. They built the pyre in the snow below the church, and the whole town came out and stood well back from it. The young queen was bound and led forward, pale and silent. Someone had already put a torch to the kindling.

But just as they were leading her up onto the pyre, a hush fell over the crowd, and through it came the foster-mother with all three children. Two she led by the hand, and the third she carried in her arms. She went straight to the young queen and held the children out to her. The prince stared and stared. The old queen stood frozen where she was.

“Here are your children,” said the foster-mother. “Now you shall have them back. I am the Virgin Mary. As sorrowful as you have been, so sorrowful was I when you let out the sun and the moon and the star. Now you have been punished for what you did, and from this day on you shall be able to speak again.”

How happy the young queen and the prince were, anyone can imagine. No one can say.

They lived happily ever after, and from that day on even the old queen loved her daughter-in-law.
