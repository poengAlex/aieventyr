Once there was a woman who set out to hire herself a herdsman. As she walked along, she met a bear.

“Where are you going?” said the bear.

“Oh, I’m going out to hire myself a herdsman,” answered the woman.

“Won’t you take me for your herdsman?” asked the bear.

“Yes—if you can call the cattle, then—” said the woman.

“Hoo—ee!” bellowed the bear.

“No, you I won’t have,” said the woman when she heard that, and she went on her way.

After she had gone a bit farther, she met a wolf.

“Where are you going?” said the wolf.

“I’m going out to hire myself a herdsman,” answered the woman.

“Won’t you take me for your herdsman?” asked the wolf.

“Yes—if you can call them, then—” said the woman.

“Uh—uh!” said the wolf.

“No, you I won’t have,” said the woman.

When she had gone a little farther still, she met a fox.

“Where are you going?” said the fox.

“Oh, I’m going out to hire myself a herdsman,” said the woman.

“Won’t you take me for your herdsman?” asked the fox.

“Yes—if you can call them, then—” said the woman.

“Dil—dal—holom!” sang the fox, so clear and fine.

“Yes, you I’ll have for my herdsman,” said the woman, and she set the fox to herd her livestock.

The first day the fox herded, he ate up all the woman’s goats; the second day he made an end of all her sheep; and the third day he ate up all her cows.

When he came home in the evening, the woman asked what he had done with all her animals.

“The bones are in the river, and the bodies are in the thicket,” said the fox.

She was just standing there churning; but she felt she ought to go and look for them all the same, and while she was gone, the fox slipped down into the churn and ate up the cream.

When the woman came back and saw it, she grew so angry that she took the little bit of cream that was left and flung it at the fox, and he got a splatter on the end of his tail; and that’s why the fox has a white tip to his tail.
