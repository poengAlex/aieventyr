Once upon a time a Rooster and a Hen went into the Hazelwood to pick nuts. The Hen was called Tuppen. She pecked and gulped, pecked and gulped, until a piece of shell stuck fast in her throat.

There she lay among the nuts and broken shells, flapping her wings, and she couldn’t get out so much as a cluck.

The Rooster stared at her with every feather on end.

“Water!” he crowed. “You’ll have water, Tuppen!”

And off he ran to the Spring, which bubbled up clear and cold between mossy stones and splashed down into a little pool.

“Dear Spring, give me water! Water I give to Tuppen, my hen, who lies at death’s door in the Hazelwood.”

“You’ll get no water from me till I get leaves from you,” said the Spring, and kept every drop to itself.

So the Rooster ran to the Linden: “Dear Linden, give me leaves! Leaves I give the Spring, the Spring gives me water, and water I give to Tuppen, my hen, who lies at death’s door in the Hazelwood.”

“You’ll get no leaves from me till I get red-gold ribbons from you,” said the Linden, and shook out its branches.

So the Rooster ran to the Virgin Mary. She was standing on the path at the edge of the wood, barefoot.

“Dear Virgin Mary, give me red-gold ribbons! Red-gold ribbons I give the Linden, the Linden gives me leaves, leaves I give the Spring, the Spring gives me water, and water I give to Tuppen, my hen, who lies at death’s door in the Hazelwood.”

The Virgin Mary smiled kindly and looked down at her bare toes.

“You’ll get no red-gold ribbons from me till I get shoes from you.”

So the Rooster ran to the Shoemaker: “Dear Shoemaker, give me shoes! Shoes I give the Virgin Mary, the Virgin Mary gives me red-gold ribbons, red-gold ribbons I give the Linden, the Linden gives me leaves, leaves I give the Spring, the Spring gives me water, and water I give to Tuppen, my hen, who lies at death’s door in the Hazelwood.”

“You’ll get no shoes from me till I get bristles from you,” said the Shoemaker, without looking up from his stitching. “Pig bristles. That’s what I sew with.”

So the Rooster ran to the Sow, who stood in the mud by a fence at the edge of the wood: “Dear Sow, give me bristles! Bristles I give the Shoemaker, the Shoemaker gives me shoes, shoes I give the Virgin Mary, the Virgin Mary gives me red-gold ribbons, red-gold ribbons I give the Linden, the Linden gives me leaves, leaves I give the Spring, the Spring gives me water, and water I give to Tuppen, my hen, who lies at death’s door in the Hazelwood.”

The Sow looked at the Rooster. Then she looked at her trough.

“You’ll get no bristles from me till I get grain from you,” she grunted.

There’s no arguing with a sow. So the Rooster ran to the Thresher, who was beating out grain on the barn floor: “Dear Thresher, give me grain! Grain I give the Sow, the Sow gives me bristles, bristles I give the Shoemaker, the Shoemaker gives me shoes, shoes I give the Virgin Mary, the Virgin Mary gives me red-gold ribbons, red-gold ribbons I give the Linden, the Linden gives me leaves, leaves I give the Spring, the Spring gives me water, and water I give to Tuppen, my hen, who lies at death’s door in the Hazelwood.”

The Thresher’s stomach rumbled.

“You’ll get no grain from me till I get lefse from you.”

So the Rooster ran to the Baking Wife. She stood at her floury table with a stack of fresh lefse in front of her, soft round flatbreads as big as plates, and a fire roaring in the hearth behind her.

“Dear Baking Wife, give me lefse! Lefse I give the Thresher, the Thresher gives me grain, grain I give the Sow, the Sow gives me bristles, bristles I give the Shoemaker, the Shoemaker gives me shoes, shoes I give the Virgin Mary, the Virgin Mary gives me red-gold ribbons, red-gold ribbons I give the Linden, the Linden gives me leaves, leaves I give the Spring, the Spring gives me water, and water I give to Tuppen, my hen, who lies at death’s door in the Hazelwood.”

The Baking Wife flung out one arm at the fire and the other at the woodpile.

“That hearth eats wood faster than I can carry it,” she said. “You’ll get no lefse from me till I get firewood from you.”

So the Rooster ran to the Woodcutter: “Dear Woodcutter, give me firewood! Firewood I give the Baking Wife, the Baking Wife gives me lefse, lefse I give the Thresher, the Thresher gives me grain, grain I give the Sow, the Sow gives me bristles, bristles I give the Shoemaker, the Shoemaker gives me shoes, shoes I give the Virgin Mary, the Virgin Mary gives me red-gold ribbons, red-gold ribbons I give the Linden, the Linden gives me leaves, leaves I give the Spring, the Spring gives me water, and water I give to Tuppen, my hen, who lies at death’s door in the Hazelwood.”

The Woodcutter was holding a handle with nothing on the end of it. The axe head had flown off into the heather.

“You’ll get no firewood from me till I get an axe from you.”

So the Rooster ran to the Smith. By now he was so out of breath that the words tripped over one another, but he got them out, every one: “Dear Smith, give me an axe! An axe I give the Woodcutter, the Woodcutter gives me firewood, firewood I give the Baking Wife, the Baking Wife gives me lefse, lefse I give the Thresher, the Thresher gives me grain, grain I give the Sow, the Sow gives me bristles, bristles I give the Shoemaker, the Shoemaker gives me shoes, shoes I give the Virgin Mary, the Virgin Mary gives me red-gold ribbons, red-gold ribbons I give the Linden, the Linden gives me leaves, leaves I give the Spring, the Spring gives me water, and water I give to Tuppen, my hen, who lies at death’s door in the Hazelwood.”

The forge was still glowing, but the Smith pointed at the sacks in the corner. They were empty.

“You’ll get no axe from me till I get charcoal from you.”

So the Rooster ran to the Charcoal Burner, who was tending his smoking kiln deep in the forest, black with soot from his hood to his boots. The Rooster took the deepest breath of his life: “Dear Charcoal Burner, give me charcoal! Charcoal I give the Smith, the Smith gives me an axe, an axe I give the Woodcutter, the Woodcutter gives me firewood, firewood I give the Baking Wife, the Baking Wife gives me lefse, lefse I give the Thresher, the Thresher gives me grain, grain I give the Sow, the Sow gives me bristles, bristles I give the Shoemaker, the Shoemaker gives me shoes, shoes I give the Virgin Mary, the Virgin Mary gives me red-gold ribbons, red-gold ribbons I give the Linden, the Linden gives me leaves, leaves I give the Spring, the Spring gives me water, and water I give to Tuppen, my hen, who lies at death’s door in the Hazelwood.”

Then he stood there panting, waiting for the next demand.

But the Charcoal Burner didn’t want anything. He felt sorry for the Rooster, and he filled a sack with charcoal and held it out to him.

And after that there was no stopping it. The Smith got charcoal, and the Woodcutter an axe, and the Baking Wife firewood, and the Thresher lefse, and the Sow grain, and the Shoemaker bristles, and the Virgin Mary shoes, and the Linden red-gold ribbons, and the Spring leaves, and the Rooster water, and he gave it to Tuppen, his hen, who lay at death’s door in the Hazelwood.

The Rooster held out the water, and Tuppen drank. She swallowed, and down went the nutshell. Her wings grew still, and the red came back into her comb.

And so she was well again. The Rooster took a little longer.
