In the days when Our Lord and Saint Peter still walked about here on earth, they came one day to a woman who sat baking flatbread. Her name was Gertrud, and she wore a red cap on her head.

They had walked a long way and were both hungry, so Our Lord asked her very kindly for a flatbread to taste.

“Of course,” said Gertrud.

She pinched off a tiny scrap of dough and rolled it out on the baking board. But when she laid it on the griddle, it spread and spread until it covered the griddle from rim to rim.

“No, that one’s too big,” said Gertrud, and pulled it back towards her. “You can’t have that one.”

So she took an even smaller scrap. The same thing happened.

“That one’s too big as well,” said Gertrud. “You can’t have that one either.”

The third time she took a scrap no bigger than a pea. But this flatbread, too, came out far too big. Now there were three enormous flatbreads beside her.

“Then I’ve nothing to give you,” said Gertrud. “You may as well go on your way without a taste. They all turn out too big.”

At that, Our Lord grew angry and raised his hand.

“Because you grudged me so much as a mouthful,” he said, “this shall be your punishment: you shall become a bird. You shall find your dry food between the bark and the wood, and you shall have nothing to drink except when it rains.”

The last word was hardly out of his mouth before she turned into the Gertrud-bird. Up she flew from the baking board and straight up the chimney in a cloud of soot, while Saint Peter stood and stared.

You can still see her to this day, flying about the woods with her red cap on her head and black all over, for the chimney blackened her. Some people call her the black woodpecker. She pecks and taps at the trees for her food, and when rain is on the way, you can hear her piping for it. She is always thirsty.
