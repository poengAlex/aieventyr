Long ago, in the days when our Lord and Saint Peter walked on earth, they came to a blacksmith’s shop.

The blacksmith had made a bargain with the Devil: for seven years he would be the best smith of them all, and after that he would belong to the Devil. They had both signed the paper. That was why the blacksmith had painted big letters over his door:

“Here lives the Master of all Masters!”

When our Lord saw that, he stepped inside.

“Who are you?” our Lord asked the blacksmith.

“Read the sign over the door,” the blacksmith said. “But maybe you can’t read, so you’ll have to wait until someone comes who can help you.”

Before our Lord could answer, a man arrived leading a horse and asked the blacksmith to shoe it.

“May I do it?” our Lord asked.

“You can try,” said the blacksmith. “You can’t make it worse than I can fix again.”

So our Lord went out to the horse. He lifted one hoof, worked quickly and carefully, and in no time the shoe fit perfectly. Then he did the next hoof, and the next, and the last—calmly, kindly, and as if he had done it all his life.

All the while, the blacksmith stood and watched.

“You’re not such a bad smith after all,” he said.

“Do you think so?” our Lord replied.

A little later, the blacksmith’s mother came hobbling to the doorway and called him home for dinner. She was very old and bent, and she had to walk slowly.

“Now watch closely,” our Lord said.

He helped the old woman sit by the warm hearth, brushed the soot from her shawl, and spoke to her so gently that her face brightened. It was as if the weariness lifted from her shoulders for a moment, and she stood a little straighter and smiled like someone much younger.

The blacksmith blinked.

“I’ll say what I said,” he muttered. “You’re no poor smith. The sign over my door says: ‘Master of all Masters,’ but I’ll tell you plainly—one can learn as long as one lives.” And with that he went home to eat.

When he came back to the shop, another man rode up and asked to have his horse shod.

“That will soon be done,” said the blacksmith. “I’ve just learned a new way of shoeing that’s very handy when the day is short.”

But instead of working one hoof at a time, he hurried and fussed, trying to do everything all at once. He heated too much, rushed too hard, and made a muddle of the shoes. One shoe didn’t fit, another pinched, and the horse stamped and snorted, unhappy with all the clanging and haste.

In the end, the man shook his head, and the blacksmith had to pay for new shoes and the trouble besides. The blacksmith didn’t like that at all.

Just then, an old poor woman came walking by, asking for a bite to eat.

The blacksmith thought, “If one thing didn’t work, maybe another will.” And he said, “Come in, and I’ll make you young and fine in the blink of an eye—free of charge!”

The old woman didn’t trust him, but he was so sure of himself that he tried anyway. He warmed the hearth too much, blew the bellows too hard, and filled the place with smoke and sparks. The poor woman coughed, waved her hands, and hurried right back out again, scolding him the whole way.

Our Lord looked at the blacksmith.

“That was not kindly done,” he said.

“Oh, there’s surely not many who care about her,” the blacksmith grumbled. “But it’s the Devil’s shame, too—he barely keeps what’s promised on my sign.”

“If I were to grant you three wishes,” our Lord said, “what would you wish for?”

“Try me,” said the blacksmith. “Then you’ll see.”

So our Lord granted him three wishes.

“First,” said the blacksmith, “I wish that anyone I tell to climb up into the pear tree by my smithy wall must stay there until I tell him to come down.

“Second, I wish that anyone I tell to sit in the armchair inside my workshop must stay seated until I tell him to stand up.

“And third, I wish that anyone I tell to crawl into the little wire purse I keep in my pocket must stay inside until I let him out.”

“You wished like a foolish man,” Saint Peter said. “First of all you should have wished for God’s friendship and grace.”

“I didn’t dare wish for something so high,” the blacksmith answered.

Then our Lord and Saint Peter said farewell and walked on.

Time passed, and when the seven years were up, the Devil came—as the contract said—to fetch the blacksmith.

“Are you ready now?” the Devil asked, poking his nose into the smithy door.

“Oh, I really must finish this one nail first,” the blacksmith said. “While you wait, climb up into that pear tree and pick yourself a pear to nibble. You might be hungry after your journey.”

The Devil liked the sound of that. Up he climbed into the pear tree.

Then the blacksmith scratched his head and said, “Now that I think about it, I won’t be able to finish this nail for a long, long time. This iron is terribly stubborn. You’ll have to sit and rest up there until I’m done.”

The Devil begged and pleaded to come down, but nothing helped. At last he promised he would not return for four more years if only the blacksmith would let him down.

“Well then, you may come down,” said the blacksmith.

Four years later, the Devil came again to fetch him.

“Now you must be finished,” said the Devil. “Surely you’ve had time to fix that nail.”

“The head is on,” said the blacksmith, “but the point still needs sharpening. I’ve never worked such stubborn iron. While I do that, why don’t you sit in my armchair and rest? You must be tired.”

“Thank you,” said the Devil, and he sat down in the armchair.

But the moment he settled in, the blacksmith sighed and said, “When I think about it properly, I won’t have that point sharpened for another four years.”

First the Devil asked politely, then he grew angry and threatened. But the blacksmith only shrugged and blamed the iron, and kept saying, “At least you’re sitting comfortably. In four years you’ll be free to stand up—right on the minute.”

There was no other way: the Devil had to promise not to come for four more years, and then the blacksmith told him he could get up. The Devil hurried away as fast as he could.

Four years after that, the Devil came once more.

“Now you are surely ready,” he said, sticking his nose into the doorway.

“Ready as can be,” said the blacksmith. “We can go whenever you like. But there’s one thing I’ve wondered for a long time: is it true what people say, that you can make yourself as small as you want?”

“Of course it’s true,” said the Devil.

“Then do me a favor,” said the blacksmith. “Crawl into this little wire purse and check whether there’s a hole in the bottom. I’m afraid I’ll lose my travel money.”

“Gladly,” said the Devil. He made himself tiny and crawled into the purse.

The moment he was inside, the blacksmith snapped it shut.

“It’s whole and tight everywhere,” the Devil called from inside.

“That’s good to hear,” said the blacksmith, “but better safe than sorry. I’ll just tighten it a little, just to be certain.”

And he held the purse near the warm hearth and tapped the metal gently with his hammer—tap, tap, tap.

“Hey! It’s getting hot in here!” yelped the Devil.

“I can’t help it,” said the blacksmith. “People say you must work while the metal is warm.”

He gave it a few more quick taps—nothing that would break the purse, but enough to make the Devil very uncomfortable.

“Let me out!” the Devil cried. “Please! If you let me out, I’ll never come back again!”

“Well,” said the blacksmith, “now I think it’s tight enough. You may come out.”

He opened the purse, and the Devil shot out like a gust of wind and ran off so fast he didn’t dare look back.

After some time, the blacksmith began to worry.

“Maybe I did wrong to make such an enemy of the Devil,” he thought. “If I can’t get into Heaven, and I can’t get into the Devil’s place either, where will I go?”

So he decided he would find out sooner rather than later. He slung his big hammer over his shoulder and set off down the road.

After he had walked a good while, he came to a crossroads where the road to Heaven turned away from the road to the Devil’s place. There he caught up with a thin tailor’s apprentice hurrying along, holding his little pressing iron.

“Good day,” said the blacksmith. “Where are you going?”

“To Heaven—if I can get in,” said the tailor. “And you?”

“We won’t be walking together long,” said the blacksmith. “I think I’ll try the Devil’s place first. I have a bit of acquaintance with him.”

So they said farewell and each went his own way. The blacksmith was strong and walked fast, and it wasn’t long before he stood at the gate of the Devil’s place. He asked the guard to tell the Devil that someone outside wished to speak with him.

“Go out and ask who it is,” said the Devil, and the guard did.

“Greet the Devil and tell him it’s the blacksmith—the one with the purse, you know,” said the man. “And ask him nicely to let me in at once, because I’ve worked until noon today and walked a long way since.”

When the Devil heard that message, he shouted to the guard, “Lock the gate with all the locks you have—and add one more besides! If he gets in here, he’ll turn the whole place upside down!”

The blacksmith heard the bolts and chains being fastened tighter than before.

“No help to be had here,” he muttered. “Then I’ll have to try Heaven.”

He turned around and went back until he reached the crossroads again, and there he took the road the tailor had taken.

He was annoyed at all the walking back and forth, so he hurried as much as he could. At last he reached Heaven’s gate just as Saint Peter opened it a little—only a little—so that the thin tailor could slip through.

The blacksmith was still several steps away.

“I’d better hurry,” he thought.

He grabbed his big hammer and threw it into the narrow opening at the same moment the tailor slipped in; but whether the blacksmith went through that opening or where he went after that, I cannot say.
