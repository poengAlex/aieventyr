There was once a couple who had a son, and his name was Halvor. From the time he was a little boy, he never wanted to do anything at all—he only sat poking about in the ashes. His parents sent him out to learn all sorts of trades, but Halvor never stayed anywhere; for once he had been gone a few days, he would run away from his apprenticeship, come home, and sit down in the hearth to rake the ashes again.

But one day a skipper came along and asked Halvor if he wouldn’t like to go with him to sea and see foreign lands. Yes, Halvor would like that well enough; and he wasn’t long getting ready.

How long they sailed I can’t say; but at last a fierce storm came upon them, and when it had blown itself out and the sea fell calm again, they did not know where they were. They had drifted to a strange coast that none of them had ever seen before.

And since it was so still that not a feather stirred, they lay there. Halvor asked the skipper if he might go ashore and have a look around, for he would rather walk than lie there sleeping. “Do you think you can show yourself among folk?” said the skipper. “You’ve no clothes but those rags you stand and walk in.” But Halvor kept on begging, and in the end he got leave—only he must come back when it began to blow.

So he went, and it was a beautiful country. Everywhere he came there were broad plains of fields and meadows, but he saw not a soul. Then it began to blow a little, but Halvor thought he hadn’t seen enough yet, and he wanted to go farther, to see if he could find people. After a while he came upon a great road, smooth enough that you could have rolled an egg along it. Halvor followed it, and toward evening he saw a huge castle far away, with light shining from every window. He had walked all day with hardly any food, and he was dreadfully hungry; yet the nearer he came to the castle, the more afraid he grew.

Inside the castle it was blazing warm. Halvor went into the kitchen, finer than any kitchen he had ever seen; there were cooking tools of gold and silver, but not a person in sight. After standing there a while with no one coming, he went and opened a door. Inside sat a princess, spinning at her wheel.

“Oh, no, no!” she cried. “Dare Christian folk come here? But you’d best go at once, unless the troll is to swallow you; for a troll lives here with three heads.”

“I don’t care if he had four,” said the boy. “I’d still like to see that fellow. And I’m not going anywhere—I’ve done no wrong. But you must give me food, for I’m fearfully hungry.”

When Halvor had eaten his fill, the princess told him he should try whether he could swing the sword that hung on the wall. No, he couldn’t swing it—he couldn’t even lift it.

“Well,” said the princess, “then you must take a swallow from the flask that hangs beside it, for that’s what the troll does when he’s going out to use the sword.”

Halvor took a swallow, and at once he could swing the sword as if it were nothing at all. Now, he thought, the troll could come whenever he pleased.

And sure enough, the troll came rushing in; Halvor stood behind the door.

“Huttetu! It smells of Christian man’s blood here!” said the troll, and shoved its head in through the doorway.

“Yes, you’ll have something to smell,” said Halvor, and hewed off all its heads.

The princess was so glad to be saved that she danced and sang; but then she thought of her sisters, and said, “If only my sisters were saved as well!”

“Where are they?” asked Halvor.

So she told him: one had been taken by a troll at a castle six miles away, and the other had been taken at a castle nine miles beyond that again.

“But first,” she said, “you must help me get this carcass out of here.”

Halvor was so strong that he dragged everything away and had the place cleared and clean in no time.

So he lived well there, and the next morning at first light he set off. He had no rest in him—he walked and ran all day. When he caught sight of the castle, he grew a little afraid again; it was far grander than the other, yet here too not a human being was to be seen. Halvor went into the kitchen and did not linger there either, but went straight on in.

“Oh, dare Christian folk come here?” cried the princess. “I don’t know how long it is since I came here, but all that time I’ve not seen a Christian soul. You’d best be off, for a troll lives here with six heads.”

“I’m not going,” said Halvor, “even if he had six to spare.”

“He’ll swallow you alive,” said the princess.

But it was no use: Halvor would not leave. He wasn’t afraid of the troll, but he wanted food and drink, for he was hungry from the journey.

He got as much as he wanted; but then the princess tried to make him go again.

“No,” said Halvor. “I’ve done no wrong, and I’ve nothing to fear.”

“He doesn’t ask about that,” said the princess. “He’ll take you without law or right. But since you won’t go, try if you can swing the sword the troll uses in war.”

He couldn’t swing it; but the princess told him to take a swallow from the flask that hung beside it, and when he did, he could swing the sword.

Just then the troll came. It was so huge and thick that it had to turn sideways to get in through the door. When it got the first head inside, it shouted, “Huttetu! It smells of Christian man’s blood here!” At that Halvor chopped off the first head, and then all the others.

The princess was so glad she didn’t know which leg to stand on; but then she thought of her sisters, and wished they too were saved. Halvor thought there would surely be a way, and wanted to set off at once; but first he must help the princess get rid of the troll’s body, and then he set out the next morning.

It was a long way to the last castle, and he walked and ran to arrive in time. Toward evening he saw it, and it was far finer than the other two. Now he was hardly afraid at all; he went through the kitchen and straight inside. There sat a princess so beautiful there was no measure to it.

She said the same as the others—that there had been no Christian folk since she came there—and begged him to go, or the troll would swallow him alive. It had nine heads, she said.

“Yes, if it has nine and nine and nine more besides, I’m not going,” said Halvor, and he went to stand by the stove.

The princess begged him as prettily as she could to leave so the troll would not eat him; but Halvor said, “Let him come when he likes.”

So she gave him the troll’s sword and told him to take a swallow from the flask so he could swing it.

And right then the troll came, with a great rushing noise. It was even bigger and thicker than the other two, and it too had to go sideways to get through the door. “Huttetu! It smells of Christian man’s blood here,” it said. At once Halvor chopped off the first head, and then all the rest; but the last was the toughest of them all, and it was the hardest work Halvor had ever done to get it off, though he felt strong enough, and then some.

Now all the princesses came together at that castle, and they were as happy as they had ever been in their lives. They were fond of Halvor, and he of them, and he could have whichever one he liked best; but the youngest was fonder of him than all the three.

Halvor went about, quiet and thoughtful, so still and silent. The princess asked him what he longed for, and whether he didn’t like being with them. Yes, he liked it well enough—they had plenty to live on, and he had it good there; but he longed to go home, for his parents were still alive, and he dearly wished to see them again.

They said that could be managed. “You’ll come safely there and back again, if you’ll do as we advise,” said the princesses. Halvor said he would do nothing but what they told him. Then they dressed him so he looked as splendid as a king’s son, and they put a ring on his finger; it was such that he could wish himself both to and fro with it. But they said he must not throw it away, and he must not speak their names, for then all the glory would be ended, and he would never see them again.

“If only I were at home—and home were here!” said Halvor. And as he wished, so it was: before he knew a word of it, he was standing outside his parents’ cottage.

It was dusk in the evening, and when they saw such a fine, stately stranger come in, they were quite flustered and began bowing and curtsying.

Halvor asked if he might stay there and have lodging for the night.

No, he couldn’t possibly. “We don’t have it so,” they said. “We have neither this nor that that such a gentleman could be served with. It’s best you go up to the farm; it’s not far—you can see the chimney from here. They have everything there.”

Halvor didn’t like the idea; he wanted to stay. But the old couple insisted he should go up to the farm, for there he could get food and drink, while they did not even have a chair to offer him.

“No,” said Halvor, “I won’t go up there until tomorrow morning. Let me stay here tonight—I can sit in the fireplace.”

They couldn’t well say anything against that, so Halvor sat down in the hearth and raked the ashes, just as he used to do when he lazed at home.

They talked of this and that, and told Halvor all sorts of things, and then he asked whether they had ever had a child.

“Yes, they had had a boy named Halvor,” they said, “but they didn’t know where he had wandered, nor whether he was dead or alive.”

“Couldn’t that be me, then?” said Halvor.

“I should think so!” said the old woman, and shifted herself. “That Halvor was so lazy and good-for-nothing he never did a thing, and he was so ragged that one patch knocked the other senseless on him. There could never come such a man out of him as you are, sir.”

After a little while the old woman came over to the hearth to poke at the warmth, and when the glow of the embers lit Halvor—just as it used to when he sat at home raking in the ashes—she knew him again.

“Well, if it isn’t you, Halvor!” she cried. Such joy came over the old parents there was no describing it, and now he had to tell them how everything had gone with him.

The old woman was so delighted with him that she wanted to go up to the farm at once and show him to the girls there, who had always been so proud and stuck-up.

So she went ahead and Halvor followed. When she got there, she told them Halvor had come home again, and now they were to see how fine he was—he looked like a prince, she said.

“I should think so,” said the girls, sniggering and nudging one another. “He’s the same ragamuffin he’s always been.”

At that moment Halvor came in, and the girls were so taken aback that they forgot the shirt they had left in the fireplace where they sat delousing themselves, and flew out in their skirts alone. When they came in again, they were so ashamed they scarcely dared look at Halvor, whom they had always been so proud and haughty toward before.

“Yes, you’ve always thought yourselves so fine and pretty that no one could be your equal,” said Halvor. “But you should only see the eldest princess I saved. Beside her you look like little shepherd girls—and the middle one is even lovelier. But the youngest, who is my sweetheart, is lovelier than both sun and moon. God grant they were only here, so you could see!”

He had scarcely said it before they were standing there. But then Halvor felt sick at heart, for now he remembered what the princesses had warned him.

Up at the farm they held a feast for the princesses and made a great fuss over them, but they would not stay. “We want to go down to your parents,” they said to Halvor, “and then we want to go out and look about.” So he went with them.

Then they came to a large lake below the farm. Close by the water there was a lovely green slope, and there the princesses wanted to sit and rest a while, for they said it was so beautiful to sit and look out over the lake.

They sat there, and after a while the youngest princess said, “I suppose I may pick your hair a little, Halvor.” Yes, Halvor laid his head in her lap; she combed and picked his hair, and it wasn’t long before Halvor fell asleep. Then she took the ring from him and put another in its place, and said, “Hold fast to me, as I hold fast to you—and if only we were at Soria Moria Castle!”

When Halvor woke, he knew well enough that he had lost the princesses, and he began to weep and take on. He was so miserable no one could comfort him. No matter what his parents begged, he would not stay; he took farewell and said he would likely never see them again, for if he did not find the princesses, he didn’t think life was worth living.

He had three hundred dalers left; he put them in his pocket and set off. After he had walked a while, he met a man with a decent horse. Halvor wanted to buy it and began bargaining.

“I hadn’t really meant to sell it,” said the man, “but if we can agree, then—”

Halvor asked what he wanted for it.

“I didn’t give much for it, and it isn’t worth much,” said the man. “It’s a good horse to ride, but it’s not much use for pulling. Still, it can carry your food-bag and you as well, when you take turns walking.”

At last they agreed on a price. Halvor put his bag on the horse, and then he walked a bit and rode a bit. In the evening he came to a green mound, and there was a big tree where he sat down. He let the horse loose and did not lie down to sleep, but took out his food-bag. At daybreak he set off again, for he felt he could have no rest in him.

So he went and rode the whole day through a great forest, where many green clearings shone brightly between the trees. He did not know where he was, nor where he was headed; but he gave himself no more time to rest than it took to let the horse have a bite and to open his bundle when he came to one of the clearings. He walked and he rode, and never did the forest seem to end.

But at dusk on the second day he saw a light glimmering among the trees. “If only there are folk there,” thought Halvor, “so I can warm myself and get something to eat.”

When he came closer, it was a small miserable hut, and through the window he saw an old couple inside; they were so old and gray-headed as a dove, and the old woman had a nose so long she sat by the fireplace and used it as a poker.

“Good evening,” said Halvor.

“Good evening,” said the old woman. “But what business have you, coming here?” she asked. “No Christian folk have been here in a hundred years.”

So Halvor said he was bound for Soria Moria Castle and asked if she knew the way.

“No,” said the old woman, “I don’t. But soon the moon will come up, and I’ll ask him. He’ll know it well enough—he must have seen it, for he shines on everything.”

When the moon stood bright and clear over the treetops, the old woman went out. “Moon, moon!” she shouted. “Can you tell me the way to Soria Moria Castle?”

“No,” said the moon, “I can’t. For the time I shone there, a cloud was before me.”

“Wait a little longer,” said the old woman to Halvor. “Soon the West Wind will come; he surely knows it, for he blows into every corner.”

“Oh! Oh! You have a horse too, do you?” said the old woman when she came back in. “Go on, let the poor creature out into the pasture yard, and don’t have it standing here by the door and starving! But won’t you trade it to me?” she said. “We’ve a pair of old boots here—you can take fifteen leagues in every step with them. You’ll have them for the horse, and then you’ll reach Soria Moria Castle sooner.”

Halvor was willing at once, and the old woman was so pleased with the horse she was nearly dancing. “For now I can ride to church myself!” she said.

Halvor had no peace in him and wanted to be off right away, but the old woman said there was no hurry. “Lie on the bench and sleep a little—you’ll get no bed here,” she said. “I’ll keep watch for when the West Wind comes.”

And sure enough, the West Wind came roaring along so the walls creaked. Out went the old woman.

“West Wind! West Wind! Can you tell me the way to Soria Moria Castle? Here is one who must go there.”

“Yes, I know it well,” said the West Wind. “I’m just on my way there to dry the wedding clothes that are to be used. If he is quick on his feet, he can come with me.”

Out went Halvor.

“You’d better hurry if you want company,” said the West Wind, and off they went far away, over moor and ridge and mountain and shore, and Halvor had all he could do to keep up.

“Well, I have no time to go with you any farther,” said the West Wind. “I must go and tear down a stretch of spruce forest first, before I come to the bleaching meadow to dry the clothes. But if you walk along the edge of the ridge, you’ll come to some girls washing clothes, and then you won’t have far left to Soria Moria Castle.”

After a while Halvor came to the girls who stood washing, and they asked if he had seen the West Wind. He was to come there and dry the clothes for the wedding. “Yes,” said Halvor, “he’s only gone to break down a patch of spruce; it won’t be long before he’s here.” And then he asked them the way to Soria Moria Castle.

They set him on the right path, and when he drew near the castle there were so many horses and people that it swarmed. But Halvor was so ragged and torn from following the West Wind through brush and bog that he kept to the side and would not step forward until the last day, when they were to sit down to dinner.

Now, as custom was, they were to drink to the bride on behalf of the maidens, and the cupbearer was to drink to them all—bride and bridegroom and knights and squires. At last he came to Halvor as well. Halvor drank the toast, slipped the ring—the one the princess had put on his finger when he lay by the lake—down into the glass, and asked the cupbearer to greet the bride and carry the glass to her.

At once the princess rose from the table. “Who has best deserved to have one of us,” she said, “the one who saved us, or the one who sits there as bridegroom?”

There could be only one opinion about that, said everyone. And when Halvor heard it, he was not long in throwing off his beggar rags and making himself ready as the bridegroom.

“Yes—there he is, the true one!” cried the youngest princess when she saw him, and she thrust the other man aside and held the wedding with Halvor.
