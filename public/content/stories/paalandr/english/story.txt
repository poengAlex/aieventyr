Once upon a time there was a woman who had a deaf husband. He was a kind, good-natured soul, and that was exactly why she was fonder of the young fellow in the other house on the farm. Everyone called him Pål Andrestuen, which is to say Pål of the Other House.

The farmhand had long since noticed something between those two. One day he said to the wife:

“Would you dare bet ten daler, missus, that I can’t make you blurt out your own shame?”

“I’d dare that any day,” she said. So they bet ten daler on it.

Now one day the farmhand and the deaf husband were in the barn, threshing. Through the open door the farmhand saw Pål Andrestuen slip across the yard and into the house. He said nothing. But a good while before breakfast, he spun round toward the barn door and shouted:

“Coming!”

“What, are we going in already?” said the husband. He hadn’t noticed a thing.

“Well, we’d better, if the missus is calling,” said the farmhand.

Out in the passage he began to cough. He coughed and hawked and cleared his throat, and then he started all over again, to give the wife time to get Pål out of sight. Inside, a great dish of sour-cream porridge stood on the table, with a pool of melted butter in the middle.

“Well, well, Mother!” said the husband. “Sour-cream porridge on a weekday?”

“Oh yes, you’re having it,” said the wife, and she was a good deal sourer than the cream.

When the men had eaten and gone, Pål crept out of the side room.

“That boy has the Devil’s own snout,” the wife hissed. “This was his doing. Now get off home, and I’ll bring you a bite down in the meadow this afternoon.”

But the farmhand was out in the passage, and he heard every word.

“Master,” he said to the husband, “we’d better go down and mend that stretch of fence that blew over. The pigs from the Other House are rooting up our whole meadow.”

“We’d better, then,” said the husband, for he did whatever anyone told him.

In the afternoon the wife came scurrying down through the meadow with something bulky under her apron.

“Well, well, Mother!” said the husband. “I don’t think you’re yourself any more. A bite in the afternoon as well?”

“Oh yes, you’ll have that too,” she said, and she was wilder than ever.

So they sat in the grass and feasted on lefse, soft Norwegian flatbread, with plenty of butter and a dram of brandy to wash it down.

“I’ll take a bit over to Pål Andrestuen as well, missus,” said the farmhand. “I don’t suppose he’s had a bite all day.”

“Yes, do,” said the wife, and just like that she was all smiles again.

The farmhand set off with the lefse, and as he went, he broke off little bits and dropped them on the path, one here and one there.

He found Pål Andrestuen out at his plough.

“You’d better watch yourself,” the farmhand said, leaning in close. “The old man has noticed you come over to the missus rather often, and he doesn’t like it one bit. Now he’s sworn to put his axe in you the moment he sets eyes on you.”

Pål went white, and his hands shook on the plough handles. The farmhand strolled back to the husband.

“There’s something wrong with Pål’s plough,” he said. “He asked if you’d bring your axe and come and have a look at it.”

So the husband took his axe and went. But no sooner had Pål Andrestuen seen him coming than he took to his heels and ran for his life.

The husband looked the plough over from above and below and from both sides, and he couldn’t find a thing wrong with it. So he walked back again, and on the way he picked up every crumb the farmhand had dropped. A man doesn’t leave good lefse lying on the ground.

The wife was still down by the fence, waiting for news of Pål. She had seen her husband go over to him with the axe. She had seen Pål run. And now her husband was coming back, stooping every few steps to pick something up. What in the world was he gathering?

“Oh, he’s picking up stones,” said the farmhand. “He’s noticed Pål Andrestuen is forever running over to our house, and the old man doesn’t like it one bit. Now he’s sworn he’ll stone you to death, missus.”

The wife picked up her skirts and ran for home as fast as her legs would carry her.

“What’s she running off like that for?” asked the husband when he came up.

“Oh, there’s likely a fire at home,” said the farmhand.

So the husband ran too. The wife looked back over her shoulder and saw him coming, and she ran and she screamed:

“Oh, don’t kill me, don’t kill me! I’ll never let Pål Andrestuen come to me again!”

“Those ten daler are mine!” shouted the farmhand.
