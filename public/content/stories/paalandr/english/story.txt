Once there was a wife who had a deaf husband, and that was a fine thing; for that reason she was all the fonder of the lad in the other room, the one they called Pål Andrestuen.

The farmhand in the deaf man’s service soon noticed there was something between those two, and one day he said to the woman, “Would you wager ten dollars, Mother, that I can make you confess your own shame?”

“Yes, I’d dare that well enough,” she said, and they wagered ten dollars.

Now one day the farmhand and the deaf man were up in the barn threshing, when the farmhand saw that Pål Andrestuen had come to the wife. He said nothing; but a good while before the midday meal he turned toward the barn door and suddenly shouted, “Well then!”

“Well now, are we going in already?” said the man; he hadn’t noticed a thing.

“Yes, we must be, since Mother’s calling,” said the farmhand.

When they came into the passageway, the farmhand began to cough and clear his throat so the wife could get Pål Andrestuen hidden away. And when they came in, there was a dish of sour-cream porridge on the table.

“No, no, Mother!” said the husband. “Are we having sour-cream porridge today?”

“Oh yes, you are,” said the wife; she was sour and wild.

When they had eaten their fill and gone out again, she said to Pål, “That boy’s the very devil’s snout; this was his doing. But now you’d better be off, and I’ll come down to the meadow to you with the afternoon meal.”

The farmhand stood in the passageway and listened.

“Father,” he said, “now I think it’s best we go down into the valley and fix the bit of fence that’s blown down, because the pigs from that other room are going and rooting up our meadow.”

“Yes, we’d best do it,” said the man, for he did whatever they told him.

At afternoon-meal time the wife came skittering down into the meadow with something under her apron.

“No, no, Mother! Now I think you’re not yourself anymore,” said the husband. “Are we to have the afternoon meal as well?”

“Oh yes, you’ll have it,” she said; and she was wilder still.

And they ate well—both flatbread and butter—and got brandy besides.

“I’ll go over to Pål Andrestuen with a bit too, Mother,” said the farmhand, “for he’s likely not had any afternoon meal.”

“Yes, do,” said the wife; and then she turned mild again all at once.

On the way the farmhand picked at the flatbread and let fall a little crumb now and then as he walked.

To Pål Andrestuen he said, “You’d better watch yourself, for our old fellow has noticed you come over to the womanfolk here far too often; he doesn’t like it, not one bit, and now he’s sworn he’ll bury the axe in you the moment he sets eyes on you.”

Pål was frightened out of his wits, and the farmhand went back to the husband.

“There’s something not right with Pål’s plough,” he said, “and he asked if you’d bring the axe with you and come over and set it a bit straight.”

So the man went and took the axe along; but Pål Andrestuen didn’t catch sight of him until he was close, and then he took to his legs as fast as he could. The husband turned the plough this way and that and looked it over on every side, and when he couldn’t see anything wrong with it, he went back again. But on the way he picked up the flatbread crumbs the farmhand had dropped.

The wife stood a while watching this and wondered what it was the man was gathering up.

“Oh, Father’s picking up stones,” said the farmhand, “for he’s noticed that Pål Andrestuen runs over here so often, and the old fellow doesn’t like it, not one bit, and now he’s sworn he’ll stone Mother to death.”

Off the wife went as fast as she could.

“What is Mother running off after like that?” asked the husband when he came up.

“Oh, there’s likely trouble heating up at home,” said the farmhand.

The husband followed after, and the wife ran ahead and screamed, “Oh, don’t kill me, don’t kill me, and I’ll never let Pål Andrestuen come to me again!”

“Now those ten dollars are mine!” shouted the farmhand.
