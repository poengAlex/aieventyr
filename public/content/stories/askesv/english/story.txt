Once there was a poor man who had three sons. When he died, the two eldest were to go out into the world to try their luck; but they didn’t want the youngest with them at all.

“You!” they said. “You’re good for nothing but sitting and poking about in the ashes, you are.”

“Then I’ll have to go alone,” said Ash-Lad.

The two set off and came to the King’s manor, where they found service—one as a stable hand under the Master of the Stables, the other under the head gardener. Ash-Lad set out too, and he took with him a great wooden kneading trough, the only thing they had left from their parents, and the one the other two had never cared a bit about. It was heavy to carry, but he wouldn’t leave it behind. After he had walked for a while, he came to the King’s manor as well and asked for work. They told him they had no use for him; but he begged so earnestly and so nicely that in the end they let him stay in the kitchen, carrying wood and water for the cook-maid.

He was diligent and handy, and it wasn’t long before everyone grew fond of him. The other two, though, were lazy; they got cuffed and paid poorly, and they grew jealous of Ash-Lad when they saw how well things went for him.

Across from the King’s manor, on the other side of a lake, there lived a troll. The troll had seven silver ducks that swam out on the water, so they could be seen from the manor. The King had often wished he could have them, and so the two brothers said to the Master of the Stables, “If our brother wanted to, he’s said he can get the King those seven silver ducks.” You can be sure it wasn’t long before the Master of the Stables told the King.

The King summoned Ash-Lad and said, “Your brothers say you can get me the silver ducks. Now you’ll do it.”

“I’ve neither thought nor said any such thing,” said the boy.

But the King would have it his way. “You’ve said it, and you will,” he said.

“All right then,” said the boy. “If it can’t be helped, let me have a quarter-measure of rye and a quarter-measure of wheat, and I’ll have a try.” He got it, poured the grain into the kneading trough he had brought from home, and rowed across in it. When he reached the far shore, he walked along the water’s edge and scattered grain and scattered grain, until at last he lured the ducks into the trough. Then he rowed back as fast as he could.

When he was halfway across, the troll came out and caught sight of him.

“Have you run off with my seven silver ducks?” it roared.

“Yes,” said the boy.

“Will you be coming again?” asked the troll.

“Could be,” said the boy.

When Ash-Lad brought the seven silver ducks to the King, everyone liked him even better than before, and the King himself said it was well done. But his brothers grew all the more bitter and envious, and they came up with something else. They told the Master of the Stables that Ash-Lad had said he could also get the King the troll’s bedspread—patched with a silver square and a gold square and a silver square and a gold square—any time he pleased. The Master of the Stables was no slower that time in telling the King.

So the King said to the boy that his brothers had told him he’d boasted he could fetch the troll’s bedspread with its silver and gold patches—and now he must do it, or he would lose his life. Ash-Lad answered that he had neither thought nor said any such thing, but it did no good. He asked for three days to consider what to do. When the days were up, he rowed across again in the kneading trough and prowled back and forth, watching and waiting.

At last he saw them inside the mountain hang the bedspread out to air. And when they had gone well back into the mountain again, Ash-Lad snatched it down and rowed back as fast as he could.

When he was halfway across, the troll came out and saw him.

“Was it you who took my seven silver ducks?” the troll shouted.

“Yes,” said the boy.

“And have you taken my bedspread too—with a silver square and a gold square and a silver square and a gold square?”

“Yes,” said the boy.

“Will you be coming again?”

“Could be,” said the boy.

When he returned with the bedspread of gold and silver patches, everyone liked him better than ever, and he became servant to the King himself. That made the two brothers even more furious, and to spite him they told the Master of the Stables, “Now our brother has said he can get the King the troll’s gold harp—the one that makes everyone glad as soon as they hear it, however sorrowful they may be.”

The Master of the Stables ran straight to the King again, and the King said to the boy, “If you’ve said it, then you’ll do it. If you can, you shall have the princess and half the kingdom; but if you cannot, you shall lose your life.”

“I’ve neither thought nor said it,” Ash-Lad replied. “But there’s no other way for it—I’ll have to try. Only I want six days to prepare.” That he was granted; but when the days were up, he had to be on his way. He put a nail, a little birch stick, and a stub of candle in his pocket, rowed across, and walked back and forth outside, spying and peering.

After a while the troll came out and saw him.

“Was it you who took my seven silver ducks?” it roared.

“Yes,” answered the boy.

“And you took my bedspread with the silver and gold patches too?” asked the troll.

“Yes,” said the boy.

Then the troll seized him and hauled him into the mountain. “Now, my daughter,” said the troll, “I’ve got hold of the one who stole my silver ducks and my bedspread with its silver and gold patches. Put him on the fattening-stall now, and we’ll slaughter him and invite our friends to a feast.”

She was willing enough, and she set him on the fattening-stall, and there he stood for eight days, getting the best food and drink he could wish for, as much as he wanted.

When the eight days were up, the troll told his daughter to go down and slice the boy’s little finger so they could see whether he was fat. She went down to the fattening-stall.

“Hold out your little finger!” she said. But Ash-Lad held out the nail instead, and she cut into it.

“Oh no,” said the troll’s daughter when she went back in to her father. “He’s hard as iron still. We can’t take him yet.”

After another eight days it went the same, only this time Ash-Lad held out the birch stick.

“He’s a little better,” she said when she came back to the troll, “but he’s still hard to chew, like wood.”

But after eight days more, the troll again told his daughter to go down and see whether he was fat yet. “Hold out your little finger!” said the troll’s daughter when she came to the fattening-stall. That time Ash-Lad held out the stub of candle.

“Now he’ll do,” she said.

“Well then,” said the troll. “Then I’ll go off and invite the guests. While I’m away, you’ll slaughter him—and roast half and boil half.”

When the troll had gone, the daughter set to sharpening a great long knife.

“Is that what you’re going to slaughter me with?” the boy asked.

“Yes, you are,” said the troll’s daughter.

“But it isn’t sharp,” said the boy. “Let me sharpen it for you, and you’ll take my life more easily.”

So she let him have the knife, and he began to grind and hone it.

“Let me try it on your braid,” said the boy. “I think it’s good now.” She gave him leave, and the moment he grabbed her braid he jerked her head back and cut it clean off. Then he boiled half of her and roasted half of her and set it on the table. After that he put on her clothes and sat down over in the corner.

When the troll came home with the feast-guests, he called to his daughter—thinking it was she—that she should come and eat too.

“No,” answered the boy. “I don’t want food. I’m so sullen and sick of myself.”

“Oh, you know a cure for that,” said the troll. “Take the gold harp and play!”

“Yes, and where is it then?” asked Ash-Lad.

“You know that well enough—you used it last. It’s hanging there over the door!”

Ash-Lad needed no second telling. He snatched it up and went in and out playing; but all at once he shoved the kneading trough out and rowed off so hard that the water foamed around it.

After a while the troll thought his daughter had been gone too long, and went after her to see what was wrong. Then he saw the boy in the trough, far, far out on the lake.

“Was it you who took my seven silver ducks?” the troll roared.

“Yes,” said Ash-Lad.

“And it was you who took my bedspread with a silver square and a gold square too?”

“Yes,” said Ash-Lad.

“And have you taken my gold harp as well?” the troll screamed.

“Yes, I have,” said the boy.

“Haven’t I eaten you after all?”

“No,” said Ash-Lad. “It was your daughter you ate.”

When the troll heard that, it grew so furious it burst. Then Ash-Lad rowed back and carried off a whole heap of gold and silver as well—everything the trough could bear. And when he came to the King’s manor with the gold harp, he got the King’s daughter and half the kingdom, just as the King had promised him. And he treated his brothers well, for he believed they had only meant the best for him by what they had said.
