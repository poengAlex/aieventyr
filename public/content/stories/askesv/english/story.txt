Once upon a time there was a poor man who had three sons. When he died, the two eldest set off into the world to seek their fortune, and they had no intention of taking the youngest along.

“You?” they said. “You’re good for nothing but sitting by the hearth and poking in the ashes.”

“Then I’ll go on my own,” said Ash-Lad.

The two eldest came to the King’s manor and were taken on there, one under the Master of the Stables and the other under the head gardener. Ash-Lad came trudging after them with a great wooden kneading trough on his back. It was the only thing their parents had left, and his brothers hadn’t wanted it. It was heavy enough to bend him double, but he would not leave it behind.

At the manor they told him they had no use for another boy. But he asked so nicely, and kept on asking, that in the end they let him work in the kitchen, fetching wood and water for the cook. He was quick and willing, and before long everyone was fond of him. His brothers, meanwhile, were lazy. They got boxed ears and poor wages, and when they saw how well their little brother was doing, they turned green with envy.

Straight across from the manor, on the far side of a lake, lived a troll. The troll had seven ducks made of solid silver, and they bobbed about on the water and flashed in the sun, so that you could see them from every window of the manor. The King looked at them every single day. He wanted those ducks more than anything.

So the brothers went to the Master of the Stables.

“Our little brother,” they said, “claims he could fetch the King those seven silver ducks, if he could be bothered.”

You may be sure it wasn’t long before the Master of the Stables told the King, and the King sent for Ash-Lad.

“Your brothers tell me you can fetch me the silver ducks,” said the King. “So now you will.”

“I never thought it and I never said it,” said Ash-Lad.

“You said it, and you’ll do it,” said the King, and that was that.

“Well,” said Ash-Lad, “if there’s no way around it, give me a sack of rye and a sack of wheat, and I’ll see what I can do.”

He got his grain, tipped it into the kneading trough and rowed across the lake in it. On the far shore he walked along the water’s edge scattering grain, scattering and scattering, until the silver ducks came paddling after it one by one and climbed right into the trough to eat. Then he pushed off and rowed back as hard as he could.

When he was halfway across, the troll came out of the mountain and saw him.

“Have you gone off with my seven silver ducks?” he bellowed.

“Ye-es,” said Ash-Lad.

“Will you be coming back?”

“Could be,” said Ash-Lad.

When he came home with the seven silver ducks, everyone at the manor liked him better than ever, and the King himself said it was well done. But his brothers grew sourer still. Now they told the Master of the Stables that Ash-Lad had boasted he could fetch the troll’s bedspread too, the one checked in silver and gold: a silver square and a gold square and a silver square and a gold square.

And the Master of the Stables was no slower to pass it on this time.

“Your brothers say you can fetch me the troll’s bedspread with the silver and gold squares,” said the King. “Do it, or lose your life.”

“I never thought it and I never said it,” said Ash-Lad.

It did him no good at all. So he asked for three days to think it over.

When the three days were up, he rowed across in the trough again and crept back and forth below the mountain, watching and waiting. At last he saw the trolls hang the bedspread out of a crack in the rock to air, flashing silver and gold against the grey stone. He waited until they had gone well back inside. Then he snatched it down, flung it into the trough and rowed for home as fast as the trough would go.

When he was halfway across, the troll came out and saw him.

“Was it you who took my seven silver ducks?” roared the troll.

“Ye-es,” said Ash-Lad.

“And have you taken my bedspread too, with a silver square and a gold square and a silver square and a gold square?”

“Ye-es,” said Ash-Lad.

“Will you be coming back again?”

“Could be,” said Ash-Lad.

When he came home with the bedspread, everyone liked him even better, and he was made the King’s own servant. That made his brothers so furious they wanted revenge.

“Now our brother says he can fetch the troll’s golden harp,” they told the Master of the Stables. “The one that makes anybody who hears it happy, however miserable they were before.”

The Master of the Stables went straight to the King.

“If you said it, you’ll do it,” the King told Ash-Lad. “Bring me the harp, and you shall have the princess and half the kingdom. Fail, and you lose your life.”

“I never thought it and I never said it,” said Ash-Lad. “But I suppose there’s nothing for it but to try. I want six days to think it over, though.”

He got his six days, and when they were up, off he had to go. He put a nail, a birch twig and a candle stub in his pocket, rowed across, and strolled up and down outside the mountain as if he had nowhere better to be. Before long the troll came out and saw him.

“Was it you who took my seven silver ducks?” roared the troll.

“Ye-es,” said Ash-Lad.

“And you who took my bedspread with the silver and gold squares?”

“Ye-es,” said Ash-Lad.

Then the troll grabbed him and dragged him into the mountain.

“Daughter!” he said. “I’ve caught the one who took my silver ducks and my bedspread. Put him in the fattening pen. We’ll butcher him and invite all our friends.”

The troll’s daughter was only too happy to oblige. She shut him in the pen, and there he stayed for eight days, eating and drinking the best of everything, as much as he wanted.

When the eight days were up, the troll told his daughter to go down and cut Ash-Lad’s little finger, to see if he was fat yet.

“Give me your little finger!” said the troll’s daughter. But Ash-Lad held out the nail, and that was what she cut.

“Oh, no,” she told her father. “He’s still as hard as iron. We can’t have him yet.”

Eight days later it went the same way, except that this time Ash-Lad held out the birch twig.

“He’s a little better,” she said. “But he’s still as tough to chew as wood.”

After eight days more, down she went again.

“Give me your little finger!” This time Ash-Lad held out the candle stub.

“Now he’ll do,” she said.

“Good!” said the troll. “Then I’ll go and invite the guests. While I’m gone, you butcher him. Roast half and boil half.”

As soon as the troll had gone, his daughter began to sharpen a great long knife.

“Is that what you’re going to butcher me with?” asked Ash-Lad.

“It is,” said the troll’s daughter.

“But it isn’t sharp,” said Ash-Lad. “Let me hone it for you. Then it’ll be much easier to finish me off.”

So she handed him the knife, and he honed it and whetted it.

“Let me try it on your braid,” he said. “I think it’s ready now.”

She let him. But the moment he had hold of the braid, he jerked her head back and cut it off. Then he boiled one half of the troll’s daughter and roasted the other, and set it all out on the table. After that he put on her clothes and sat down in the corner.

When the troll came home with his guests, he called over to the corner, where of course he thought his daughter was sitting.

“Come and eat with us!”

“No,” said Ash-Lad. “I don’t want anything. I feel so gloomy and low.”

“Well, you know the cure for that,” said the troll. “Take the golden harp and play!”

“Yes, but where is it?” asked Ash-Lad.

“You know perfectly well where it is. You were the last one to use it. It’s hanging over the door!”

Ash-Lad didn’t need telling twice. He took down the harp and went in and out of the door, playing. Then, all at once, he pushed the trough out onto the lake and rowed off so hard the water foamed around it.

After a while the troll thought his daughter had been gone rather long, and went out to see what was the matter with her. And there was Ash-Lad in the trough, far, far out on the water.

“Was it you who took my seven silver ducks?” screamed the troll.

“Yes,” said Ash-Lad.

“And you who took my bedspread with the silver and gold squares?”

“Yes,” said Ash-Lad.

“Have you taken my golden harp as well?”

“Yes, I have,” said Ash-Lad.

“But didn’t I eat you up after all?”

“No, that was your daughter you ate,” said Ash-Lad.

When the troll heard that, he flew into such a rage that he burst. Then Ash-Lad rowed back and loaded the trough with as much gold and silver as it could carry. When he came to the manor with the golden harp, he got the princess and half the kingdom, just as the King had promised. And he was good to his brothers, for he believed they had only wanted the best for him in everything they had said.
