Once upon a time there was a poor man who lived in a little cottage far away in the forest, and he made his living by hunting.

He had only one daughter, and she was both pretty and gentle. Her mother had died early, and now that the girl was nearly grown she said she wanted to go out among other folk so she too could learn to earn her daily bread. “Yes, my daughter,” said her father, “to be sure you’ve learned nothing from me but how to pluck birds and roast them, but you’ll have to try to work for your bread all the same.”

So the girl set off to look for service, and after she had walked awhile she came to the king’s manor. There she was taken in, and the queen liked her so well that the other maids grew downright jealous of her. They hit on the idea of telling the queen that the girl had boasted she could spin a pound of flax in twenty-four hours; for the queen set great store by every kind of handwork. “Well then—if you have said it, you shall do it,” said the queen to her; “though you may have a little longer than that, if need be.”

The poor girl didn’t dare to confess that she had never spun in her life, but only begged for a room to herself. She got one, and up to it they carried a spinning wheel and flax. There she sat weeping, wretched and at her wit’s end; she fussed with the wheel and turned it this way and that and didn’t know how to set about it—she had never even seen a spinning wheel before.

But as she sat there, an old woman came in.

“What’s the matter with you, my child?” she asked.

“Oh,” said the girl, “there’s no use telling you; you can’t help me anyway!”

“No one knows that,” said the old woman. “Maybe I know a remedy for it after all.”

Well, I might as well tell her, thought the girl, and she told her how her fellow servants had spread it about that she had claimed she could spin a pound of flax in twenty-four hours. “And I, poor wretch,” she said, “I’ve never in my days seen a spinning wheel, much less could I spin that much in a day and a night.”

“That’s neither here nor there, child,” said the old woman. “If you will call me Aunt on your day of honor, I’ll spin for you. You can go and lie down and sleep.”

The girl was only too glad to do that, and she went and lay down.

In the morning when she woke, all the flax lay spun on the table, so neat and fine that no one had ever seen such even, lovely thread. The queen was greatly pleased with the beautiful yarn she had gotten, and she liked the girl even better than before. But that only made the others more jealous, and they went and told the queen that now the girl had said she could weave the yarn she had spun in twenty-four hours. Again the queen said that if she had said it, she must do it; though if it wasn’t exactly within twenty-four hours, she might have a little longer day. The girl didn’t dare to say no this time either, but asked for a room to herself, so she could at least try.

There she sat again, crying and in misery, not knowing what to do; and in came an old woman again and asked, “What’s the matter, my child?”

At first the girl didn’t want to say, but at last she told her what she was so sorrowful about.

“Well,” said the old woman, “it’s all the same. If you will call me Aunt on your day of honor, I’ll weave for you. You can go and lie down and sleep.”

The girl was glad to, and she went and lay down.

When she woke, the bolt of cloth lay on the table, woven as smooth and tight as any cloth could be. She took it and carried it down to the queen, who was overjoyed at the fine weaving she had received and loved the girl more than ever. But the others grew still more envious and thought of nothing but what else they might contrive.

At last they told the queen that now the girl had said she could sew up the woven cloth into shirts in twenty-four hours. It went just as before: the girl didn’t dare to say she couldn’t sew; she was sent up to a room by herself again, and she sat there weeping and grieving. Then once more an old woman came to her and promised to sew for her if the girl would call her Aunt on her day of honor. The girl promised that more than gladly, and then did as the old woman told her and went and lay down to sleep. In the morning when she woke, she found the bolt of cloth sewn into shirts, lying on the table. Never had anyone seen such fine stitching, and the shirts had buttons and were completely finished.

When the queen saw the work, she was so delighted with the sewing that she clapped her hands. “Such sewing I have never had nor seen,” she said, and after that she loved the girl as though she were her own child.

“If you want the prince, you shall have him,” she said to the girl, “for you need never hire anything done; you can sew and spin and weave it all yourself.” Since the girl was pretty, and the prince took a liking to her, they had a wedding at once. But just as the prince had sat down with her at the bridal table, an old, hideous hag came in with a long nose—surely it was three ells long.

The bride rose at once, curtsied, and said, “Good day, Aunt!”

“Is that Aunt to my bride?” asked the prince.

Yes, she was.

“Then she must sit down at the table,” said the prince; but both he and the rest thought it dreadful to have such a creature at table with them.

And before they had got over that, in came another old, ugly hag. She had a backside so thick and broad that she could scarcely squeeze through the door. The bride stood up immediately and greeted her: “Good day, Aunt!” and the prince asked again whether this was Aunt to his bride. Both answered yes, and the prince said that if so, she too must sit down at the table.

But she had hardly seated herself before yet another old, ugly hag came in, with eyes as big as plates, so red and running that it was a fright to look at. The bride rose again and said, “Good day, Aunt!” and the prince asked her to sit down at the table as well; but he was not pleased, and he thought to himself, “God help me—what aunts my bride has!”

After he had sat awhile, he could hold his tongue no longer and asked, “But how in all the world can my bride, who is so lovely, have such dreadful, misshapen aunts?”

“I’ll tell you that,” said one of them. “I was as pretty as your bride when I was her age; but the reason I’ve got such a long nose is that I have always sat and jerked and bobbed over spinning, and so my nose has stretched until it became as long as you see.”

“And I,” said the second, “ever since I was young I have sat pushing the loom back and forth, and by that my backside has grown as huge and swollen as you see.”

Then the third said, “From the time I was quite small I have sat staring and sewing night and day; and so my eyes have become so ugly and red, and now there is no remedy for them.”

“Well then,” said the prince, “it’s a good thing I learned that; for if people can grow so ugly and dreadful from it, then my bride shall never spin nor weave nor sew another day in her life!”
