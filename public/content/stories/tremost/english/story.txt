Once upon a time there was a poor man who had a hut deep in the forest and lived on whatever he could shoot. He had just the one daughter. Her mother had died when the girl was small.

When she was half grown, the girl said she wanted to go out among people and learn to earn her own bread.

“Well, my girl,” said her father, “it’s true you’ve learned nothing from me but how to pluck a bird and roast it. Still, I suppose you’ll have to try.”

So off she went to find a place in service, and in the end she came to the King’s manor, where they took her on. She was pretty and cheerful, and before long the Queen was letting her hold the skeins while she wound her wool. The other maids had been waiting years for that honour, and they could not forgive her.

The Queen, you see, was mad about handiwork of every kind. She could not sit through a meal, a sermon or a thunderstorm without a bit of knitting in her lap. The maids knew it. So they went to the Queen.

“That new girl,” they said, “has been boasting that she can spin a pound of flax in twenty-four hours.”

“If you’ve said it, you shall do it,” the Queen told the girl. “But you may have a little longer.”

The poor girl didn’t dare say that she had never spun a thread in her life. She only asked for a room to herself. She got one, and up to it they carried a spinning wheel and a great heap of flax.

There she sat and cried. She turned the wheel this way and that. She pushed the treadle, and the wheel whirred round and round, but no thread came of it. The hunter’s daughter had never even seen a spinning wheel before.

As she sat there, the door opened a crack, and round it came a nose. It was a very long nose, and it arrived a good while before the old woman it belonged to. She stepped into the room, calm as you please, and looked at the girl like someone who already knew.

“What’s the matter, my child?” she said.

“Oh,” said the girl, “there’s no use telling you. You can’t help me anyway.”

“Nobody knows that,” said the old woman. “Maybe I know a way, all the same.”

Well, I might as well tell her, the girl thought, and she told her the whole story. “And I’d never laid eyes on a spinning wheel before today,” she said, “let alone spun a pound of flax on one in a day and a night.”

“Never mind that, child,” said the old woman. “If you’ll call me Aunt on your day of honour, I’ll spin for you. Then you can go and lie down and sleep.”

The girl was glad to do that, and went and lay down.

When she woke in the morning, the whole pound of flax lay on the table, spun into thread and wound in pale, shining coils, so fine and even that nobody had ever seen the like. The girl stood beside it with her hand pressed to her heart. Over by the door, the old woman was just picking up her basket. She gave the girl a nod and was gone, nose first.

The Queen was delighted with the lovely thread and liked the girl better than ever. But that only made the others more jealous.

“Now she says she can weave all that thread into cloth in twenty-four hours,” they told the Queen.

“If you’ve said it, you shall do it,” said the Queen. “But you may have a little longer.”

The girl didn’t dare say no this time either. She asked for a room to herself, so that she could at least try.

There she sat, crying and at her wits’ end, and in came an old woman again. Not the same one, the girl thought, though it was hard to tell through the tears.

“What’s the matter, my child?”

At first the girl wouldn’t say. But in the end she told her everything.

“Never mind that,” said the old woman. “If you’ll call me Aunt on your day of honour, I’ll weave for you. Then you can go and lie down and sleep.”

The girl was glad to. She went and lay down.

When she woke, the cloth lay on the table, woven as close and smooth as cloth can be woven. She carried it down to the Queen, who liked her better with every day that passed. But the others only grew more spiteful, and thought of nothing but what to come up with next.

At last they told the Queen that the girl had boasted she could sew the whole bolt into shirts in twenty-four hours.

Well, it went just the same as before. The girl didn’t dare say she couldn’t sew. She was given a room to herself, and there she sat and cried, and in came an old woman again. This one didn’t even ask what the matter was.

“If you’ll call me Aunt on your day of honour,” she said, “I’ll sew for you.”

The girl promised more than gladly, and went and lay down to sleep.

In the morning the shirts lay finished on the table. Nobody had ever seen such stitching, and the shirts even had their buttons on.

When the Queen saw the work, she was so taken with the sewing that she clapped her hands.

“Such stitching I have never had nor seen!” she said, and from that day on she loved the girl like her own child.

“If you want the Prince, you shall have him,” she said, “for you’ll never need to hire help. You can spin and weave and sew it all yourself.”

The girl said nothing to that.

She was pretty, and the Prince had taken a liking to her, so the wedding was held at once. But the Prince had barely sat down beside his bride at the feast when in came a hideous old woman with a long nose. It must have been six feet long if it was an inch.

The bride rose, sank into a deep curtsy and said, “Good day, Aunt!”

All round the table the guests shrank back and whispered behind their napkins.

“Is that my bride’s aunt?” said the Prince.

Indeed she was.

“Well then, she’d better sit down with us,” said the Prince, who had been well brought up. But he and everyone else thought she was a dreadful sight to have at the table.

Before anyone had got over it, in came another ugly old woman, with a backside so broad that she only just squeezed through the door. At once the bride stood up and said, “Good day, Aunt!”

“Is that my bride’s aunt?” asked the Prince.

“Yes,” they both said.

“Then she’d better sit down too,” said the Prince. She wedged herself onto the bench, and the whole bench had to shuffle along.

But she had hardly sat down when in came a third ugly old woman, with eyes as big as dinner plates, so red and runny that it was dreadful to see. The bride rose again and said, “Good day, Aunt!” The Prince asked her to sit down as well, but he was not a happy man. God help me, he thought, what aunts my bride has!

He sat for a while and held his tongue. Then he couldn’t keep it in any longer, and he leaned forward.

“But how in all the world,” he said, “can my bride, who is so lovely, have such dreadful, misshapen aunts?”

The bride sat very still, with her hands clasped in her lap.

“I’ll tell you,” said the first aunt, and tapped her nose. “I was every bit as pretty as your bride when I was her age. But I’ve spent my whole life nodding and pecking over the spinning wheel, and my nose has stretched and stretched until it’s as long as you see.”

“And I,” said the second, patting her hips, “ever since I was young, I’ve sat on the weaving bench shoving back and forth, back and forth, and that’s how my backside got as big and swollen as you see.”

“From the time I was a little girl,” said the third, dabbing at her eyes, “I’ve sat staring and sewing, night and day. That’s what made my eyes so red and ugly, and now there’s nothing to be done for them.”

“Is that so?” said the Prince. “Well, it’s a good thing I found out. If people can get so ugly and dreadful from it, then my bride shall never spin nor weave nor sew another day in her life!”

The bride bore it bravely.
