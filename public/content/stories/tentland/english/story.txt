Once there was a fisherman who lived close by the palace and fished for the king’s table. One day when he was out fishing he caught nothing; he did all he could—baited and fished, and fished and baited—but not so much as a fishbone hung on his hook. But late in the day a head rose out of the water and said, “If I get what your wife carries under her belt, you shall have fish enough.”

The man said yes at once, for he did not know she was with child. After that he got fish every day, and plenty of it—more than he wanted.

But when he came home that evening and told how he had got all the fish, his wife began to weep and lament, and prayed God to comfort her for the promise her husband had made; for she carried a child under her belt, she said. It was soon heard up at the palace that the fisherman's wife was so grieved, and when the king heard of it he promised he would take the child to himself and see that it was saved.

Time passed, and when her days were fulfilled the wife bore a baby boy. The king took the child and raised him as his own son until the boy was grown.

One day the boy begged that he might be allowed to go with his father out fishing; he longed for it with all his heart, he said. The king was loath to grant it, but at last the boy got leave. He went with his father, and all went well the whole day, until they came to land in the evening. Then the son had forgotten his handkerchief behind; he wanted to jump out to the boat to fetch it. But the moment he stepped into it the boat began to run off with him so the water foamed around it, and though the boy pulled against it with the oars with all his might, it did no good. On and on it went all night, and at last he came far, far away to a white shore.

He went ashore, and after he had walked a little way he met an old man with a long white beard.

“What is this place called?” the boy asked.

“Whiteland,” the man answered, and then he begged the boy to tell him where he came from and what he wanted, and the boy told him everything.

“Yes,” said the old man. “Now if you go on along this shore you will come to three king’s daughters who are set down in the earth, so that only their heads are above ground. The first—the eldest—will call to you and beg you ever so prettily to come to her and help her, and the second will do the same; but you must not go to either of them. Hurry past them as though you had never heard or seen them. But to the third you must go, and do what she asks you; that will be your good fortune.”

When the boy came to the first princess she called to him and begged him so earnestly and sweetly that he would come to her; but he walked on as though he did not see her. Just the same he went past the second. But to the third he went.

“If you will do what I tell you, you shall have whichever of us three you wish,” said the princess.

Yes, he would gladly do that. And then she told him that three trolls had set all three of them down in the earth there; before that they had lived in the castle he could see out in the forest. “Now you must go into the castle and let the trolls whip you one night for each of us,” she said. “If you can bear it, you will free us.”

Yes, the boy said, he would try.

“When you go in,” said the princess again, “there are two lions standing in the gate; but if you just walk straight between them, they will do you no harm. Then go straight on into a small dark room; there you must lie down. Then the troll will come and beat you; but you must take the bottle that hangs on the wall and rub yourself where he struck you, and you will be whole again. Then seize the sword that hangs beside the bottle and hew the troll to pieces.”

Yes, he did as the princess said. He walked right between the lions as though he did not see them, and straight into the little chamber, and there he lay down.

The first night a troll came with three heads and three birch rods and whipped the boy cruelly; but he held out until the troll was done. Then he took the bottle and anointed himself, and then he seized the sword and hewed the troll to pieces. When he came out in the morning, the princesses stood above the earth up to their waists.

The second night it went the same way; but the troll that came then had six heads and six rods, and it whipped him even worse than the first. Yet when he came out in the morning, the princesses stood above the earth up to their armpits.

The third night a troll came with nine heads and nine rods, and it struck and whipped the boy so long that at last he fainted. Then the troll took him and hurled him against the wall, and by that the jar fell down so that the ointment splashed all over him, and at once he was sound again. Then he was not slow; he seized the sword and hewed the troll to pieces. And when he came out of the castle that morning, the princesses stood wholly above ground.

Then he took the youngest of them for his queen, and lived well and happily with her for a long time.

But at last he grew eager to travel home for a while and see his parents. His queen did not like it much; but since he longed so and at last would and must go, she said to him, “One thing you must promise me: do what your father tells you, but not what your mother tells you.” And he promised.

Then she gave him a ring such that whoever wore it could wish for two things, whatever he pleased. He wished himself home, and his parents could not marvel enough at how splendid and fine he had become.

When he had been home a few days, his mother wanted him to go up to the palace and show the king what a man he had become. His father said, “No, he ought not to do that, for then we shall have no joy of him from that hour.” But it did no good; his mother begged and pleaded so long that he went.

When he came up there he was finer in clothes and in everything than the other king himself. The king did not like that at all, and he said, “Well, now you can see what my queen is like; I can’t get to see yours. I don’t believe you have so lovely a queen.”

“If only she were standing here, you should see it!” said the young king, and at once there she stood.

But she was deeply distressed and said to him, “Why did you not obey me and listen to what your father told you? Now I must go home again at once, and you have used both your wishes.” With that she tied a ring into his hair, with her name on it, and then wished herself home.

Then the young king was overwhelmed with sorrow, and day after day he walked about thinking only of how he might get back to his queen. “I must see if somewhere I can find out where Whiteland lies,” he thought, and set out into the world.

After he had traveled awhile, he came to a mountain, and there he met one who was lord over all the beasts of the forest—for they came whenever he blew a horn he carried. The king asked him about Whiteland.

“Yes, I don’t know,” the man answered, “but I will ask my beasts.” So he blew them in and asked whether any of them knew where Whiteland lay; but there was not one that knew.

Then the man gave him a pair of skis. “When you stand on these,” he said, “you will come to my brother who lives a hundred miles from here; he is lord over all the birds in the air. Ask him. When you have come there, you need only turn the skis so the tips point this way, and they will go home of themselves.”

When the king came there, he turned the skis as the lord of the beasts had said, and then they went back.

He asked again about Whiteland, and the man blew in all the birds and asked whether any of them knew where Whiteland lay. No, none of them knew. Long after the others came an old eagle too; she had been away a full ten years, but she did not know either.

“Well, well,” said the man. “Then you shall borrow a pair of skis from me. When you stand on them you will come to my brother who lives a hundred miles from here; he is lord over all the fish in the sea. You must ask him. But don’t forget to turn the skis!”

The king thanked him, stepped onto the skis, and when he reached the lord over the fish of the sea, he turned them, and they went back just like the others.

Then he asked about Whiteland again. The man blew in the fish, but none of them knew anything. At last there came an old, old pike, and he had great trouble blowing her in. When he asked her, she said, “Yes, I know it well enough; for now I have been cook there for ten years. Tomorrow I am going there again, for then the queen—the one the king disappeared from—is to have a wedding with another.”

“If that is so, I will give you a piece of advice,” said the lord of the fish. “Over there on a bog stand three brothers who have stood there a hundred years fighting over a hat, a cloak, and a pair of boots; when a man has those three things, he can make himself invisible and wish himself as far as he likes. You can tell them you want to try the things and then give judgment between them.”

The king thanked him, went, and did so. “What is it you stand here fighting forever and ever about?” he said to the brothers. “Let me try the things, and I will judge between you.” That they were happy to do. But when he had got the hat, the cloak, and the boots, he said, “When we meet next time you shall hear the judgment,” and with that he wished himself away.

As he flew through the air, he came alongside the North Wind.

“Where are you going?” said the North Wind.

“To Whiteland,” said the king, and told him what had happened.

“Yes,” said the North Wind, “you travel rather faster than I do; I must go into every nook and gust and blow. But when you come there, stand on the steps beside the door, and I will come rushing as though I meant to blow the whole castle down. When the prince who is to have your queen comes out to see what is going on, you must seize him by the nape of the neck and throw him out; after that I will see if I can get him off and away.”

Yes, the king did as the North Wind had said. He stood on the steps, and when the North Wind came roaring and crashing and gave the castle wall such a tug that it shook, the prince came out to see what was the matter. But the moment he came, the king grabbed him by the neck and flung him out, and then the North Wind took him and carried him away.

When he was rid of him, the king went into the castle. At first the queen did not know him, because he had grown so thin and pale from wandering so long and being so sorrowful; but when he showed her the ring, she was glad with all her heart. Then the true wedding was held, and it was spoken of far and wide.
