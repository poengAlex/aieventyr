Once upon a time there was a king who had twelve sons. When they were grown, he told them to go out into the world and find themselves wives. But each wife had to be able to spin and weave and sew a shirt in a single day, or he wouldn’t have her for a daughter-in-law.

He gave each of his sons a horse and a new suit of armour, and off they rode. But they hadn’t gone far before the eleven eldest turned round in their saddles.

“We’re not taking you,” they told the youngest. “You’re good for nothing.”

And they rode on without Ash-Lad.

So Ash-Lad had to stay behind, and there was no help for it. He didn’t know what to do or which way to turn, and he grew so miserable that he climbed off his horse, sat down in the long grass by the road and cried.

He hadn’t been sitting there long when one of the tussocks in front of him began to stir. The grass parted like a curtain, and out came a little white thing. When it came closer, Ash-Lad saw that it was a girl, and a lovely one, but tiny. She was no taller than his hand.

“Won’t you come down and visit the Doll in the Grass?” she asked, and beckoned to him.

Yes, he would. And so he did.

Down under the tussock she sat herself on a little chair, smoothed her dress and looked up at him. She was the Doll in the Grass herself, you see, and a lady doesn’t receive her guests at the side of the road.

“Where are you going,” she asked, “and what is your errand?”

Ash-Lad told her about the King and the twelve brothers and the shirt that had to be sewn in a day.

“But if you’ll do it, and if you’ll be my wife,” he said, “I won’t go a step further.”

She was more than willing. She spun and she wove and she sewed, and before the day was out, the shirt was finished. Only it had come out so dreadfully small. It was no longer than—well, about this long.

Ash-Lad rode home with it, but when he took it out to show the King, he was ashamed of it, it was so small. The King held it up to the light for a long while. Then he said Ash-Lad should have her all the same, and Ash-Lad rode back, glad and happy, to fetch his little sweetheart.

When he got there, he wanted to lift her up in front of him on his horse. But no, she wouldn’t hear of it. She meant to ride in a silver spoon, she said, and she had two little white horses of her own to pull it.

So they set off, he on his horse and she in her silver spoon, and her two little white horses were two little white mice. Ash-Lad kept to the far side of the road the whole way. He was terrified of riding over her, she was so tiny.

When they had gone a good way, they came to a great lake. There Ash-Lad’s horse took fright. It shied and sprang sideways across the road, straight into the spoon, and over it went. The mice scattered along the bank, and the Doll in the Grass tumbled out and fell into the water.

Ash-Lad was beside himself. The lake was wide and dark, and he couldn’t think how he would ever get her back.

But after a little while the water began to bubble, and up came a merman with long green hair and a tail like a salmon’s. He held the Doll in the Grass high above his head, and now she was as tall as any grown woman, and far lovelier than before. The merman handed her to Ash-Lad on the bank. Then he sank back down, and he was gone.

Ash-Lad set her in front of him on his horse and rode home.

All his brothers had come home too, each with a sweetheart. But the sweethearts were so ugly and nasty, and so bad-tempered, that they had been pulling the brothers’ hair all the way home. Every one of them wore a hat daubed with tar and soot, and the rain had made it run down their faces, so they were uglier and nastier than ever.

When the brothers saw Ash-Lad’s sweetheart, all eleven of them were eaten up with jealousy. But the King was so taken with the two of them that he sent all the others packing. Then Ash-Lad held his wedding with the Doll in the Grass, and after that they lived well and happily for a long, long time. And if they aren’t dead, they’re living still.
