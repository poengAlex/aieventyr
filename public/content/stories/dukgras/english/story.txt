Once there was a king who had twelve sons. When they were grown, he said to them that they must travel out into the world and find themselves wives; but each wife must be able to spin and weave and sew a shirt all in one day, or else he would not have her as a daughter-in-law. To each son he gave a horse and a brand-new suit of armor, and off they rode into the world to look for brides. But when they had gone a little way, they said they would not have Ash-Lad with them, for he was good for nothing.

So Ash-Lad had to stay behind; there was no help for it. He didn’t know what to do or where to turn, and he grew so miserable that he climbed down from his horse and sat in the grass and wept. But after he had sat a little while, one of the grassy tussocks began to stir, and out of it came a tiny white thing; and when it came nearer, Ash-Lad saw it was a pretty little girl—only she was ever so small. She went up to him and asked if he wouldn’t come down and see the Doll in the Grass. Yes, he would, he said, and so he did.

When he had come down there, the Doll in the Grass was sitting on a chair. She was so lovely and so finely dressed, and she asked Ash-Lad where he was going and what errand he was traveling on.

He told her they were twelve brothers, and that the king had given them horses and armor and told them to ride out into the world to find wives, and each wife must be able to spin and weave and sew a shirt in one day. “But if you will do that, and you will be my wife, then I won’t travel any farther,” said Ash-Lad to the Doll in the Grass. Yes, she would gladly, and she hurried to spin and weave and sew the shirt—but it turned out terribly small, no longer than so—.

With that Ash-Lad rode home; but when he came with it, he felt shy and ashamed because it was so tiny. Still, the king said he should have her, and then Ash-Lad rode back, glad and content, to fetch his little sweetheart. When he came to the Doll in the Grass, he wanted to lift her up onto the horse with him; but no, she would not. She said she would sit and ride in a silver spoon, and she had two little white horses of her own that would pull her.

So they set off—he on his horse and she in the silver spoon—and the two horses that pulled her were two little white mice. But Ash-Lad always kept to the other side of the road, for he was so afraid he might ride into her, she was that tiny. When they had gone a bit, they came to a great stretch of water. Then Ash-Lad’s horse shied, sprang across to the other side of the road, and tipped the spoon over, so that the Doll in the Grass fell into the water.

Ash-Lad was beside himself; he did not know how he could ever get her up again. But in a little while a merman came up with her, and now she had grown to be as tall as any full-grown person, and much lovelier than before. So Ash-Lad set her in front of him on the horse and rode home.

When Ash-Lad arrived, all his brothers had come too, each with his own sweetheart; but they were so ugly and horrible, and so wicked besides, that they had been clawing at their sweethearts’ hair along the way. On their heads they wore hats smeared with tar and soot, and it had run down from the hats over their faces, so they had become even uglier and more dreadful.

When the brothers saw Ash-Lad’s sweetheart, they all grew jealous of him. But the king was so delighted with the two of them that he drove all the others away. Then Ash-Lad held his wedding with the Doll in the Grass, and afterward they lived well and happily for a long, long time—and if they are not dead, why, then they are living still.
