Once upon a time there were two poor folk who had nothing in the world but three sons. What the two eldest were called I couldn’t tell you, but the youngest was called Per.

When the parents died, the sons were to share what was left, and that was a pot, a griddle for baking flatbread, and a cat.

The eldest, who was to have the best, took the pot.

“When I lend it out, they’ll always let me scrape it,” he said.

The second took the griddle.

“When I lend it out, they’ll always give me a taste of the baking,” he said.

The youngest didn’t have much to choose from. If he was to have anything, it would have to be the cat.

“If I lend out the cat, I’ll get nothing for her,” said Per. “If anyone gives her a drop of milk, she’ll want it for herself. But I’ll take her with me all the same. It would be a shame to leave her here in the old cottage to starve.”

So the brothers went out into the world to try their luck, and each of them went his own way.

When Per had walked a while, the cat said, “You’ll be rewarded for not leaving me behind to starve. Now I’m going into the forest to fetch some fine beasts, and you’re to go up to the King’s manor over there and tell the King you’ve brought him a small gift. When he asks who it’s from, say it’s from Sir Per.”

Per hadn’t been waiting long before the cat came out of the forest with a reindeer. She had leapt up onto its head, and once she had settled herself between the antlers, she said, “If you don’t go straight to the King’s manor, I’ll claw your eyes out.”

The reindeer didn’t dare do otherwise.

When Per came to the manor, he led the reindeer into the kitchen, with the cat still sitting between its antlers.

“I’ve brought a small gift for the King,” he said, “if he won’t turn his nose up at it.”

The King came out into the kitchen, and when he saw the great, handsome reindeer, he was delighted.

“My dear young friend,” he said, “who is it that sends me such a splendid gift?”

“Oh, it comes from Sir Per,” said the boy.

“Sir Per, of course,” said the King. “And where was it he lived again?”

For he thought it a disgrace not to know so fine a gentleman.

But the boy wouldn’t tell him that for the world. He didn’t dare, he said, for fear of his master. That was true enough. His master was sitting between the reindeer’s antlers, washing a paw.

So the King gave Per a handful of silver and told him to send his greetings home, with many thanks for the gift.

The next day the cat went into the forest again and came back riding a stag. Per led it into the King’s kitchen and said he’d brought a small gift for the King, if he wouldn’t turn his nose up at it. The King was even more pleased with the stag than he had been with the reindeer, and again he asked who could send him such a splendid gift.

“It comes from Sir Per,” said the boy. But when the King wanted to know where Sir Per lived, he got the same answer as the day before, and this time he gave Per even more silver.

On the third day the cat came with a moose.

“I’ve brought a small gift for the King,” said Per, “if he won’t turn his nose up at it.”

The moose filled half the kitchen. The cooks backed into the pantry, the maids climbed onto the benches, and the courtiers crowded into the doorway to look. When the King caught sight of it, he was so happy he didn’t know which foot to stand on. Nobody so much as glanced at the cat sitting between the antlers. There was a moose in the kitchen.

That day the King gave Per many, many more silver pieces, a hundred at the very least. He was determined to find out where Sir Per lived, and he dug and pried and asked about this and that, but the boy said he truly didn’t dare tell, for his master had strictly forbidden it.

“Then ask Sir Per to come and visit me,” said the King.

Yes, the boy would do that, he said.

But when he came out of the manor and met the cat, Per said, “Well, you’ve got me into a fine mess. Now the King wants me to visit him, and I’ve nothing but the rags I stand in.”

“Oh, don’t worry about that,” said the cat. “In three days you’ll have horses and a carriage, and clothes so fine the gold will drip off you. Then you can visit the King. But whatever you see at the King’s, you must say you have it far finer at home. Don’t forget that.”

No, Per would remember, he said.

When the three days were up, the cat came with a carriage and horses and clothes and everything Per needed, and for herself a red hat with a feather in it and a little green cloak. It was all so splendid that nobody had ever seen the like. So Per drove off to the manor, and the cat ran alongside.

The King gave him a fine welcome. He showed him his stables.

“Not bad,” said Per. “But I have it finer at home.”

The King showed him his great hall.

“Not bad,” said Per. “But I have it finer at home.”

The King sat him down to the best he had, with silver spoons and silver ladles and silver cups.

“Not bad,” said Per. “But I have it a great deal finer at home.”

The cat sat under the table and said nothing. She was busy.

The King didn’t much care for this, but Per stuck to his story, and in the end the King grew so angry he couldn’t hold himself back.

“Now I’m coming home with you,” he said, “to see if it’s true that you have everything so much finer. But if you’re lying, God help you. That’s all I’ll say.”

“Well, now you’ve really got me into a fine mess,” Per told the cat. “The King wants to come home with me, and my home isn’t so easy to find.”

“Oh, never mind that,” said the cat. “Just drive after me, where I run ahead.”

So they set off: first Per, driving after the cat as she ran ahead, and then the King with all his people.

When they had driven a good way, they came to a great flock of handsome sheep, with wool so long it nearly swept the ground. The cat got there well ahead of the others. Far back down the road, a cloud of dust showed where the carriages were coming.

She held out a silver spoon to the shepherd boy. “If you say this flock belongs to Sir Per when the King asks you,” she said, “you shall have this spoon.”

She had brought it with her from the King’s manor.

Yes, the boy would gladly do that.

When the King came up, he leaned out of his carriage and said, “I’ve never seen such a big, handsome flock! Who owns it, my little lad?”

“That’ll be Sir Per,” said the boy.

A little farther on they came to a great herd of brindled cows, so fat they gleamed.

“If you say this herd belongs to Sir Per when the King asks you,” the cat told the girl who was minding them, “you shall have this silver ladle.”

She had brought the ladle from the King’s manor too.

“Gladly,” said the girl.

When the King came up, he was quite amazed, for he didn’t think he had ever seen such a fine herd, and he asked the girl whose brindled cattle they were.

“Oh, they’re Sir Per’s,” said the girl.

They drove on a little and came to a great drove of horses, the finest anyone could wish to see, big and glossy, six of every colour: red ones and dun ones and blue ones.

“If you say these horses belong to Sir Per when the King asks you,” the cat told the boy who was minding them, “you shall have this silver cup.”

She had brought the cup from the King’s manor too.

Yes, he would, said the boy.

When the King came up, he could hardly believe his eyes. He had never seen horses to match them, he said, and he asked who owned the red and dun and blue horses.

“That’ll be Sir Per,” said the boy.

When they had driven a good long way farther, they came to a castle. First there was a gate of brass, then a gate of silver, then a gate of gold. The castle itself was made of silver, and it was so bright it hurt to look at, for the sun was shining on it just as they arrived.

This, the cat told Per, was where he should say he lived.

“Welcome to my home,” said Per, with a sweep of his arm. He said it the way other people say good morning.

The King stared up at the towers and said nothing at all.

Inside, the castle was grander still. Everything was made of gold: the chairs and the tables and the benches. The King walked round and looked at it all, high and low, and the more he looked, the smaller he felt.

“Yes, Sir Per has it finer than I do,” he said at last. “There’s no use denying it.”

Then he wanted to go home. But Per asked him to stay for supper, and so the King stayed, though he was sour and sulky the whole time.

While they sat at the table, the troll who owned the castle came home and hammered on the gate.

“Who’s that in there eating my food and swilling my mead like swine?” roared the troll.

The moment the cat heard him, she ran out to the gate.

“Wait a little,” she said, “and I’ll tell you how the farmer goes about his winter rye.”

“First the farmer ploughs his field,” she said. “Then he spreads the muck. Then he ploughs it again.”

The troll stood still.

“Then he harrows it,” said the cat, “and then he sows it, and then he harrows it again so the crows won’t get the seed.”

And on she went, at great length, all night long. She told him how the rye sprouts in the autumn, and how the snow comes and lies over it, and how it lies under the snow all winter, waiting. The troll stood there and waited with it.

Then the sun came up.

“Look round,” said the cat, “and you’ll see the loveliest maiden standing right behind you!”

So the troll turned round, and saw the sun, and burst.

The cat put her tail in the air and walked back in through the gate.

“Now all this is yours,” she said to Sir Per. “And now you must cut off my head. That’s all I ask of you for what I’ve done for you.”

“No,” said Sir Per. “I won’t.”

“You will,” said the cat, “or I’ll claw your eyes out.”

So Sir Per had to do it, much as he hated to. He cut off the cat’s head. But in that very moment she turned into the loveliest princess anyone ever laid eyes on, and Sir Per was very glad he still had both of his.

“Yes, all this splendour was mine once,” said the princess, “but that troll put a spell on me, and I had to live as a cat with your parents. Now you may do as you please, and take me for your queen or not. For now you are king of the whole realm.”

Well, it may just be that Sir Per wanted her for his queen. So there was a wedding, and a feast that lasted eight days, and after that I wasn’t with Sir Per and his queen any more.
