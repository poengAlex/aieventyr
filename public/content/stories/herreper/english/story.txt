There was once a pair of poor folk; they had nothing in the world except three sons. What the two eldest were called, I don’t know, but the youngest was named Per.

When the parents died, the children were to inherit from them, but there was nothing to take except a pot, a griddle-stone, and a cat. The eldest, who was to have the best, took the pot. “When I lend the pot out, I always get to scrape what’s left in it,” he said. The next took the griddle-stone. “For when I lend the griddle-stone out, I always get a taste-cake,” he said. But the youngest had little to choose from; if he was to have anything at all, it had to be the cat.

“If I lend the cat out, I won’t get anything for her,” he said. “If the cat gets a splash of milk, she’ll drink it herself. Still, I’ll take her with me; it’s a shame she should stay here and starve.”

So the brothers set out into the world to try their luck, and each took his own road.

But when the youngest had walked a while, the cat said, “You shall be paid for not wanting me to stay behind in the old room and starve. Now I’ll go off into the forest and get hold of some fine creatures, and you shall go up to the King’s hall you see over there and tell the King you’re bringing him a small gift. And when he asks who it is from, you shall say it is from Sir Per.”

Per hadn’t waited long before the cat came back out of the forest with a reindeer; she had leapt up onto its head, and when she had seated herself between its antlers, she said, “If you don’t go straight to the King’s hall, I’ll claw your eyes out.” The reindeer didn’t dare do anything else.

When Per came to the King’s hall, he led the reindeer into the kitchen and said, “I’ve brought a small gift for the King, if he won’t despise it.”

The King came out into the kitchen, and when he saw the great, splendid reindeer, he was delighted. “But my dear friend! Who is it that sends me so fine a present?” said the King.

“Oh, it comes from Sir Per,” said the boy.

“Sir Per?” said the King. “And where am I to say he lives?” For he thought it a disgrace that he shouldn’t know so worthy a man.

But the boy would not tell him at all; he didn’t dare, he said, for his master’s sake. So the King gave Per plenty of drinking money and asked him to give his greetings at home and say many thanks for the gift.

The next day the cat went into the forest again, sprang up onto a stag’s head, sat between its antlers, and threatened it into going to the King’s hall. Per went into the kitchen with it again and said he had brought a small gift for the King, if he would not despise it. The King was even gladder of the stag than he had been of the reindeer, and again he asked who it was who could send him so fine a present.

“It is from Sir Per,” said the boy; but when the King wanted to know where Sir Per lived, he got the same answer as the day before, and that time he gave Per even more drinking money.

The third day the cat came with a moose. And when Per went into the kitchen at the King’s hall, he said he had yet another small gift for the King, if he would not despise it. The King came at once into the kitchen, and when he caught sight of that great, splendid moose, he was so happy he scarcely knew which foot to stand on, and that day he gave Per many, many more coins—surely a hundred dollars. He was determined to know where Sir Per lived, and he pried and asked about this and that, but the boy said he simply didn’t dare to tell, because his master had forbidden it so strictly.

“Then tell Sir Per to visit me,” said the King.

Yes, the boy would do so, he said.

But when he came out of the King’s hall again and met the cat, Per said, “You’ve got me into a fine scrape. Now the King wants me to come visit him, and I have nothing but these rags I’m standing in.”

“Oh, don’t be afraid of that,” said the cat. “In three days you’ll have horses and a carriage, and such grand clothes that gold will drip from you; then you can well visit the King. But everything you see at the King’s you must say you have far grander and finer at home. Don’t forget that.”

No, Per would remember it, he thought.

When the three days were up, the cat came with carriage and horses and clothes and everything Per needed; it was all so splendid that no one had ever seen the like. So he set out, and the cat sprang along with him. The King received him well enough; but whatever the King offered him and whatever he showed him, Per said it might be fine, but at home he had it finer and grander still. The King did not like that overmuch; but Per held to it, and at last the King grew so angry he could no longer contain himself.

“Now I will go home with you,” said the King, “and see if it is true that you have things so much better and grander. But if you lie, God have mercy on you—I’ll say no more!”

“You’ve got me into a fine scrape,” said Per to the cat. “Now the King wants to come home with me; but my home is not so easy to find.”

“Oh, don’t trouble yourself about that,” said the cat. “Just drive after me, where I run ahead.”

So they traveled: first Per, driving after the cat as she ran ahead, and then the King with all his men.

After they had driven a good way, they came to a great, thick flock of beautiful sheep; their wool was so long it almost reached the ground.

“If you will say that flock of sheep belongs to Sir Per when the King asks you, you shall have this silver spoon,” said the cat to the shepherd; she had brought it with her from the King’s hall.

Yes, he would gladly do that.

So when the King came, he said to the shepherd boy, “Now I have never seen so large and handsome a flock of sheep! Who owns it, my little lad?”

“It belongs to Sir Per,” said the boy.

A little farther on they came to a great, great herd of fine brindled cows, so fat they shone.

“If you will say that herd belongs to Sir Per when the King asks you, you shall have this silver ladle,” said the cat to the herding girl—the ladle too she had taken from the King’s hall.

“Yes, gladly,” said the herding girl.

When the King came up, he was quite astonished at the great, splendid herd, for such a fine herd he thought he had never seen before, and he asked the girl who went herding whose brindled cattle they were.

“Oh, they belong to Sir Per,” said the girl.

Then they traveled on a bit more, and came to a great, great drove of horses—the finest horses one could see, big and fat, and six of each color: red and dun and blue-gray.

“If you will say that drove of horses belongs to Sir Per when the King asks you, you shall have this silver beaker,” said the cat to the herder—she had taken that too from the King’s hall.

Yes, he would, said the boy.

When the King came up, he marveled beyond measure at that great, splendid drove of horses; for such horses he had never seen the like of, he said. Then he asked the herding boy whose the red and dun and blue-gray horses were.

“They belong to Sir Per,” said the boy.

And when they had traveled a long stretch farther, they came to a castle. First there was a gate of brass, then one of silver, and then one of gold. The castle itself was of silver, so bright it hurt the eyes, for the sun shone on it just as they arrived. They went in, and there the cat told Per to say he lived. Inside the castle was even grander than without: everything was gold—chairs and tables and benches.

When the King had walked about and looked at everything, high and low, he grew quite ashamed. “Yes, Sir Per has things finer than I do; there’s no use denying it,” he said, and he wanted to leave. But Per asked him to stay and eat supper with him, and the King did—though he was sour and peevish the whole time.

While they sat at table, the troll who owned the castle came and pounded on the gate.

“Who is it that eats my food and drinks my mead like swine in there?” bellowed the troll.

The moment the cat heard him, she ran out to the gate.

“Wait a little, and I’ll tell you how the farmer goes about his winter rye,” said the cat, and she spoke at length and breadth about winter rye. “First the farmer plows his field,” she said, “then he manures it, and then he plows it again.”

Just then the sun rose.

“Turn around and look, and you shall see the beautiful, lovely maiden behind you!” said the cat to the troll.

So the troll turned, and saw the sun, and burst.

“Now all this is yours,” said the cat to Sir Per. “Now you must cut my head off; that is the only thing I ask of you for what I have done for you.”

“No,” said Sir Per, “I won’t do that.”

“Yes,” said the cat. “If you don’t, I’ll claw your eyes out.”

So Sir Per had to do it, though he hated it: he cut the cat’s head off. But at that very moment she became the loveliest princess anyone could set eyes on, and Sir Per was quite taken with her.

“Yes, all this splendor was once mine,” said the princess, “but that troll enchanted me so that I had to be a cat with your parents. Now you may do as you will—whether you take me for your queen or not; for now you are king over the whole realm,” said the princess.

Oh yes, it may well be that Sir Per wanted her for his queen. So there was a wedding and a feast for eight days, and then I was no longer with Sir Per and his queen.
