Once there was an old woman who had a son. She was poor and frail, so at dinnertime she sent the boy out to the storehouse to fetch meal for porridge. But just as he stepped onto the storehouse steps, the North Wind came roaring and swirling, snatched the meal from him, and shot up into the sky with it.

The boy went back for more. Yet when he came out on the steps again, the North Wind came sweeping past once more and took the meal from him. And the same thing happened the third time. This made the boy angry; it seemed downright unreasonable to him that the North Wind should behave like that. So he decided to go look for him and demand the meal back.

Off he set. The road was long, and he walked and walked; at last he came to the North Wind’s home.

“Good day,” said the boy, “and thank you for last time.”

“Good day,” answered the North Wind—he spoke rough and blunt—“and thank you for last time yourself. What do you want?” he said.

“Oh,” said the boy, “I’d like to ask you, if you’d be so good as to give me back that meal you took from me on the storehouse steps. We’ve little enough, and if you’re going to swoop down like that and snatch away the only grain we have, it’ll be nothing but starving to death.”

“I don’t have any meal,” said the North Wind. “But since you’re in such need, you shall have a cloth that will provide you with anything you could wish for, as long as you say: Cloth, spread yourself and lay out all kinds of costly dishes!”

The boy was well pleased with that. But the road home was so long he couldn’t reach it before nightfall, so he stopped at an inn along the way. When the people there were about to sit down to supper, he laid the cloth on a table in the corner and said, “Cloth, spread yourself and lay out all kinds of costly dishes!”

He had never said it before, but at once the cloth did it, and everyone thought it a marvelous thing. No one liked it better than the innkeeper’s wife. No more fuss with roasting and boiling, no more setting tables and carrying platters, no more fetching and serving, she thought. So when night grew late and everyone was asleep, she took the boy’s cloth and put another in its place—one exactly like it to look at, but that couldn’t set out even an oatcake.

When the boy woke, he took his cloth and went on his way. That day he reached home to his mother.

“Well,” he said, “I’ve been to the North Wind. He’s a decent fellow, for he gave me this cloth, and when I only say: Cloth, spread yourself and lay out all kinds of costly dishes! then I get all the food I could ever want.”

“Oh, I’m sure,” said his mother, “but I won’t believe it until I see it.”

The boy hurried to set out a table, laid the cloth upon it, and said, “Cloth, spread yourself and lay out all kinds of costly dishes!” But the cloth didn’t lay out so much as a bite of flatbread.

“There’s nothing for it but that I must go to the North Wind again,” said the boy, and off he went. Toward afternoon he came to where the North Wind lived.

“Good evening,” said the boy.

“Good evening,” said the North Wind.

“I want justice for the meal you took from me,” said the boy, “for the cloth I got didn’t do much.”

“I don’t have any meal,” said the North Wind, “but there you have a billy-goat that makes gold ducats, if you only say: My goat, make money!”

That suited the boy well. But it was so far home that he couldn’t get there that day either, so he spent the night again at the inn. Before he asked for anything, he tested the goat, for he wanted to see if it was true what the North Wind had said—and true it was. But when the innkeeper saw it, he thought it was a fine goat indeed, and when the boy had fallen fast asleep, he took another goat that certainly did not make gold ducats and put it in its place.

Next morning the boy set off, and when he came home to his mother he said, “The North Wind is a kind man after all; now he’s given me a goat that can make gold ducats, if I only say: My goat, make money!”

“Oh, I’m sure,” said his mother. “That’s nothing but talk, and I won’t believe it till I see it.”

“My goat, make money!” said the boy; but money was not what the goat made.

So he went again to the North Wind and said the goat was no good, and that he wanted justice for the meal.

“Well, now I have nothing else to give you,” said the North Wind, “except that old stick standing over there in the corner. But it’s the sort of stick that, when you say: My stick, strike! it strikes, until you say: My stick, stand still!”

Since the road was long, the boy went that evening as well to the inn. But now he could guess what had happened to the cloth and the goat, so he lay down at once on the bench and began to snore, pretending to be asleep. The innkeeper, who could also guess the stick was worth something, hunted up another that looked like it and meant to swap it as soon as he heard the boy snoring. But at the very moment the innkeeper reached for the stick, the boy shouted, “My stick, strike!”

And the stick flew at the innkeeper and began to thrash him so that he sprang over tables and benches, howling and screaming, “Oh Lord God! oh Lord God! make the stick stop, or it will beat me to death! You shall have back both the cloth and the goat!”

When the boy thought the innkeeper had had enough, he said, “My stick, stand still!” Then he took the cloth and tucked it into his pocket, held the stick in his hand, tied a cord around the goat’s horns, and walked home with everything. That was fine justice for the meal!
