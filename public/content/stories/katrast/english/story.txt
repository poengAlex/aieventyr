Once upon a time there was a king whose queen had died. She left him a daughter who was so kind and so lovely that nobody anywhere could have been kinder or lovelier.

For a long time the King went about grieving. He had loved his queen dearly. But in the end he grew tired of living alone, and he married a widowed queen who had a daughter of her own. That daughter was every bit as ugly and mean as the King’s daughter was kind and fair.

The new Queen and her daughter hated the girl for being so lovely. But as long as the King was at home, they didn’t dare lay a finger on her. He was far too fond of her.

Then the King went to war with another king and rode off at the head of his army, and now the Queen could do as she liked. She starved the girl and beat her and chased her from corner to corner. In the end she decided that everything in the palace was too good for her, and sent her out to herd the cattle.

So the King’s daughter trudged after the cattle all day long, through the forest and up on the mountain. She was given little to eat, or nothing at all. She grew pale and thin, and she cried nearly all the time.

Among the cattle was a big blue bull who always looked freshly polished. He often came over to her and let her stroke him. One day, when she sat crying again, the bull came up to her.

“Why are you so sad?” he asked.

She didn’t answer. She just went on crying.

“Well,” said the bull, “I know why, even if you won’t tell me. You’re crying because the Queen is cruel to you and wants to starve you to death. But you needn’t worry about food. In my left ear there’s a cloth. Take it out and spread it, and you can have as many dishes as you like.”

She knelt beside him and slipped her hand into his left ear, and there it was: a cloth, folded small. She spread it on the grass, and at once it set itself with the finest food anyone could wish for. There was roast meat still steaming, white bread and sweet cakes, and wine and mead to drink.

She soon had flesh on her bones again. She grew so rosy and round and fair that the Queen and her scrawny daughter went blue and white with spite. The Queen couldn’t make it out. How could the girl look so well on next to nothing? One of the servants had to be feeding her on the sly. So she sent a maid to follow her into the forest and find out.

The maid crept after her and crouched behind a tree among the ferns. She saw the girl take a cloth out of the blue bull’s ear and spread it on the grass. She saw the cloth set out a feast, and she saw the girl eat her fill. Then she ran home and told the Queen.

Now the King came home from the war. He had beaten the other king, and there was great rejoicing all over the palace, and nobody was happier than his daughter. But the Queen took to her bed. She drew the curtains and groaned whenever anyone came near, and she pressed a heavy purse into the doctor’s hand.

“Her Majesty will never get well,” said the doctor, “unless she eats the flesh of the blue bull.”

The princess asked whether nothing else would do, and so did all the servants. They begged for the bull, for everyone was fond of him.

“There isn’t a bull like him in the whole kingdom!” they said.

But no. The bull had to be slaughtered, the bull would be slaughtered, and that was all there was to it.

When she heard that, her heart sank, and she went down to the byre. There stood the bull with his head hanging, looking so miserable that she burst into tears.

“What are you crying for?” said the bull.

“Father’s home,” she said, “and the Queen has taken to her bed, and the doctor says she’ll never get well unless she eats you. They’re coming to kill you in the morning.”

“If they take my life first, they’ll soon take yours,” said the bull. “If you feel as I do, we’ll leave tonight.”

It was hard to run away from her father. But it was harder still to stay under the same roof as the Queen. So she promised the bull she would come.

That night, when everyone was asleep, she crept down to the byre. The bull took her on his back and was off as fast as his legs would carry him. At first light the men came with their knives, and the stall was empty. When the King got up and asked for his daughter, she was gone too. He sent riders out in every direction and had the news called out on the church hill, but nobody had seen a thing.

Meanwhile the bull carried the King’s daughter through many lands, and at last they came to a great copper wood. Every trunk and branch, every leaf and flower in it was copper.

Before they went in, the bull said, “Now take care in this wood. Don’t touch so much as a leaf, or it’s all over for you and for me. A troll with three heads lives here, and the wood is his.”

Goodness, no. She would be ever so careful and touch nothing at all.

And she was careful. She leaned this way and that and pushed the branches aside with her hands. But the trees grew so close that there was hardly room to get through, and whatever she did, a leaf came off in her hand.

“Ow, ow! What have you done now?” said the bull. “Now we’ll have to fight for our lives. But keep that leaf safe.”

They had barely reached the far edge of the wood when a troll with three heads came rushing at them.

“Who’s that touching my wood?” said the troll.

“It’s as much mine as yours,” said the bull.

“We’ll fight you for it!” screamed the troll.

“Suits me,” said the bull.

They ran at each other and fought. The bull butted and kicked with all the strength in his body, but the troll hit back just as hard, and it took all day before the bull made an end of him. By then the bull was so torn and battered that he could barely walk, and they had to rest for a day. Then he told her to take the horn of ointment that hung from the troll’s belt and rub him with it. She did, and he was himself again, and the next day they plodded on.

They travelled for many, many days, and at long last they came to a silver wood. Every trunk and branch, every leaf and flower in it was silver.

Before they went in, the bull said, “For heaven’s sake, take care in this wood. Don’t touch anything, not so much as a leaf, or it’s all over for you and for me. A troll with six heads owns this one, and I hardly think I can beat him.”

“I’ll take care,” said the princess. “I won’t touch anything you don’t want me to.”

But the silver wood was so thick and so tangled that they could barely get on. She went as carefully as she knew how, ducking and pushing the branches away in front of her, but they kept whipping her across the eyes, and whatever she did, a leaf came off.

“Ow, ow! What have you done now?” said the bull. “Now we’ll have to fight for our lives, and this troll has six heads and is twice as strong as the last one. But keep the leaf, and keep it safe.”

The words were hardly out of his mouth when the troll came.

“Who’s that touching my wood?” he said.

“It’s as much mine as yours,” said the bull.

“We’ll fight you for it!” screamed the troll.

“Suits me,” said the bull, and he went for the troll and gored his eyes out and drove his horns clean through him. But the troll hit just as hard, and it took three whole days before the bull finished him off. By then the bull was so weak that he could scarcely move, and so badly wounded that the blood ran off him. He told her to take the horn of ointment from the troll’s belt and rub him with it. She did, and he mended, but they had to lie there a whole week before he could walk.

When they set off again, the bull was still weak, and they went slowly. The princess wanted to spare him. She was young and light on her feet, she said, and she could easily walk. But he wouldn’t hear of it. Up on his back she had to go.

They travelled a long time and through many lands, and she had no idea where they were going. But at last they came to a golden wood. It was so splendid that gold dripped from it, and every trunk and branch, every flower and leaf was pure gold.

Here it went just as it had in the copper wood and the silver wood. The bull told her she must on no account touch anything, for this wood belonged to a troll with nine heads, bigger and stronger than the other two put together, and the bull didn’t believe he could beat him.

No, she would be careful. He could count on that.

But the golden wood was thicker than the silver one, and the further in they went, the worse it grew. The trees crowded closer and closer and the way grew tighter and tighter, until she thought they would never get through. She was so afraid of tearing anything off that she twisted and ducked and bent from side to side and pushed the branches away in front of her. But they kept lashing her across the eyes, so that she couldn’t see what she was grabbing, and before she knew it she had a golden apple in her hand.

She was so frightened that she cried, and she wanted to throw it away. But the bull said, “Keep it, and hide it well,” and he comforted her as best he could. All the same, he thought it would be a hard fight, and he doubted it would end well.

Then the troll with nine heads came, and he was so hideous that she hardly dared look at him.

“Who’s that touching my wood?” he screamed.

“It’s as much mine as yours,” said the bull.

“We’ll fight you for it!” screamed the troll.

“Suits me,” said the bull.

Then they ran at each other, and it was so dreadful to watch that the King’s daughter nearly fainted. She clutched the golden apple to her chest and turned her face away. The bull gored out the troll’s eyes and drove his horns deep into his body, but the troll hit back just as hard. Every time the bull killed one of the heads, the others blew life into it again. It went on for a whole week before the bull could finish him.

By then the bull was so broken and so weak that he couldn’t stir. He was covered in wounds from horns to tail, and he couldn’t even tell her to take the troll’s horn of ointment and rub him with it. She did it anyway, and he came round. But they had to lie there for three weeks before he was fit to go on.

Then they set off slowly, for the bull said they had a little further to go. They crossed many great ridges thick with forest, and after a while they came up onto the mountain.

“Can you see anything?” asked the bull.

“No, nothing but the sky and the wild mountain,” said the King’s daughter.

The higher they climbed, the flatter the mountain became, and the further they could see.

“Can you see anything now?” said the bull.

“Yes, I can see a little castle, far, far away,” said the princess.

“It isn’t so little, though,” said the bull.

At long last they came to a great grassy mound at the foot of a sheer cliff.

“Can you see anything now?” said the bull.

“Yes, now I can see the castle close by, and now it’s much, much bigger,” said the King’s daughter.

“That’s where you’re going,” said the bull. “Just below the castle there’s a pigsty, and that’s where you’ll live. In the pigsty you’ll find a smock made of wood. Put it on, go up to the castle, say your name is Kari Woodsmock, and ask for work. But first you must take your little knife and cut off my head. Then skin me, roll up the hide and lay it under the cliff here, and put the copper leaf and the silver leaf and the golden apple inside it. Over by the rock there’s a stick. Whenever you want anything of me, just knock on the cliff with it.”

“No,” she said. “I can’t.”

“It’s the only thanks I want,” said the bull, “for all I’ve done for you.”

There was no refusing him after that. It hurt her to the heart, but she toiled away at the great beast with her little knife until his head was off and his hide was free. She rolled up the hide, and with tears running down her face and her hands shaking, she pushed the copper leaf and the silver leaf and the golden apple inside it. She wedged the bundle into a hollow under the cliff, where the shadow lay deepest. The stick stood against the rock, just where the bull had said, a plain stick like any other.

Then she went down to the pigsty, crying all the way. The smock was there. It was made of thin wooden boards laced together like the staves of a barrel, and it rattled at every step. She put it on and went up to the castle, and in the kitchen she asked for work and said her name was Kari Woodsmock.

“Yes,” said the cook, “you can stay and do the washing-up. The last girl has just left. But when you get tired of it here, you’ll be off too, I dare say.”

“No,” said Kari. “I won’t.”

She turned out to be rather good at washing up, and before long she was as black with soot as the pots she scrubbed.

One Sunday there were guests coming to the castle, and Kari asked if she could carry the washing water up to the Prince. The others laughed at her.

“What would you do up there?” they said. “Do you think the Prince wants to see the likes of you?”

But Kari didn’t give up. She begged and begged, and in the end they let her go. As she climbed the stairs, the wooden smock clattered so loudly that the Prince came out to see.

“And what are you supposed to be?” he said.

“I’m bringing your washing water,” said Kari.

“Do you think I want washing water you’ve carried?” said the Prince, and he threw it all over her.

There was nothing she could do about that. But afterwards she asked if she might go to church, and they said yes, since the church was close by. First, though, she went up to the cliff and knocked with the stick, just as the bull had told her. At once a man came out.

“What do you want?” he asked.

“I’ve been given leave to go to church and hear the priest,” said Kari, “but I’ve nothing to wear.”

So he brought her a dress that shone like the copper wood, and a horse and saddle as well.

At church she was so lovely and so splendid that everybody wondered who she was. Hardly anyone heard a word the priest said, because they were all too busy looking at her, and the Prince couldn’t take his eyes off her for a moment.

As she went out, the Prince ran after her, and in the crush at the church door he caught hold of one of her gloves, and it came away in his hand. She went to her horse and mounted, and the Prince came after her and asked where she was from.

“I’m from Washland,” said Kari. And when the Prince held out the glove to give it back, she said:

“Light before and dark behind, so where I ride the Prince can’t find!”

The Prince had never seen a glove like it. He rode far and wide asking for the land that the proud lady had named, but nobody could tell him where it was.

The next Sunday somebody had to take a towel up to the Prince.

“Oh, can I take it?” said Kari.

“What’s the use?” said the others in the kitchen. “You saw what happened last time.”

But Kari wouldn’t give up. She begged until they let her, and then she ran up the stairs so that the wooden smock clattered. Out came the Prince, and when he saw it was Kari, he snatched the towel and flung it straight in her face.

“Get out of my sight, you ugly troll!” he said. “Do you think I want a towel you’ve had your black fingers on?”

Then the Prince went off to church, and Kari asked if she could go too. What did she want in church, they asked, when she had nothing to wear but that wooden smock and was so black and so ugly? But Kari said the priest was such a fine preacher, and she got so much good out of what he said. In the end they let her go.

She went to the cliff and knocked, and the man came out and gave her a dress much finer than the first. It was stitched all over with silver and shone like the silver wood. And she got a splendid horse, with a saddlecloth embroidered in silver and a silver bit.

When the King’s daughter reached the church, the churchgoers were still standing outside. They all wondered who she could be, and the Prince was there in a flash to hold her horse while she got down. But she jumped down by herself and said there was no need. Her horse was so well trained that it stood still when she told it to and came when she called.

Then everyone went in, but hardly anyone heard what the priest said, because they were all looking at her, and the Prince was even more taken with her than before.

When the sermon was over and she came out to mount her horse, the Prince was there again, asking where she was from.

“I’m from Towelland,” said the King’s daughter, and at that moment she dropped her riding whip. As the Prince bent down to pick it up, she said:

“Light before and dark behind, so where I ride the Prince can’t find!”

And she was gone. The Prince had no idea where. He rode far and wide asking for Towelland, but nobody could tell him where it was, and he had to be patient once more.

The next Sunday somebody had to take a comb up to the Prince. Kari asked if she could take it. The others reminded her what had happened the last two times and scolded her for wanting to show herself to the Prince, black and ugly as she was in that wooden smock. But she kept on asking until they let her go. When she came clattering up the stairs again, the Prince came out, took the comb and threw it after her.

“Clear off!” he said.

Then the Prince went to church, and Kari asked to go too. Once again they asked what she wanted there, with no clothes fit to be seen in. If the Prince or anyone else caught sight of her, they said, it would be the ruin of her and of them too. But Kari said people would have other things to look at, and she kept asking until they let her go.

Then it went just as it had the two times before. She went to the cliff and knocked with the stick, and the man came out and gave her a dress finer still. It was almost all gold and diamonds. And she got a noble horse, with a saddlecloth embroidered in gold and a golden bit.

This time, when she reached the church, the priest himself and the whole congregation were standing outside, waiting for her. The Prince came running to hold her horse, but she jumped down and said:

“No, thank you, there’s no need. My horse is so well trained, it stands still when I tell it to.”

Then everyone hurried inside, and the priest climbed into the pulpit, but nobody heard a word he said. They were all looking at her and wondering where she came from, and the Prince was more in love than ever. He noticed nothing and heard nothing. He only looked at her.

The Prince had a plan, though. He had poured a pot of raw tar across the church porch, so that he could help her over it. But when the sermon was over and she came out, she didn’t wait for any help. She put her foot right in the middle of the tar and jumped across, and one of her gold shoes stayed stuck in it. By the time the Prince came racing out of the church, she was already on her horse, and he asked her where she was from.

“From Combland,” said Kari. And when the Prince held out the gold shoe, she said:

“Light before and dark behind, so where I ride the Prince can’t find!”

This time too the Prince had no idea where she had gone. He travelled around the world for a long, long time asking for Combland, but nobody could tell him where it was. So he let it be known that he would marry the woman who could wear the gold shoe.

Women came from far and near, pretty and plain, but not one of them had a foot small enough for the gold shoe. At long last Kari Woodsmock’s wicked stepmother came too, and she brought her daughter, and the shoe fitted the daughter. But she was so ugly and so sour that the Prince had no wish at all to keep his promise. All the same, the wedding was made ready and she was dressed as a bride. And as they rode to church, a little bird sat in a tree and sang:

“Heel cut short and toe cut too, there’s blood in Kari Woodsmock’s shoe!”

They looked, and the bird had told the truth. Blood was trickling out of the shoe. So all the maids and all the other women in the castle had to come to the great hall and try the shoe on, while servants and townsfolk crowded in to watch. It wouldn’t fit any of them.

“But where’s Kari Woodsmock?” asked the Prince, when everyone else had tried. He understood the speech of birds, and he hadn’t forgotten what this one had sung.

“Oh, her!” said the others. “No use bringing her in. She’s got feet like a horse.”

“Maybe so,” said the Prince, “but everyone else has tried, so Kari can try too.”

“Kari!” he called through the door, and up the stairs she came, with the wooden smock clattering as if a whole regiment of dragoons were charging up.

“Now you’ll try on the gold shoe and be a princess too!” said the other girls, and they laughed and jeered at her.

Kari picked up the shoe and slipped her foot into it as easily as anything. Then she threw off the wooden smock, and it fell in a heap at her feet. There she stood in the dress of gold and diamonds, shining, and on her other foot she wore the gold shoe’s twin. The laughing stopped.

The Prince knew her at once. He was so happy that he ran to her and put his arms around her waist and kissed her. When he heard that she was a king’s daughter too, he was happier still. And then there was a wedding.

Snip, snap, snout, this tale’s told out.
