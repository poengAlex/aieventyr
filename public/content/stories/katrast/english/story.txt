Once there was a king who had become a widower. By his queen he had a daughter, so gentle and so beautiful that no one could be gentler or more beautiful. For a long time he mourned the queen he had loved so dearly; but at last he grew weary of living alone and married a widowed queen who also had a daughter. Yet that daughter was as ugly and wicked as the other was kind and fair. The stepmother and her daughter envied the king’s daughter because she was so lovely; but as long as the king was at home they did not dare do her harm, for he was very fond of her.

Time passed, and the king went to war against another king and rode out with his men. Then the queen thought she could do as she pleased, and she both starved and beat the king’s daughter and hounded her into every corner. At last she decided that everything was too good for the girl and sent her out to herd the cattle. So the princess went with the herd and watched them in the forest and on the mountain. She got little or no food; she grew pale and thin and almost always wept and was miserable.

Among the cattle was a big blue bull that always kept himself sleek and shining. He often came over to the princess and let her stroke him. One time, as she sat crying and grieving again, the bull came up to her and asked why she was so sorrowful. She answered nothing, only went on crying.

“Yes,” said the bull, “I know well enough, even if you won’t tell me. You weep because the queen is cruel to you and means to starve you to death. But you needn’t worry about food. In my left ear there lies a cloth, and when you take it out and spread it, you can have as many dishes as you like.”

So she did—took the cloth and spread it on the grass, and at once it set out the most delicious dishes anyone could wish for; there was wine and mead and sweet cakes. Before long she grew strong again, and became so rosy and round and fair that the queen and her scrawny daughter turned blue and pale with spite. The queen could not understand how her stepdaughter could look so well on such poor fare. So she told a maidservant to follow the girl into the woods and watch how it was done, for she believed one of the servants was feeding her. The maid went after her and spied, and she saw the princess take the cloth out of the blue bull’s ear and spread it, and the cloth laid out the finest meal, which the princess ate with pleasure. The maid went home and told the queen.

Now the king came home, having defeated the king he had been at war with. There was great rejoicing throughout the palace, and no one was happier than the king’s daughter. But the queen took to her bed and played at being sick, and she gave the doctor a great deal of money so that he would say she could not recover unless she ate the flesh of the blue bull. The king’s daughter and the household asked whether nothing else could help and begged for the bull, for all were fond of him, and they said there was no bull like him in the whole realm. But no—he had to be slaughtered; he would be slaughtered; there was no other remedy.

When the king’s daughter heard it, she felt sick at heart and went down to the byre to the bull. There he stood too, with his head hanging, looking so sorrowful that she began to cry at the sight.

“What are you crying for?” said the bull.

Then she told him the king had come home, and the queen had taken to her bed, and she had bribed the doctor to say she could not get well unless she ate the blue bull’s flesh, and now he was to be killed.

“If they take my life first, they’ll soon take yours as well,” said the bull. “If you feel as I do, we’ll run away tonight.”

The princess thought it bitter to leave her father, but it was worse still to live in the same house as the queen; so she promised the bull she would come.

In the evening, when all the others had gone to bed, she crept down to the byre. The bull took her on his back and flew away as fast as he could. When the people rose at dawn next day to slaughter the bull, he was gone; and when the king got up and asked for his daughter, she too was missing. He sent messengers in every direction to search, and had them called for from the church hill, but no one had seen a trace of them.

Meanwhile the bull carried the princess through many lands, and they came at last to a great copper wood—trunks and branches and leaves and flowers, everything, was copper.

But before they went in, the bull said to the princess, “When we enter this wood you must take great care not to touch even a leaf, or it will be the end of both you and me; for a troll with three heads lives here and owns it.”

No, indeed—she would watch herself well and not touch a thing.

She was as careful as could be, bending aside for the branches and pushing them away with her hands; but the wood was so thick that it was almost impossible to get through, and no matter how she tried, she still tore off a leaf and ended with it in her hand.

“Ah, ah—what have you done now?” said the bull. “Now it’s a fight to the death. But keep the leaf safe.”

At once they were at the edge of the wood, and there came a troll rushing at them with three heads.

“Who is it that’s meddling with my wood?” said the troll.

“It’s as much mine as yours,” said the bull.

“Then we’ll wrestle for it!” screamed the troll.

“So be it,” said the bull.

They charged and fought. The bull gored and kicked with all his strength, but the troll struck just as hard. It lasted the whole day before the bull got the better of him, and then the bull was so covered in wounds and so battered that he could hardly walk. They had to rest for a day. Then the bull told the princess to take the healing horn that hung at the troll’s belt and rub him with it. She did so, and he recovered, and the next day they limped onward.

They traveled for many, many days, and at last they came to a silver wood—trunks and branches and leaves and flowers, everything, was silver.

Before the bull went in, he said, “When we enter this wood, for God’s sake be careful. You must not touch anything—don’t tear off so much as a leaf—or it will be the end of both you and me. A troll with six heads owns it, and I doubt I can manage him.”

“No,” said the princess, “I’ll watch myself and not touch what you don’t want me to touch.”

But when they entered the wood, it was so dense and cramped they could scarcely get forward. She went as warily as she could, bending aside for the branches and pushing them away with her hands; but every moment the branches struck her in the eyes, and no matter what she did she tore off a leaf.

“Ah, ah—what have you done now?” said the bull. “Now it’s a fight to the death. This troll has six heads and is twice as strong as the other. But keep the leaf and guard it well.”

In a moment the troll arrived.

“Who is it that’s meddling with my wood?” he said.

“It’s as much mine as yours,” said the bull.

“Then we’ll wrestle for it!” screamed the troll.

“So be it,” said the bull, and he hurled himself at the troll and gored out his eyes and drove his horns straight through him so his guts spilled out. But the troll struck just as hard, and it lasted three full days before the bull could take his life. Then the bull was so exhausted and broken he could barely move, and so full of wounds that blood ran from him. He told the princess to take the healing horn from the troll’s belt and rub him with it. She did, and he recovered; but they had to lie there and rest a whole week before he had strength to go farther.

At last they set off again. The bull was still fragile, and at first they went slowly. The princess wanted to spare him and said she was young and light of foot and could walk. But he would not allow it—she had to climb onto his back again.

So they traveled a long time through many lands, and the princess did not know where they were bound, but at last they came to a golden wood, so glorious that gold seemed to drip from it—trunks and branches and flowers and leaves were all pure gold.

Here it went the same as in the copper wood and the silver wood. The bull told the princess she must not, in any way, touch it, for a troll with nine heads owned this wood; it was far bigger and stronger than both the others together, and he did not believe he could master it. No, she would be careful—he could be sure of that. But when they entered, the wood was even thicker than the silver one, and the farther they went the worse it became: tighter and tighter, more and more cramped, until she felt they could not get through at all. She was so afraid of tearing anything that she twisted and bent this way and that to avoid the branches and pushed them aside with her hands; but every moment they whipped her in the eyes so she could not see where she grasped, and before she knew it she had a golden apple in her hand. She was so frightened she wept and tried to throw it away, but the bull told her to keep it and guard it well, and comforted her as best he could. Still, he believed it would be a hard battle and doubted it would end well.

Then the nine-headed troll came; he was so terrible the princess scarcely dared look at him.

“Who is it that’s meddling with my wood?” he roared.

“It’s as much mine as yours,” said the bull.

“Then we’ll wrestle for it!” roared the troll.

“So be it,” said the bull.

They charged and fought, and it was so dreadful to see that the princess nearly fainted. The bull gored out the troll’s eyes and drove his horns straight through him so his entrails poured out; but the troll struck just as hard, for every time the bull gored one head dead, the others blew life back into it again. It lasted a whole week before the bull could finally take his life. But then the bull was so ruined and frail he could not move. Wounds covered him everywhere; he could not even speak to tell the princess to take the healing horn and rub him. But she did it anyway, and he recovered; still, they had to lie there and rest three full weeks before he was fit to go on.

Then they set off little by little, for the bull said they needed only go a bit farther. They crossed many great wooded ridges, and after a while they came up onto the mountain.

“Do you see anything?” asked the bull.

“No, I see nothing but the sky and the wild mountain,” said the princess.

As they climbed higher, the mountain leveled out so they could see farther.

“Do you see anything now?” said the bull.

“Yes, I see a little castle far, far away,” said the princess.

“It’s not so little after all,” said the bull.

At length they came to a great mound where a sheer cliff face rose straight up.

“Do you see anything now?” said the bull.

“Yes—now I see the castle close by, and now it is much, much bigger,” said the princess.

“That is where you must go,” said the bull. “Just below the castle is a pigsty where you are to be. When you get there, you will find a wooden smock; you must put it on, go up to the castle, say your name is Kari Woodsmock, and ask for service. And now you must take your little knife and cut off my head. Then you must skin me and roll the hide together and lay it under the cliff here, and inside the hide you must put the copper leaf and the silver leaf and the golden apple. Over by that rock stands a stick; whenever you need me, just knock on the cliff with it.”

At first she would not; but when the bull said it was the only thanks he wanted for all he had done for her, she could do no other. It was so bitter to her, but she struggled and sawed with the knife at the great beast until she got off his head and took his hide; then she folded it and laid it under the cliff face and put the copper leaf and the silver leaf and the golden apple inside it.

When she had done that, she went over to the pigsty; but as she walked she wept and was miserable. There she put on the wooden smock and went up to the king’s hall. When she came into the kitchen, she asked for work and said her name was Kari Woodsmock. Yes, said the cook, she could have work—she could stay and wash the dishes, for the one who had done it before had recently gone off; “but when you grow tired of being here, you’ll likely go off too.” No, she would not, she said.

She was neat enough at washing dishes. On Sunday there were to be guests at the hall, and Kari begged permission to carry wash-water up to the prince. The others laughed at her and said, “What do you want up there? Do you think the prince will have anything to do with you, looking like that?”

But she would not give in; she begged and begged until at last she was allowed.

As she went up the stairs the wooden smock clattered and rattled, so the prince came out and asked, “What are you?”

“I’m to carry wash-water to you,” said Kari.

“Do you think I want the wash-water you carry?” said the prince, and he flung the water over her.

She had to go back with it. Then she asked permission to go to church as well, and she got it, for the church stood close by. But first she went to the cliff and knocked on it with the stick, just as the bull had told her. At once a man came out and asked what she wanted. The princess said she had been allowed to go to church and hear the parson, but she had no clothes to wear. So he brought her a dress as bright as the copper wood, and he gave her a horse and saddle too.

When she came to church she was so fair and splendid that everyone wondered who she was, and hardly anyone listened to the parson because they stared so at her. The prince himself liked her so well he could not take his eyes off her for a moment.

As she went out of the church, the prince sprang after her and pulled the church door shut behind her, and he kept one of her gloves in his hand. When she went and mounted her horse, the prince came after and asked where she was from.

“I’m from Washland,” said Kari; and when the prince took out the glove to give it back, she said,

“Light before and dark behind,
so the prince can’t see where I ride today!”

The prince had never seen the like of that glove, and he rode far and wide asking after the land the proud lady had named, but no one could tell him where it lay.

The next Sunday someone was to carry a towel up to the prince.

“Oh, let me take it up,” said Kari.

“What would be the point?” said the others in the kitchen. “You saw how it went last time.”

Kari would not give in, but begged until she was allowed. She ran up the stairs so the wooden smock rattled. The prince rushed out, and when he saw it was Kari he snatched the towel and flung it straight into her eyes.

“Be off with you, you ugly troll!” he said. “Do you think I want a towel you’ve handled with those black fingers of yours?”

Afterward the prince rode to church, and Kari begged to go too. They asked what she wanted at church, she who had nothing to wear but that wooden smock and was so black and hideous. But Kari said the parson was such a fine man to preach, and she got so much good from what he said, and at last they let her go. She went to the cliff and knocked, and the man came out and gave her a dress much finer than the first, embroidered all over with silver; it shone like the silver wood. And he gave her a splendid horse with a silver-embroidered caparison and a silver bit.

When the princess came to church, the people were still standing outside on the hill. All wondered who she was, and the prince was at once on the spot and came to hold her horse while she dismounted. But she leaped down and said he needn’t, for the horse was so well trained it stood still when she told it, and came when she called. Then they all went into church; but hardly anyone listened to the parson because they stared so at her, and the prince was even more taken with her than the first time.

When the sermon was over and she went out of the church and was about to mount, the prince came again and asked where she was from.

“I’m from Towelland,” said the princess, and just then she dropped her riding whip. As the prince bent to pick it up, she said,

“Light before and dark behind,
so the prince can’t see where I ride today!”

She was gone again, and the prince could not tell where she had gone. He rode far and wide asking after Towelland, but no one could say where it lay, and the prince had to be patient again.

The next Sunday someone was to take a comb up to the prince. Kari begged to take it, but the others reminded her how it had gone before and scolded her for wanting to show herself to the prince, so black and hideous in her wooden smock. Still she did not stop begging until they let her go. When she came rattling up the stairs again, the prince rushed out, took the comb and threw it after her, and told her to be off.

After that the prince rode to church, and Kari begged to go too. Again they asked what she wanted there, she who was so ugly and black, and had no clothes fit to be seen among people; let the prince or anyone else see her, they said, and both she and they would come to grief. But Kari said they had enough else to look at, and she did not stop begging until she got leave.

Now it went just as both the times before. She went to the cliff and knocked with the stick, and the man came out and gave her a dress even finer than the others—almost all of gold and diamonds. And he gave her a fine horse with a gold-embroidered caparison and a gold bit.

When the princess came to church, the parson and the congregation were still on the hill, waiting for her. The prince came running to hold her horse, but she leaped down and said, “No thank you, it isn’t needed; my horse is so well trained it stands still when I tell it.” Then they all hurried into the church and the parson to the pulpit; but no one listened to what he said because they stared so at her and wondered where she was from, and the prince was more in love than both the times before; he noticed nothing, only looked at her.

When the sermon ended and the princess was to leave the church, the prince had smeared a patch of raw tar in the porch so he could help her over. But she did not trouble about it—she set her foot right in the tar and sprang across, and one of her gold shoes stuck fast. When she had mounted her horse, the prince came flying out of the church and asked where she was from.

“From Combland,” said Kari. But when the prince would hand her the gold shoe, she said,

“Light before and dark behind,
so the prince can’t see where I ride today!”

The prince could not tell where she had gone now either, and he traveled for a long time through the world asking after Combland. But since no one could say where it was, he had it proclaimed that he would marry the one whose foot fitted the gold shoe.

So both fair and foul came from many quarters; but there was no one with so small a foot that she could get into the gold shoe. At last Kari Woodsmock’s wicked stepmother came too, with her daughter, and the shoe fitted her—yet she was ugly, and so hateful to look at that the prince was very loth to do what he had promised. Still the wedding was prepared, and she was dressed as a bride. But as they rode to church, a little bird sat in a tree and sang:

“A slice from the heel
and a slice from the toe—
Kari Woodsmock’s shoe
is full of blood!”

And when they looked, the bird had sung true, for blood seeped out of the shoe. Then all the serving girls and all the women at the castle had to come forward and try the shoe on; but it would fit none of them.

“But where is Kari Woodsmock?” asked the prince, when all the others had tried, for he understood bird-speech and remembered well what the bird had said.

“Oh, her!” said the others. “It can never help to bring her in—she has feet like a horse.”

“Maybe,” said the prince, “but since all the others have tried, Kari shall try as well.”

“Kari!” he called through the door, and Kari came up the stairs, and the wooden smock clattered as if a whole regiment of dragoons were charging up.

“Now you’ll fit the gold shoe and be a princess too!” said the other girls, laughing and mocking her.

Kari took up the shoe, slipped her foot into it as easily as she pleased, and threw off the wooden smock. Then she stood there in her gold dress, shining, and on her other foot she had the mate to the gold shoe. The prince knew her at once, and he was so glad he ran to her, took her around the waist, and kissed her. And when he heard she was a king’s daughter, he was gladder still—and then there was a wedding.

Snip, snap, snout—this tale is told out.
