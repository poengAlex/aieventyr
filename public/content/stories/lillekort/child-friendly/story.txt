There was once a very poor husband and wife. They lived in a tiny, drafty hut, and there was so little in it that some days they hardly had food, and the fire went out for want of wood. But even if they had almost nothing else, they had been blessed with many children, and each year there seemed to be one more.

Now they were expecting again, and the husband grew worried and grumbly. “We can barely feed the ones we have,” he sighed. And when the time came, he went out to the forest to fetch wood, because he felt too upset to stay inside and hear a new baby crying before there was any porridge to give.

As soon as the husband had gone, the wife gave birth to a fine little baby boy. And the very moment he arrived, he looked around the room as if he had been there before.

“Oh, dear Mother,” he said, in a small but clear voice, “give me some old clothes after my brothers and a little food for a couple of days. Then I will go out into the world and try my luck. I can see you already have plenty of children.”

“Goodness help me,” said his mother, “you are far too little. That will never do.”

But the tiny boy begged so hard and so long that at last she gave him some worn clothes and a little bundle of food. And out he went, as cheerful and brave as if the road were made for him.

Hardly had he gone when the wife had another baby boy. This one, too, looked around as soon as he was born.

“Oh, dear Mother,” he said, “give me some old clothes after my brothers and a little food for a couple of days. Then I will go out into the world and find my twin brother. You have children enough, I can see.”

“Goodness help me,” said the mother, “you are far too little, you poor thing. That will never do.”

But he begged and begged until she had to give in. She gave him old clothes and a small bundle of food, and he set off, determined to find the brother who had left first.

After he had walked a little while, he caught sight of someone ahead on the road. It was his twin. He called out, “Wait! You’re marching as if someone’s paying you. You might at least have looked back for your youngest brother before you went off into the wide world.”

The older one stopped and turned around. When the younger had hurried up and told him how it was—that they were twins and had both set out—the older said, “Then let’s sit down and see what Mother packed for us.” So they sat, shared their food, and felt less alone.

They walked on, and after a while they came to a little brook running through a green meadow. There the younger said, “Since we hurried away so fast we didn’t even have time to choose names at home, we should do it here.”

“What will you be called?” asked the older.

“I will be called Lillekort,” said the younger. “And you—what will you be called?”

“I will be called Kong Lavring,” said the older.

So they gave each other their names beside the brook and went on together. But after a while they reached a crossroads and decided to part, each taking a different way. They did so—but before long, somehow, they met again. They tried once more, and again they met. They tried a third time, and still they crossed paths as if the road itself kept folding them together.

At last they agreed, “Now we will truly go to opposite ends: one to the east and one to the west.”

“But if you ever get into real trouble,” said Kong Lavring, “call my name three times, and I will come help you. Only—don’t call until you truly need it.”

“I don’t think we’ll see each other soon,” said Lillekort.

Then they said goodbye. Lillekort went east, and Kong Lavring went west.

Lillekort had not walked very long alone before he met a very, very old woman, bent like a hook, with only one eye. She was patting the grass and the stones, searching and muttering to herself.

“My eye, my eye—where did it go?” she groaned.

Lillekort looked carefully, and there, by her shoe, lay the eye she had dropped—round and shiny like a little glass marble. He picked it up.

“Here it is,” said Lillekort, “but what will you give me for finding your eye?”

“I will give you a sword,” said the old woman, “a sword so good that it can beat a whole crowd, no matter how big.”

“Then give it here,” said Lillekort.

She handed him the sword, and he gave her back her eye. Then they each went their way.

After a while he met another very, very old bent woman with only one eye. She, too, was searching.

“My eye! I’ve lost my eye!” she cried.

Lillekort found it for her as well, and said, “What will you give me for finding your eye?”

“I will give you a ship,” said the old woman, “a ship that can travel on fresh water and salt water, over mountains and through deep valleys.”

“Then give it here,” said Lillekort.

She gave him a tiny little ship, so small he could slip it into his pocket. He returned her eye, and off he went.

After walking a long time, he met a third old bent woman with one eye, and she was searching and scolding the ground as if it had stolen from her.

“I’ve lost my eye!” she wailed.

Lillekort found it and held it up. “What will you give me for finding your eye?”

“I will give you a skill,” said she, “the skill to brew a hundred great loads of malt in a single batch.”

Lillekort thought that sounded useful, so he took the gift, gave her back her eye, and continued on his way.

After a little while he thought it might be worth trying the ship. He took the tiny ship from his pocket and set one foot in it. At once it began to grow. When he stepped in with the other foot, it grew as big as a real sea-going ship.

“Now go,” said Lillekort, “over fresh water and salt water, over mountains and through deep valleys, and don’t stop until you come to the king’s house.”

At once the ship rushed off as fast as a bird in the sky. It stopped just below the king’s castle.

People in the castle windows had seen a ship coming as if it were sailing through the air. They ran down to the shore, amazed. But while they were running, Lillekort stepped out, and the ship shrank at once back to pocket-size. He tucked it away before anyone could see it clearly.

So when the castle folk arrived, they saw only a small ragged boy standing by the water. The king asked where he came from.

“I don’t really know,” said Lillekort, “and I don’t quite know how I got here either. But please, could I have work at the castle? If there’s nothing else, I can carry wood and water for the kitchen girl.”

That seemed harmless enough, so he was allowed to stay.

When Lillekort came up to the castle, he saw that everything was covered in black cloth—outside and inside, walls and ceilings too. He asked the kitchen girl why.

“I’ll tell you,” she said. “Long ago the princess was promised to three trolls. Next Thursday evening the first troll will come to fetch her. Sir Red says he will save her, but nobody knows if he truly can. So there is sorrow in this house.”

When Thursday evening came, Sir Red led the princess down to the shore, for that was where she must meet the troll. Sir Red was supposed to guard her—but he didn’t look very brave. As soon as the princess sat on a rock by the water, Sir Red climbed up into a big tree and hid himself among the branches.

“Please don’t leave me,” the princess begged, crying.

“It’s better that one should be in danger than two,” said Sir Red, and he stayed hidden.

Meanwhile Lillekort begged the kitchen girl, very politely, to let him go down to the shore for a little while.

“What do you want down there?” she asked. “You’ve nothing to do with that.”

“Oh please,” said Lillekort. “I’d so like to go and see what’s happening, and maybe play a little with the other boys.”

“Well then,” said the kitchen girl, “go. But don’t you stay past the time I must hang the evening pot and set the roast. And bring back a good armful of wood.”

Lillekort promised and ran down to the shore.

No sooner had he reached the princess than the first troll came rushing in with wind and spray and a great roaring sound. It was huge and clumsy, and it had five heads.

“Fire!” roared the troll.

“Fire back!” said Lillekort.

“Can you fence?” roared the troll.

“If I can’t, I can learn,” said Lillekort.

The troll swung an iron bar so hard the sand and turf flew up in lumps. Lillekort sprang aside.

“Well!” said Lillekort. “That was something. Now you’ll see a swing from me!”

He drew the sword he had been given, and with one clean stroke he struck at the troll. The five heads flew off like big puffballs, rolling over the sand—and then, before they could do any harm, they turned to smoke and vanished. The troll itself sagged and melted away into the sea like dark mist.

When the princess saw she was safe, she laughed and cried at once. She danced right there on the sand.

“Rest a little on my lap,” she said to Lillekort. While he rested, she pulled a gold garment over him, so fine it shone even in the evening light.

Before long, Sir Red climbed down from the tree, peeking around to make sure everything was quiet. Then he frightened the princess with sharp words until she promised to say it was he who had saved her. As proof, he snatched the troll’s iron belt-clasp—the troll-mark—and hid it in his handkerchief. Then he led the princess back to the castle like a hero.

And now there was great praising of Sir Red. The king sat him close by his side at table and spoke of honoring him.

Lillekort, meanwhile, went to where the troll had come from and found a heap of strange treasures—gold and silver belts and shining things. He gathered a bundle, hurried back, and gave them to the kitchen girl.

She stared, hands to her cheeks. “Dear Lillekort, where did you get all that?”

“Oh,” said Lillekort, “I stopped by home for a moment, and some of these fell off a cart along the way, so I brought them to you.”

Since he said they were for her, she didn’t ask any more. She thanked him and hid them away.

The second Thursday evening came, and everything happened much the same. The castle was hung in black again. Sir Red boasted, “If I saved the princess from one troll, I can save her from another,” and he led her down to the shore.

But when it grew near the time, he climbed into the same tree again.

“It’s better that one should be in danger than two,” he whispered, and hid.

Again Lillekort begged to go down. Again the kitchen girl said he could—so long as he came back by the time the roast must be turned, and brought wood.

Lillekort ran down.

The second troll came rushing in even louder than the first. It was twice as big, and it had ten heads.

“Fire!” it roared.

“Fire back!” said Lillekort.

“Can you fence?” it roared.

“If I can’t, I can learn!” said Lillekort.

The troll swung a bigger iron bar. The ground jumped and the sand flew. But Lillekort held tight to his sword.

“Well!” said Lillekort. “That was something. Now you’ll see a swing from me!”

He struck, and the ten heads tumbled away over the sand and turned to smoke. The troll faded and vanished, leaving nothing but a cold gust and a few dark ripples on the water.

The princess clapped her hands with relief. “Rest a little on my lap,” she said. And while Lillekort rested, she pulled a silver garment over him.

Then Sir Red climbed down again, put on a brave face, and forced the princess to promise—again—that she would say he had saved her. He snatched the troll’s belt-clasp and hid it away, and marched the princess home as if he had done it all.

The king and everyone in the castle cheered him, and the king could hardly think of a reward grand enough.

Lillekort brought another bundle of gold and silver belts from the troll’s things and gave them to the kitchen girl. She asked where they came from, and he gave her the same simple answer, and she was glad.

When the third Thursday evening came, the castle was wrapped in black cloth once more, and sorrow filled the halls. Sir Red bragged louder than ever. “Two trolls were nothing,” he said. “A third will be nothing too.”

He led the princess to the shore—but when the time drew near, he climbed into the tree and hid.

The princess begged him to stay, but he would not. “It’s better that one should be in danger than two,” he said.

Lillekort begged to go down again, and at last the kitchen girl let him go, as long as he returned when the roast must be turned.

Lillekort ran down to the shore.

The third troll came rushing in like a storm. It was bigger than either of the others, and it had fifteen heads.

“Fire!” it roared.

“Fire back!” said Lillekort.

“Can you fence?” it roared.

“If I can’t, I can learn,” said Lillekort.

“I’ll teach you!” roared the troll, and swung its iron bar so hard that sand and turf flew.

“Well!” said Lillekort. “That was something. Now you’ll see a swing from me!”

He struck with the magic sword, and the fifteen heads rolled away over the beach and turned to smoke. The troll itself thinned into dark mist and vanished out over the sea.

The princess thanked Lillekort with all her heart.

“Rest a little on my lap,” she said, and while he rested, she pulled a bronze-colored garment over him.

“But how will everyone learn that it was you who saved me?” she asked softly.

“I’ll tell you,” said Lillekort. “Sir Red will lead you home and pretend he did it. They’ll promise him you and half the kingdom. But on the wedding day, when they ask whom you want to pour the toasts, you must say: ‘I want the little boy in the kitchen who carries wood and water.’ When I pour, I will drip one drop on Sir Red’s plate, but none on yours. He will get angry and give me a hard tap. We will let it happen three times. But the third time, you must say: ‘Shame on you for striking my dear one! He is the one who saved me, and he is the one I choose.’”

The princess promised.

Then Lillekort hurried back, and on the way he gathered more treasure from the troll’s things—gold, silver, and bright valuables—and again he gave the kitchen girl a great bundle of belts.

Sir Red climbed down from his tree as soon as he felt safe. He scared the princess into promising—one last time—that she would say he had saved her. Then he took the third troll-mark, the belt-clasp, and led her back to the castle.

The king was overjoyed and said Sir Red should have the princess and half the kingdom.

At last the wedding day came. Then the princess said, “I want the little boy in the kitchen—the one who carries wood and water—to pour the toasts.”

“What do you want with that sooty ragged boy?” Sir Red complained.

But the princess said firmly, “Only him.” And at last they had to agree.

So Lillekort came in, still looking like a kitchen boy. He poured, and—just as he had planned—he let one tiny drop fall onto Sir Red’s plate, but none onto the princess’s.

Sir Red scowled and gave Lillekort a rough tap. At once Lillekort’s kitchen rags slipped away, and underneath was the bronze garment.

Lillekort poured again. Another drop landed on Sir Red’s plate. Sir Red tapped him again—harder—and the bronze garment fell away, and underneath was the silver garment.

Lillekort poured a third time. Again a drop fell. Sir Red, angry and foolish, struck again—and the silver garment fell away, and Lillekort stood in shining gold, bright as sunrise.

Then the princess stood up and said so everyone could hear, “Shame on you for striking my dear one! He saved me, and he is the one I choose!”

Sir Red shouted that it was a lie. “I saved her!” he cried.

The king raised his hand. “Whoever saved my daughter should be able to show some proof.”

Sir Red ran at once and brought his handkerchief with the three troll-marks—the belt-clasps—inside it. Lillekort brought in the treasure he had taken from the trolls’ ship: gold and silver and costly things no one in the kingdom had ever seen before.

The king looked at both.

“Treasure like this,” said the king, “comes only from trolls. The one who has this must be the one who faced them.”

So Sir Red was sent away in disgrace and locked up far from the castle, and Lillekort was promised the princess and half the kingdom.

One day, as the king and Lillekort walked together, Lillekort asked, “Have you had any more children?”

“Yes,” said the king sadly. “I had another daughter. But the trolls took her away long ago, because there was no one who could rescue her. You shall have one daughter and half my kingdom. But if you can bring back the other, you may gladly have her too—and the other half as well.”

“I can try,” said Lillekort. “But I must have an iron rope five hundred ells long, and five hundred men, and food for them for fifteen weeks, because we must travel far out to sea.”

The king agreed, though he worried. “But I don’t have a ship big enough for all that.”

“I have a ship,” said Lillekort, and took the tiny ship from his pocket.

The king laughed, thinking it was a joke, but Lillekort only asked for what he had requested.

When everything was brought down to the shore, Lillekort said they must lay the rope into the ship first. No one could manage it—there was hardly even room for them all around the tiny boat. But Lillekort took hold of the rope himself and began feeding it in link by link. As he did, the ship grew larger and larger, until at last it was big enough for the rope, the five hundred men, the food, and Lillekort as well.

“Now go,” said Lillekort, “over salt water and fresh water, over mountains and through deep valleys, and don’t stop until you come to where the king’s daughter is.”

At once the ship sped away, whistling through the air and skimming over land and sea.

After they had traveled far, far away, the ship stopped in the middle of the ocean.

“Now we are here,” said Lillekort. “But the question is how we come back.”

He tied one end of the iron rope around his waist. “Now I must go down,” he said. “When I tug hard on the rope to come up, you must all pull together, every one of you, or neither you nor I will have much hope of getting home.”

Then he jumped into the sea with a great splash, and sank down, down, down until he reached the bottom.

There he saw a great mountain with a door in it, and he walked inside. In a room within sat the other princess, sewing quietly. When she saw Lillekort, she pressed her hands together.

“Thanks be to heaven!” she cried. “I haven’t seen a human face since I came here.”

“I’ve come for you,” said Lillekort.

“Oh, you can’t,” whispered the princess, frightened. “If the troll sees you, he will be terribly angry.”

“Then I suppose I should meet him,” said Lillekort. “Where is he?”

The princess told him the troll had gone out searching for someone who could brew a hundred great loads of malt in a single batch, because the troll planned a great feast and wanted more drink than anyone could make.

“I can do that,” said Lillekort.

“If only the troll weren’t so quick-tempered,” said the princess, “I could tell him safely. But I’m afraid he will roar the moment he comes in. Still—I must try to think of something. Hide in that little closet there.”

Lillekort did as she said. He had barely hidden when the troll came thundering in.

“Ugh!” growled the troll. “It smells like people in here.”

“A bird flew over the roof,” said the princess quickly, “with something from a ship in its beak, and it dropped it down the chimney. I cleaned it away at once, but perhaps the smell stayed.”

“Well,” grumbled the troll, “perhaps so.”

Then the princess asked, “Did you find anyone who can brew a hundred loads of malt in one batch?”

“No,” said the troll. “No one can do such a thing.”

“A little while ago,” said the princess, “someone came here who said he could.”

“You’re always so clever,” said the troll. “Why did you let him go, then?”

“I didn’t,” said the princess. “I was afraid you’d storm at him, so I hid him in the closet. If you didn’t find anyone, he’s still here.”

“Bring him out,” said the troll.

So Lillekort came forward. The troll stared at him and asked if it was true.

“Yes,” said Lillekort.

“Good,” said the troll. “Start at once. And mind you—make it strong!”

“It will have plenty of taste,” said Lillekort, and set to work.

“But I need more trolls to carry the brewing lids and heavy tubs,” he said. “The ones here aren’t enough.”

So the troll called in more and more helpers, until the place was crawling with them.

At last the brew was ready, and of course they all wanted to taste it—first the troll, then the others. But Lillekort had made it so strong and sleepy-making that, as each troll drank, it yawned and flopped down on the floor, snoring like thunder. One after another they fell fast asleep, and soon the whole mountain was quiet except for snoring.

Only a very old, thin troll-woman lay behind the oven, blinking.

“Oh, you poor thing,” said Lillekort. “You should have a taste too.”

He scraped the last of the brew from the bottom and gave it to her, and she, too, drifted off to sleep.

Then Lillekort looked around and spotted a great chest. He filled it with gold and silver, tied the rope around himself, the princess, and the chest, and tugged hard.

Up above, the five hundred men hauled together, and up came Lillekort and the princess and the treasure, safe and sound.

Once Lillekort stood on deck again, he said to the ship, “Now go—over salt water and fresh water, over mountains and through deep valleys—and don’t stop until you come to the king’s house.”

At once the ship sped home, leaving foam and spray behind.

When the people at the castle saw it, they hurried down with music and cheering. The king was happiest of all, because he had both his daughters back.

But now Lillekort felt uneasy, because both princesses had grown fond of him, and he only wished to marry the one he had saved first—the youngest. He didn’t want to hurt the other princess’s feelings either, and he walked about thinking and thinking, trying to find a kind solution.

Then he remembered his twin brother, Kong Lavring—so like him that no one could tell them apart. “If only my brother were here,” thought Lillekort, “then he could marry the other princess and share the kingdom, and everyone would be glad.”

So Lillekort went out of the castle and called, “Kong Lavring!”

No one came.

He called again, louder.

Still no one came.

Then he called a third time with all his strength, and at once Kong Lavring stood beside him.

“I told you not to call me unless you truly needed me,” said Kong Lavring. “And here there isn’t even a mosquito to bother you.”

And, being startled and cross, he gave Lillekort a firm shove so he stumbled onto the grass.

“Don’t go around pushing people,” said Lillekort, brushing himself off. “Listen: I’ve won one princess and half the kingdom, and then I brought back the other princess and the other half. I thought I’d give you one princess and half the kingdom with me. Do you think it’s fair to shove me after that?”

When Kong Lavring heard this, he apologized at once, and they were brothers and friends again.

“Now,” said Lillekort, “since we look so alike, swap clothes with me and go into the castle. The princesses will think you are me. Whichever one kisses you first, you shall marry, and I will marry the other.” For Lillekort knew the older princess was stronger and quicker, and he guessed what would happen.

Kong Lavring agreed. They swapped clothes, and Kong Lavring went into the castle.

When he entered the princesses’ room, both hurried toward him, believing he was Lillekort. But the older princess reached him first and hugged him tight and kissed him, and so she became Kong Lavring’s bride. And Lillekort married the youngest princess, the one he had saved first.

And then there were two weddings, with feasting and singing—so much that people said it was talked about across seven kingdoms.
