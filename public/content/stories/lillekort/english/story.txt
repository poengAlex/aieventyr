Once upon a time there was a pair of poor folk. They lived in a wretched little hut where there was nothing but black misery, so they had neither bite nor fire. But if they had almost nothing else, they had been given God’s blessing of children, and with each year there came one more. Now they were expecting yet another. The man was not glad of it; he went about grumbling and complaining, and said that once it seemed to him there could be enough of those gifts from God. And when the time came for his wife to give birth, he went off into the woods to fetch firewood, because he didn’t want to see the new squalling throat; he’d soon enough hear it screaming for food, he said.

When the man was well gone, the wife gave birth to a fine baby boy, and the moment he came into the world he looked about the room.

“Oh dear Mother,” said he, “give me some old clothes after my brothers and a couple of days’ provisions, and I’ll go out into the world and try my luck; you’ve children enough as it is, I see.”

“God preserve me for you, my son,” said his mother. “You are far too little yet; it can never do.”

But the little fellow stood by it and begged and pleaded, until at last his mother had to let him have some old rags and a bit of food tied up in a bundle, and off he went, cheerful and stout-hearted, out into the world.

But he had hardly left before the wife gave birth to a boy again; he too looked about and said, “Oh dear Mother, give me some old clothes after my brothers and a couple of days’ provisions, and I’ll go out into the world and find my twin brother again; you have children enough anyway.”

“God preserve me for you—poor little thing, you are far too small,” said the wife. “It can never do.”

But it was no use; the little one begged and pleaded so long that he too got some old rags and a bundle of food, and then he set off bravely into the world to find his twin brother again.

After the younger had walked a while, he caught sight of his brother some way ahead. He called out and begged him to stop. “Wait for me,” he said. “You’re hurrying along as though you were paid for it; but you ought to have seen your youngest brother too before you went out into the world.” So the elder stopped and looked back; and when the younger came up and told him how it was that he could be his brother, he went on: “But now let us sit down and look at what Mother gave us for provisions,” and so they did.

When they had walked a bit farther, they came to a brook running through a green meadow, and there the younger said they ought to give each other names. “Since we had to hurry so much we didn’t have time to do it at home, we’ll do it here,” said he.

“What will you be called then?” asked the elder.

“I’ll be called Littlecourt,” answered the other. “And you—what will you be called?”

“I’ll be called King Lavring,” said the elder.

So they named each other and went on. But after they had walked a while, they came to a crossroads, and there they agreed to part and each take his own way. They did so; but they had scarcely gone a little while before they met again. Then they parted anew and took separate paths; but after a short while it happened just the same—they met again before they knew it. And so it went the third time too. Then they made this bargain: they would go to opposite ends, one east and the other west.

“But if ever you truly fall into need and misfortune,” said the elder, “then call my name three times, and I’ll come and help you; but you must not call on me until you are in your utmost need!”

“It won’t be so soon we meet again, then,” said Littlecourt.

So they said farewell, and Littlecourt went east and King Lavring west.

Now when Littlecourt had gone a good while alone, he met an old, old bent-backed hag who had only one eye. Littlecourt stole it.

“Ow, ow!” cried the hag. “Where has my eye gone?”

“What will you give me to get an eye back?” said Littlecourt.

“I will give you a sword such that it can overcome a whole army, however great,” answered the woman.

“Yes, hand it over,” said Littlecourt.

So the hag gave him the sword, and then she got her eye back.

Then Littlecourt went on, and after he had walked a while he met again an old, old bent-backed hag with only one eye. Littlecourt stole it before she knew it.

“Ow, ow! Where has my eye gone?” cried the hag.

“What will you give me to get an eye back?” said Littlecourt.

“I will give you a ship that can travel fresh water and salt water, over mountains and deep valleys,” said the hag.

“Yes, hand it over,” said Littlecourt.

The woman gave him a tiny little ship, no bigger than that he could slip it into his pocket. Then the woman got her eye again, and each went his way.

After he had wandered a long while, for the third time he met an old, old bent-backed hag with only one eye. Littlecourt stole it again, and when the hag shrieked and carried on and asked where her eye had gone, Littlecourt said, “What will you give me to get an eye back?”

“I will give you the art of brewing a hundred lasts of malt in a single brewing.”

So for that art the hag got her eye again, and they went each their way.

But when Littlecourt had gone a little distance, it struck him that it might be worth trying the ship. So he took it out of his pocket and put one foot into it, and then the other; and no sooner had he stepped in with one leg than the ship became much larger, and when he stepped in with the other it grew as big as the ships that sail the sea. Littlecourt said, “Now go fresh water and salt water, mountains and deep valleys, and do not stop before you reach the king’s manor!” And at once the ship shot off as fast as a bird in the air, until it came to a stop a little below the king’s manor.

In the windows of the king’s manor they had stood watching Littlecourt come sailing; and they were all so astonished that they ran down to see who it could be who came flying through the air in a ship. But while they ran down, Littlecourt had stepped out of his ship and slipped it back into his pocket; for the moment he stepped out of it, it became as small again as when he got it from the old woman. The folk from the manor saw nothing but a little ragged boy standing on the shore. The king asked where he was from, but the boy said he didn’t know; nor did he know how he had come there. But he begged so prettily to be given service at the king’s manor: if there was nothing else for him to do, he could carry wood and water for the kitchen girl, he said—and that he was allowed.

When Littlecourt came up to the king’s manor, he saw that everything there, inside and out, walls and roof, was draped in black. He asked the kitchen girl what it meant. “Oh, I can tell you that,” said the kitchen girl. “The king’s daughter was long ago promised to three trolls, and next Thursday evening the first will come and fetch her. Sir Red has sworn to save her, but God knows if he can do it. So you can well understand there is sorrow and distress here.”

When Thursday evening came, Sir Red led the princess down to the shore—for there she was to meet the troll—and he was to stay down there and guard her. But he did the troll little harm, I should think, for no sooner had the princess sat down on the edge of the sand than Sir Red crawled up into a great tree that stood there and hid himself as best he could among the branches. The princess wept and begged him with all her heart not to leave her, but Sir Red cared nothing for it. “Better that one lose his life than two,” said Sir Red.

Meanwhile Littlecourt begged the kitchen girl so nicely if he might be allowed to go down to the shore a bit.

“Oh, what would you do there?” said the kitchen girl. “You have nothing to do there.”

“Oh yes, dear heart, let me go,” said Littlecourt. “I’d so like to go down and amuse myself a little with the other boys, I would.”

“Well, well—go then,” said the kitchen girl. “But mind you’re not there longer than until the evening pot is to be hung up and the roast set on the spit; and then bring back a good armful of wood into the kitchen.”

Littlecourt promised, and ran down to the shore.

The moment he came to where the king’s daughter sat, the troll came rushing so that it whistled and roared all around; it was so huge and monstrous it was dreadful, and it had five heads.

“Fire!” screamed the troll.

“Fire again!” said Littlecourt.

“Can you fence?” shouted the troll.

“If I can’t, I can learn,” said Littlecourt.

Then the troll struck at him with a great thick iron bar it held in its fist, so that the earth flew five ells into the air.

“Pah!” said Littlecourt. “That was something, that was! Now you shall see a stroke from me!”

And with that he grabbed the sword he had got from the old bent-backed hag and cut at the troll, so that all five heads flew away over the sand.

When the princess saw she was saved, she was so glad she didn’t know what to do with herself; she both hopped and danced. “Now sleep a little while on my lap,” she said to Littlecourt, and while he lay there she drew a golden garment over him.

But it wasn’t long before Sir Red crawled down from the tree again, when he saw there was no more danger about. He threatened the princess until she had to promise she would say that he had saved her—for if she didn’t say so, he would take her life. Then he cut out the troll’s lungs and tongues and put them in his handkerchief, and led the princess back to the king’s manor; and all the honor that had not been shown him before was shown now. The king did not know what to do to reward him, and always he sat at the king’s right hand at the table.

Littlecourt first went out to the troll’s ship and took a great heap of gold and silver girdles with him, and then he scampered back to the king’s manor. When the kitchen girl saw all the gold and silver, she was quite frightened and asked, “But dear Littlecourt, where did you get all that?” for she feared he had not got it in an honest way. “Oh,” said Littlecourt, “I was home a bit, and there these girdles had fallen off some wagons, and so I brought them along to you, I did.” Well, when the kitchen girl heard they were for her, she asked no more about it; she thanked Littlecourt, and all was well again.

The second Thursday evening it went just the same. Everyone was in sorrow and distress; but Sir Red said that if he had been able to save the king’s daughter from one troll, he could surely save her from another, and led her down to the shore. But he did the troll little harm, he did not, for when the time came they might expect it, he said as before, “Better that one lose his life than two,” and crawled up into the tree again.

Littlecourt begged the cook again for leave to go down to the shore a bit.

“Oh, what do you want there for?” said the kitchen girl.

“Please, dear, let me go,” said Littlecourt. “I’d so like to go down and amuse myself a little with the other boys.”

So he was allowed to go that time too; but first he had to promise he would be back when the roast was to be turned, and then he must have a big armful of wood with him.

Littlecourt had hardly reached the shore before the troll came rushing so that it whistled and roared all around; it was twice as big as the first troll, and it had ten heads.

“Fire!” screamed the troll.

“Fire again!” said Littlecourt.

“Can you fence?” shouted the troll.

“If I can’t, I can learn!” said Littlecourt.

Then the troll struck at him with its iron bar—it was even bigger than the first troll’s—so that the earth flew ten ells into the air.

“Pah!” said Littlecourt. “That was something, that was! Now you shall see a stroke from me!”

And with that he seized the sword and cut at the troll, so that all ten heads danced away over the sand.

Then the king’s daughter said to him again, “Sleep a little while on my lap,” and while Littlecourt lay there she drew a silver garment over him.

As soon as Sir Red sensed there was no longer any danger, he crawled down from the tree and threatened the princess until she again had to promise to say it was he who had saved her. Then he took the troll’s tongues and lungs and put them in his handkerchief, and led the king’s daughter back to the castle. There was joy and gladness, you may be sure, and the king did not know what to do to show Sir Red honor and glory enough.

But Littlecourt took with him an armful of gold and silver girdles from the troll’s ship. When he came back to the manor, the kitchen girl clasped her hands and marveled at where he had got all that gold and silver; but Littlecourt answered that he had been home a bit, and they were girdles that had fallen off some wagons, and he had brought them to the kitchen girl.

Now when the third Thursday evening came, it went exactly the same as the first two times: the whole manor was draped in black, and everyone was in sorrow and distress. But Sir Red said he didn’t think they had so much to fear; if he had saved the king’s daughter from two trolls, he could surely save her from the third as well. So he led her down to the shore; but when it drew near the time the troll was to come, he crawled up into the tree and hid again. The princess wept and begged him to stay, but it did no good; he stuck to the old tune: “Better that one lose his life than two,” said Sir Red again.

That evening too Littlecourt begged leave to go down to the shore. “Oh, what do you want there for?” said the kitchen girl. But he begged so long that at last he was allowed to go, only he had to promise he would be back in the kitchen again when the roast was to be turned.

He had hardly reached the shore before the troll came so that it whistled and roared; it was far, far bigger than any of the others, and it had fifteen heads.

“Fire!” shouted the troll.

“Fire again!” said Littlecourt.

“Can you fence?” screamed the troll.

“If I can’t, I can learn,” said Littlecourt.

“I’ll teach you!” screamed the troll, and struck at him with its iron bar, so that the earth flew fifteen ells into the air.

“Pah!” said Littlecourt. “That was something, that was! Now you shall see a stroke from me!”

At once he seized the sword and cut at the troll, so that all fifteen heads danced away over the sand.

Then the princess was saved, and she both thanked and blessed Littlecourt for rescuing her.

“Now sleep a little while on my lap,” she said, and while he lay there she drew a garment of brass over him.

“But how shall we reveal that it is you who saved me?” said the king’s daughter.

“I’ll tell you,” said Littlecourt. “When Sir Red has led you home again and claims to be the one who saved you, you know he is to have you and half the kingdom. But when, on the wedding day, they ask you whom you want to pour the toasts for you, you must say: ‘I want the little boy who is in the kitchen, who carries wood and water for the kitchen girl.’ As I pour, I will spill a drop on his plate but none on yours, and then he will get angry and strike me, and we will do that three times. But the third time you must say: ‘Shame on you, striking my dearest! He saved me, and he is the one I will have!’”

Then Littlecourt sprang back to the manor as he had the other times; but first he went out to the troll’s ship again and took a great deal of gold and silver and other precious things, and of that he gave the cook another whole armful of gold and silver girdles.

No sooner had Sir Red seen that all danger was past than he crawled down from the tree and threatened the king’s daughter until she promised she would say that he had saved her. Then he led her back to the manor; and if they had not honored him enough before, they honored him now. The king thought of nothing but how he might reward the man who had saved his daughter from the three trolls; it was settled that he should have both her and half the kingdom, the king said.

But on the wedding day the princess asked that she might have the little boy who was in the kitchen, carrying wood and water for the kitchen girl, to pour the toasts at the bridal table. “Oh, what do you want with that black ragged brat in here?” said Sir Red. But the princess said she wanted him to pour and no other, and at last she got her way. Then everything went just as Littlecourt and the king’s daughter had agreed: he spilled a drop on Sir Red’s plate but none on hers, and each time Sir Red got angry and struck him. At the first blow Littlecourt’s ragged kitchen clothes fell away; at the second blow the brass garment fell off; at the third the silver garment dropped, and there he stood in the gold garment, so bright and fine that he shone.

Then the king’s daughter said, “Shame on you, striking my dearest! He saved me, and he is the one I will have!”

Sir Red swore and cursed that he had rescued her; but then the king said, “He who has saved my daughter surely has something to show for it.” Sir Red ran at once to fetch his handkerchief with the trolls’ lungs and tongues, and Littlecourt fetched all the gold and silver and all the precious things he had taken from the troll ship; and so they each laid their proofs before the king.

“He who has such costly things of gold and silver and gemstones,” said the king, “must have slain the troll, for such things are not to be had from anyone else.” And so Sir Red was thrown into the snake pit, and Littlecourt was to have the princess and half the kingdom.

One day the king and Littlecourt were out walking, and Littlecourt asked the king whether he had had any more children.

“Yes,” said the king. “I had another daughter; but the troll took her, because there was no one who could save her. Now you shall have my one daughter; but if you can save the one the troll took, you may gladly have her and the other half of the kingdom too.”

“I’ll have to try,” said Littlecourt. “But I must have an iron cable five hundred ells long, and I want five hundred men with me, and provisions for them for fifteen weeks—for I am going far out to sea.”

Yes, he should have it; but the king was afraid he did not have a ship large enough to carry it all.

“I have a ship myself,” said Littlecourt, and took the one he had got from the old hag out of his pocket.

The king laughed and thought it was only a jest; but Littlecourt only asked for what he had demanded, and said the king would soon see.

So they brought everything, and Littlecourt asked them to lay the cable in the ship first. But no one could manage to lift it, and many couldn’t even get room around the tiny ship at once. Then Littlecourt himself took hold of one end of the cable and laid a few lengths into the ship; and as he tossed the cable in, the ship grew larger and larger, until at last it was so big that the cable and the five hundred men and the provisions and Littlecourt all had plenty of room. “Now go fresh water and salt water, mountains and deep valleys, and do not stop until you come to where the king’s daughter is,” said Littlecourt to the ship; and at once it shot off so that it screamed and whistled, over land and water.

When they had sailed far, far away like that, the ship stopped in the middle of the sea. “Well, now we have come here,” said Littlecourt, “but another matter it is how we are to get away again.” Then he took the iron cable and tied one end around his waist. “Now I must go to the bottom,” said he, “but when I pull hard on the cable and want to come up again, you must haul, all as one man—otherwise there’s no more life to think of for you than for me.” And with that he leapt into the water, so that the yellow spray stood around him. He sank and sank, and at last he reached the bottom. There he saw a great mountain with a door in it, and he went in. When he came inside, he found the other princess; she sat sewing, but when she saw Littlecourt she clasped her hands. “Oh, thanks be to God!” she cried. “I have not seen a Christian man since I came here.”

“Yes, I’ve come for you,” said Littlecourt.

“Oh, you won’t get me,” said the king’s daughter. “It will never do for you to think it. If the troll sees you, he will take your life.”

“So it was him you were talking about,” said Littlecourt. “Where is he? It would be amusing to see him.”

Then the princess told him the troll was out, trying to catch someone who could brew a hundred lasts of malt in one brewing; for the troll was to hold a feast, and nothing less would do.

“I can do that,” said Littlecourt.

“If only the troll were not so hasty-tempered, so I could tell him,” said the princess. “But he is so ill-natured that I fear he will tear you to pieces the moment he comes in. Still, I must try to think of something. You can hide in that little closet there, and we’ll see how it goes.”

So Littlecourt did, and he had scarcely crawled into the closet and hidden himself before the troll came in.

“Ugh! It smells of Christian man’s blood here,” said the troll.

“Yes, a bird flew over the roof with a Christian man’s bone in its beak, and it dropped it down the chimney,” said the princess. “I hurried to get it away, but it must be that you smell all the same.”

“Yes, that must be it,” said the troll.

Then the princess asked whether he had found anyone who could brew a hundred lasts of malt in one brewing.

“No, there is no one who can do it,” said the troll.

“A little while ago there was one in here who said he could,” said the princess.

“You’re always so clever, you are,” said the troll. “Why did you let him go then? You knew I wanted such a one.”

“Oh, I didn’t let him go either,” said the princess. “But Father is so hasty-tempered, so I hid him in the closet. If Father hasn’t found anyone, he’s surely here.”

“Let him come in,” said the troll.

When Littlecourt came out, the troll asked whether it was true he could brew a hundred lasts of malt in one brewing.

“Yes,” said Littlecourt.

“Then it’s a good thing I caught you,” said the troll. “Set to work at once—and God help you if you don’t brew the ale strong!”

“Oh, it shall have flavor enough,” said Littlecourt, and he set about brewing at once. “But I must have more trolls to carry brewing-lids,” said Littlecourt. “The ones I have got can’t manage anything.”

So he got more—so many that they swarmed—and so the brewing went on.

When the wort was ready, they all had to taste, of course—first the troll itself, and then the others. But Littlecourt had brewed the wort so strong that they fell dead like flies as they drank. At last there was none left but a miserable old hag lying behind the oven. “Oh, you poor thing,” said Littlecourt. “You shall taste the wort too!” Then he went and scraped the bottom of the brewing vat with the ladle and gave it to her, and so he was rid of them all.

As he stood looking about, his eye fell on a great chest. Littlecourt took it and filled it with gold and silver, then tied the cable around himself, the princess, and the chest, and gave a mighty tug; and the men hauled them up safe and sound.

When Littlecourt had come up onto the ship, he said, “Now go salt water and fresh water, mountains and deep valleys, and do not stop before you reach the king’s manor,” and at once the ship shot off so that the yellow foam stood around it.

When they at the king’s manor saw the ship, they were quick to come out with song and music and receive Littlecourt with great joy; but the gladest of all was the king, who had now got back his other daughter too. Yet Littlecourt was not at ease, for both princesses wanted him, and he wanted no one but the one he had first saved—the younger one. So he often went about thinking how he might manage to get her, for he was loath to wrong the other as well.

One day, as he went pondering this back and forth, it came to him that if only he had his brother with him, King Lavring—so like him that no one could tell them apart—then King Lavring could have the other princess and half the kingdom, for Littlecourt himself felt he had enough with one half. No sooner had the thought come than he went outside the castle and called for King Lavring. No one came. He called again, louder, but still no one came. Then Littlecourt called a third time, with all his might—and there stood his brother.

“I said you mustn’t call on me unless you were in your utmost need,” said he to Littlecourt, “and here there isn’t even a gnat that can hurt you.”

With that he slapped him, so that Littlecourt rolled away across the meadow.

“Oh, shame on you for striking!” said Littlecourt. “First I won the one king’s daughter and half the kingdom, and then the other king’s daughter and the other half, and now I meant to give you one princess and half the kingdom with me—do you think it’s fair to strike me like that?”

When King Lavring heard that, he begged his brother’s forgiveness, and at once they were good friends again.

“Now you know,” said Littlecourt, “we are so alike that no one can tell one from the other. Exchange clothes with me and go up to the castle, and the princesses will think it is I who come in. Whichever one kisses you first, you shall have; I’ll take the other.” For he knew the elder princess was the strongest, and could well guess how it would go.

King Lavring was willing at once. He swapped clothes with his brother and went into the castle. When he came into the princesses’ chamber, they thought it was Littlecourt, and both sprang at once toward him; but the elder, who was bigger and stronger, shoved her sister aside, threw her arms around King Lavring’s neck, and kissed him. So he got her, and Littlecourt got the younger king’s daughter. And then there were weddings—such weddings that they were heard of and asked about across seven kingdoms.
