Once upon a time there was a pair of poor folk who lived in a wretched little hut far away in the forest. They barely had enough to live on—hand to mouth, and scarce even that. But they had three sons, and the youngest was Ash-Lad, for he did nothing but lie and poke about in the ashes.

One day the oldest boy said he wanted to go out into the world and hire himself out for service. He got permission at once and set off. He walked and walked all day, and when evening drew on he came to a king’s manor. The king was standing out on the steps and asked where he was bound.

“Oh, I’m only out looking for a place, sir,” said the boy.

“Will you serve me and herd my seven colts?” the king asked. “If you can herd them for a whole day and tell me in the evening what they eat and drink, you shall have the princess and half the kingdom. But if you cannot, I’ll cut three red straps from your back.”

The boy thought it sounded easy enough; he would surely manage it.

In the morning, at first light, the master of the stables let out the seven colts. Off they sprang, and the boy after them; and away it went over hills and through valleys, through thickets and bogs. After running a good while the boy began to tire, and after still more he was heartily sick of the herding. Just then he came to a cleft in the mountain, where an old hag sat spinning on a distaff. When she saw the boy running after the colts, sweat streaming off him, she cried, “Come here, come here, my dear son—shall I comb your hair for you?”

The boy was only too glad. He sat down in the mountain cleft with the old hag, laid his head in her lap, and she combed and soothed him all day long while he lay there and enjoyed himself.

Toward evening the boy got up to go. “I may as well stroll straight home,” he said, “for there’s no use going back to the king’s manor.”

“Wait a bit till dusk,” said the hag. “Then the colts will come past here on their way back to the king. You can run home with them. No one will know you’ve been lying here all day instead of herding them.”

When the colts came, she gave the boy a water flask and a clump of moss. He was to show it to the king and say that was what the seven colts ate and drank.

“Have you herded faithfully and well all day?” the king asked when the boy appeared before him that evening.

“Yes, I have,” said the boy.

“Then you can tell me what my seven colts eat and drink?” said the king.

The boy held out the water flask and the clump of moss the hag had given him. “There’s their food, and there’s their drink,” he said.

But the king understood at once how the boy had done his herding. He grew so furious he ordered them to drive the boy away at once—but first they were to cut three red straps from his back and rub salt into the wounds.

When the boy came home you can imagine his state. Once he had gone out to seek service, he said, but he would never do it again.

The next day the second son said he wanted to go out into the world and try his luck. His parents said no and begged him to look at his brother’s back, but the boy would not give in. At last he got permission and set out. After walking all day he too reached the king’s manor. The king was standing on the steps and asked where he was bound, and when the boy said he was looking for service, the king told him he could serve as herdsman for his seven colts—and he set the same reward and the same punishment as he had for the brother. The boy agreed at once; he would watch the colts well and tell the king what they ate and drank, he thought.

At grey dawn the master of the stables let out the seven colts again. Off they went over hill and dale, and the boy after them. But it went with him just as it had with his brother. After running a long, long while until he was sweaty and worn out, he passed the mountain cleft where the old hag sat spinning on her distaff, and she called to him, “Come here, come here, my dear son—shall I comb your hair for you?”

The boy liked the sound of that. He let the colts run their own way and sat down in the cleft with the hag. There he sat and there he lay, and being combed and coddled was what he did all day long.

When the colts came back toward evening, he too received a clump of moss and a water flask from the hag to show the king. But when the king asked, “Can you tell me what my seven colts eat and drink?” and the boy held out the moss and the water and said, “There’s their food, and there’s their drink,” the king grew furious again. He ordered them to cut three red straps from the boy’s back, rub salt into them, and drive him home at once. When the boy returned he told how it had gone, and said that once he had gone out to seek service, but surely he would never do so again.

On the third day Ash-Lad wanted to set off. He had a mind to try herding the seven colts too, he said.

The others laughed and mocked him. “If it went like that for us, you’ll surely manage it—yes, you look the sort, you do, who’s never done anything but lie in the ashes and poke around!” they said.

“I’m going anyway,” said Ash-Lad. “I’ve got it into my head.” And though the others laughed and his parents begged, it was no use. Ash-Lad set out.

After walking all day he reached the king’s manor at dusk. The king stood on the steps and asked where he was bound.

“I’m out looking for service,” said Ash-Lad.

“And where are you from?” the king asked, for now he wanted to know more before he took anyone on.

Ash-Lad told him, and said he was brother to the two who had herded the king’s seven colts, and he asked if he might have leave to try the next day.

“Oh, nonsense,” said the king—he grew angry just remembering the others—“if you’re brother to those two, you’re likely good for little as well. I’ve had enough of the likes of you!”

“Yes, but since I’ve come all this way, I might as well be allowed to try too,” said Ash-Lad.

“Oh, if you’re set on having your back flayed, that’s fine by me,” said the king.

“I’d rather have the princess,” said Ash-Lad.

In the morning at grey dawn the master of the stables let out the seven colts again, and off they went over hill and dale, through thickets and bogs, with Ash-Lad behind them.

After he had run a good while he too came to the mountain cleft. There sat the old hag again, spinning on her distaff, and she called to Ash-Lad, “Come here, come here, my dear son—shall I comb your hair for you?”

“Kiss me behind, kiss me behind!” said Ash-Lad, and he hopped and sprang and held fast to a colt’s rump.

When he had passed the cleft, the youngest colt said, “Climb onto my back, for we’ve a long way yet,” and Ash-Lad did so.

They traveled a long, long distance. “Do you see anything now?” asked the colt.

“No,” said Ash-Lad.

They traveled a good way farther.

“Do you see anything now?” asked the colt.

“Oh, no,” said Ash-Lad.

After they had traveled a long, long distance again, the colt asked once more, “Do you see anything now?”

“Yes,” said Ash-Lad, “now I think I see something whitening. It looks like a great big birch stump.”

“Yes, that’s where we’re going in,” said the colt.

When they came to the stump, the oldest colt shoved it aside, and there was a door where the stump had stood. Inside was a little room, and in the room there was hardly anything but a small hearth and a couple of stools; but behind the door hung a great rusty sword and a small jar.

“Can you swing the sword?” asked the colt.

Ash-Lad tried, but he could not. So he took a draught from the jar—first once, then again, then yet again—and then he could handle the sword as if it were nothing.

“Now you must take the sword with you,” said the colt. “With it you are to cut off the heads of all seven of us on your wedding day, and then we shall be princes again as we were before. For we are brothers of the princess you shall have, if you can tell the king what we eat and drink; but a dreadful troll has cast a spell upon us. When you have cut off our heads, take great care to lay each head by the tail of the body it belonged to, where it used to sit—then the witchcraft will have no more power over us.”

Ash-Lad promised, and they traveled on.

After they had gone a long, long way, the colt asked, “Do you see anything?”

“No,” said Ash-Lad.

They went on a while longer. “And now?” asked the colt. “Do you see anything now?”

“Oh, no,” said Ash-Lad.

Then they traveled many, many miles over hills and valleys.

“Now then,” said the colt, “don’t you see anything yet?”

“Yes,” said Ash-Lad, “now I see something like a strip that’s turning blue far, far away.”

“Yes, that’s a river,” said the colt. “We must cross it.”

Across the river ran a long fine bridge, and when they had come to the other side they traveled another long, long stretch. Then the colt asked again whether Ash-Lad saw anything.

Yes—this time he saw something darkening far away, like a church tower.

“Yes, we’re going in there too,” said the colt.

When the colts came into the churchyard, they became people again and looked like king’s sons, dressed so splendidly that they shone. They went into the church, and there the priest standing at the altar gave them bread and wine. Ash-Lad went in too; and when the priest had laid his hands on the princes and spoken the blessing, they went out of the church again, and Ash-Lad went out as well—but he took with him a bottle of wine and a communion wafer.

The very moment the seven king’s sons came out into the churchyard they became colts again. Ash-Lad climbed onto the youngest one’s back, and then they raced back the same way they had come, only much, much faster than before. First they went over the bridge, then past the stump, and then past the hag who sat in the mountain cleft and spun. They went so fast that Ash-Lad could not make out what the hag shrieked after him, but he heard enough to know she was fearfully angry.

It was nearly dark when they returned to the king’s manor that evening, and the king himself stood in the yard waiting for them.

“Have you herded faithfully and well all day?” the king asked Ash-Lad.

“I’ve done my best,” Ash-Lad answered.

“Then you can tell me what my seven colts eat and drink?” said the king.

Ash-Lad brought out the communion wafer and the bottle of wine and showed them to the king. “There’s their food, and there’s their drink,” he said.

“Yes, you have herded faithfully and well,” said the king, “and you shall have the princess and half the kingdom.”

So preparations were made for a wedding, and it was to be so grand and splendid that folk would hear of it and talk of it, said the king. But while they sat at the wedding feast, the bridegroom rose and went down to the stable, for he had forgotten something there and must fetch it, he said. When he came down he did just as the colts had told him. He cut off the heads of all seven—first the oldest, then the others in order of age—and he took care to lay each head by the tail of the colt it had belonged to. As he did so they became princes again. When he came back into the hall with the seven princes, the king was so glad he both kissed and clapped Ash-Lad; and his bride was gladder in him than ever before.

“Half the kingdom you have now,” said the king, “and the other half you shall have after my death; for my sons can win land and realm for themselves now that they have become princes again.” And it may well be there was joy and merriment at that wedding.

I was there too; but no one had time to remember me. All I got was a buttered slice of bread. I laid it on the stove, and the bread burned and the butter ran, and never again did I get so much as a crumb.
