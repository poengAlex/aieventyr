Once there was a Queen who had gone out riding in a sleigh. It was winter, and fresh snow had just fallen. When she had gone a little way along the road, her nose began to bleed, and she had to step down from the sleigh. As she stood by the fence looking at the red blood on the white snow, she thought of how she had twelve sons and not a single daughter, and she said to herself, “If only I had a daughter as white as snow and as red as blood, then I could well do without my sons.”

She had scarcely said it before a troll-wife came to her. “A daughter you shall have,” said the troll-wife, “and she shall be as white as snow and as red as blood—but your sons shall be mine. Still, you may keep them with you until the child is christened.”

When the time came, the Queen bore a daughter, and she was as white as snow and as red as blood, just as the troll-wife had promised. So they named her Snow-White and Rose-Red. There was great rejoicing in the king’s hall, and the Queen was so glad there was no measure to it; but when she remembered what she had promised the troll-witch, she had a silversmith make twelve silver spoons, one for each prince, and then she had him make one more, and that she gave to Snow-White and Rose-Red.

As soon as the princess was christened, the princes were transformed into twelve wild ducks and flew away, and they never saw them again; they were gone, and gone they stayed.

The princess grew up, and she became both tall and beautiful; yet she was often strangely sad, and no one could understand what ailed her.

But one evening the Queen too was sorrowful, for she had many strange thoughts when she remembered her sons. Then she said to Snow-White and Rose-Red, “Why are you so sad, my child? If anything is wrong, tell me. If there is something you want, you shall have it.”

“Oh, it feels so lonely,” said Snow-White and Rose-Red. “Everyone else has brothers and sisters, but I am all alone; I have none. That is what I grieve for.”

“You have had brothers too, my child,” said the Queen. “I had twelve sons, who were your brothers—but I gave them away in order to have you,” she said, and then she told her the whole tale.

When the princess heard it, she could not rest. No matter how the Queen wept and pleaded, it did no good—she would go, she had to go, to search for her brothers, for she felt it was all her fault. And at last she left the king’s hall. She walked and walked, far out into the wide world—so far you would scarcely believe so fine a maiden could have endured it.

Once she had walked for a long time in a great, great forest. One day she grew tired and sat down on a mound, and there she fell asleep. Then she dreamed she went deeper into the forest to a little timbered hut, and there were her brothers. At that she woke, and straight before her she saw a trodden path through the green moss, and the path led deeper into the woods. She followed it, and at last she came to just such a little log house as she had dreamed of.

When she went into the room, there was no one inside; but there stood twelve beds, and twelve chairs, and twelve spoons—twelve of everything that was there. When she saw it, she grew so glad as she had not been in many years, for at once she understood her brothers lived there, and that the beds and chairs and spoons belonged to them. She set to making a fire, and sweeping, and making the beds, and cooking food, and putting everything as neat and pretty as she could. And when she had cooked and laid it out for them all, she ate herself—but she forgot to remove her own spoon from the table, and then she crept in under the youngest brother’s bed and lay there.

She had scarcely lain down before she heard a rushing and whistling in the air, and then all twelve wild ducks came darting in. But the moment they crossed the threshold, they turned at once into princes.

“Oh, how good and warm it is here!” they said. “God bless whoever has made the fire and cooked such good food for us!” And then each took his silver spoon and was about to eat.

But when each had taken his, there was still one spoon left lying there, and it was so like the others they could not tell it apart. They looked at one another and wondered. “That is our sister’s spoon,” they said, “and if her spoon is here, she cannot be far away.”

“If it is our sister’s spoon, and she is here, then she must be killed,” said the eldest prince, “for she is the cause of all the evil we suffer.” And under the bed she lay and heard it.

“No,” said the youngest, “it would be a sin to kill her for that. She cannot help that we suffer; if anyone is to blame, it must be our own mother.”

So they began to search for her, high and low, and at last they searched under all the beds too. When they came to the youngest prince’s bed, they found her and dragged her out.

Again the eldest prince said she must be killed, but she wept and begged so prettily for her life. “Oh, dear God, do not kill me,” she said. “I have walked for many years searching for you, and if I could save you, I would gladly give my life.”

“Yes, if you will save us,” they said, “then you shall live—for if you truly wished to, you could.”

“Only tell me how it may be done, and I will do whatever it is,” said the princess.

“You must gather bog-cotton,” said the princes, “and you must card it and spin it and weave cloth from it; and when you have done that, you must cut and sew twelve caps, twelve shirts, and twelve pieces of cloth from it, one for each of us. And while you do this, you must neither speak nor laugh nor weep. If you can do that, then we are saved.”

“But where shall I find bog-cotton enough for so many pieces and caps and shirts?” said Snow-White and Rose-Red.

“We will show you,” said the princes, and they took her out to a great, great bog where bog-cotton grew so thick, swaying in the wind and glinting in the sun, that it shone like snow a long way off.

Never had the princess seen so much bog-cotton before. She began at once to pick and gather the best she could, and when she came home in the evening, she set to carding and spinning thread from it.

So it went on, well and long. She gathered bog-cotton and carded it, and in between she cared for the princes: she cooked and made their beds. In the evening they came rushing and whirring home as wild ducks; at night they were princes, but in the morning they flew off again and were wild ducks all day.

But then it happened once, as she was out on the bog to gather bog-cotton—and if I am not mistaken it was likely the last time she needed to go—that the young king who ruled the land was out hunting and came riding up to the bog and caught sight of her. He stopped and wondered who the beautiful maiden could be who walked there gathering bog-cotton, and he asked her as well. When he got no answer to what he asked, he wondered all the more, and he took such a liking to her that he wanted to bring her home to the palace and marry her.

So he told his servants to take her and set her on his horse. Snow-White and Rose-Red wrung her hands and made signs and pointed to the sacks that held all her work. When the king understood she wanted them brought too, he told his servants to load the sacks as well. When they had done so, the princess resigned herself little by little, for the king was both a fine man and a handsome man, and he was as gentle and kind to her as if she were a precious toy.

But when they came home to the palace, and the old Queen, who was his stepmother, set eyes on Snow-White and Rose-Red, she grew so angry and jealous at her beauty that she said to the king, “Can you not see that the one you have brought home and mean to marry is a witch? She neither speaks nor laughs nor weeps.”

The king paid no heed to what she said, but held a wedding and married Snow-White and Rose-Red, and they lived in great joy and splendor; yet she did not forget to sew the shirts.

Before the year was out, Snow-White and Rose-Red had a little prince, and that made the old Queen even more angry and jealous. When night had deepened, she crept into Snow-White and Rose-Red’s room while she slept, took the child and threw it into the snake-yard; then she cut the queen’s finger and smeared the blood around her mouth, and went to the king.

“Come and see now,” she said, “what kind of woman you have taken for your queen. Now she has eaten her own child.”

The king was so sick at heart he was near to weeping, and he said, “It must be true, since I see it with my own eyes; but surely she will not do it again. This time I will spare her.”

Before the year was out, she bore a son again, and with him it went exactly as with the first. The king’s stepmother grew still more wicked and jealous. Again she crept in at night while the queen slept, took the child and threw it into the snake-yard, cut the queen’s finger and smeared blood about her mouth, and then told the king the queen had eaten that child too. Then the king was so grieved you could never believe it, and he said, “It must be true, since I see it with my own eyes; but surely she will not do it again. I will spare her this time too.”

Before the year was out, Snow-White and Rose-Red gave birth to a daughter, and this child too the old Queen took and threw into the snake-yard. While the young queen slept, she cut her finger, smeared the blood around her mouth, and went to the king and said, “Now you may come and see whether it is not as I say—that she is a troll-witch. For now she has eaten her third child as well.”

Then such sorrow fell on the king there was no measure to it; for now he could not spare her any longer, but had to command that she be burned alive on a pyre.

When the pyre was blazing and they were to set her upon it, she made signs that they should take twelve boards and lay them around the fire. On these boards she laid the pieces of cloth and the caps and the shirts for her brothers; but in the youngest brother’s shirt the left sleeve was missing—she had not been able to finish it in time.

She had never done this before, when she heard a rushing and roaring in the air, and then twelve wild ducks came flying over the forest, and each took its clothing in its beak and flew off with it.

“Do you see now?” the wicked Queen said to the king. “Now you can truly see she is a witch. Hurry and burn her before the wood burns down.”

“Oh,” said the king, “we have wood enough—we have a whole forest to take from. I will wait a little longer, for I would like to see how this ends.”

At that very moment the twelve princes came riding up, as handsome and well-grown as anyone could wish to see; but the youngest prince had a duck’s wing instead of his left arm.

“What is going on here?” the princes asked.

“My queen is to be burned, because she is a witch and has eaten her own children,” said the king.

“She has not eaten her children,” said the princes. “Speak now, sister! You have saved us—save yourself now as well!”

Then Snow-White and Rose-Red spoke, and told how it had all happened: how each time she had lain in childbed, the old Queen—the king’s stepmother—had crept in at night, taken the child from her, cut her finger, and smeared the blood around her mouth.

And the princes took the king and led him to the snake-yard. There lay the three children, playing with snakes and toads, and lovelier children you could not have set eyes upon.

The king took them up and carried them away to his stepmother, and asked her what punishment she thought fitting for one who could find it in her heart to betray an innocent queen and three such blessed children.

“She should be bound between twelve unbroken horses, so that each tears away its share,” said the old Queen.

“You have spoken your own sentence, and you shall suffer it yourself,” said the king. And so the old wicked Queen was bound between twelve unbroken horses, and each tore away its piece of her.

But Snow-White and Rose-Red took the king and their children and the twelve princes, and they journeyed home to her parents and told all that had happened. And there was great rejoicing throughout the whole kingdom, because the princess was saved—and had saved her twelve brothers as well.
