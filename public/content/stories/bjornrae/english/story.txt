One frosty morning the Bear met Reynard the Fox, who came slinking out of the trees with a bundle of fish clutched to his chest. He had stolen every one of them.

“Where did you get those?” asked the Bear.

“I’ve been out fishing, Mister Bear,” said the Fox, as innocent as you please.

The Bear reared up on his hind legs for a better look. The fish were fat and silver, and all at once he wanted to catch some of his own.

“Will you teach me how?” he asked.

“Oh, it’s an easy art for a fellow like you,” said the Fox. “You’ll have it learned in no time.”

“All you do is go out on the ice,” the Fox went on, “cut yourself a hole and stick your tail down into it. Then you hold it there. The longer the better.”

So the Bear went out on the frozen lake and chopped a hole in the ice, and he knelt down and lowered his tail into the black water. The Fox sat down a comfortable distance away to supervise.

It was bitterly cold. Frost crept into the Bear’s fur, and after a while his tail began to sting.

“It stings,” said the Bear.

“That’s the fish nibbling,” said the Fox.

The Bear gritted his teeth and held on. The stinging got worse.

“It stings a lot,” said the Bear.

“Then a lot of fish are nibbling,” said the Fox. “The longer you hold on, the more you’ll catch.”

So the Bear held on for a long, long time, until his tail was frozen fast.

“Now!” cried the Fox. “Pull it up, quick!”

The Bear gave a mighty heave. Snap! went his tail, clean off, and there it stayed in the ice. The Bear has gone about stumpy-tailed ever since.

You might think the Bear would have had enough of the Fox after that. But the Bear was not one to bear a grudge, and that winter the two of them bought a tub of butter together to have at Christmas. They hid it under a thick spruce bush. Then they strolled a little way off and lay down on a sunny slope to sleep.

They hadn’t been lying there long when the Fox pricked up his ears.

“Yes!” he called towards the trees, making out that somebody had called him, and off he ran. He went straight to the butter tub and ate a good third of it.

When he came back, his muzzle was shining with grease.

“Where have you been?” asked the Bear, half awake.

“Wouldn’t you know it? I was invited to a christening,” said the Fox.

“How nice,” said the Bear. “What did they call the baby?”

“Just-Begun,” said the Fox.

They settled down to sleep again. Before long the Fox pricked up his ears once more. “Yes!” he called, and away he went to the tub, and this time he ate a good big lump.

“Where were you this time?” asked the Bear.

“Would you believe it? Another christening!”

“And what did they call this one?”

“Half-Gone,” said the Fox.

The Bear thought that was an odd sort of name, but he hadn’t puzzled over it for long before he yawned and dropped off again. And it went just the same as before. Up jumped the Fox. “Yes!” he called, and off he raced to the tub, and this time he ate every last scrap.

“And the baby’s name?” mumbled the Bear when the Fox got back.

“Licked-Clean,” said the Fox.

They slept a good while longer. At last they got up to go and look at their butter, and the tub was empty.

“You ate it!” said the Bear.

“Me? You ate it!” said the Fox. “I’ve been lying here asleep the whole time.”

So it went, back and forth, each of them swearing the other had been at the tub while he himself lay sleeping.

“Well, well,” said Reynard at last. “We’ll soon find out which of us stole the butter. We’ll lie down in the sun again, and whoever has the greasiest backside when we wake up is the thief.”

The Bear was glad to agree. He knew he hadn’t had so much as a lick of that butter, so he lay down in the sun without a care and went straight to sleep.

Then Reynard crept back to the tub. A little scrap of butter was still stuck in a crack in the wood. He picked it out with his claw, tiptoed back to the Bear and smeared it on his backside. Then he lay down again and shut his eyes, the picture of innocence.

When they woke, the sun had melted the butter. So the Bear was the one who had eaten it after all.
