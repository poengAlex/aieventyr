Once upon a time there was a man called Gudbrand. His farm lay so far up a steep hillside that people called him Gudbrand-on-the-Slope.

He and his wife got along so well that whatever the man did, his wife thought it was the wisest thing he could have done. Nobody could have done it better. However he went about things, she was just as pleased.

They owned their land outright. At the bottom of the chest lay a hundred dalers, and in the barn stood two cows.

“Do you know what I’ve been thinking?” his wife said one day. “We should take one of the cows to town and sell her. We’re as good as anybody, and we ought to have a few coins to jingle, the way other people do. We can’t touch the hundred dalers in the chest. But what do we want with two cows? And we’d gain something besides, because I’d only have one to milk and water instead of two.”

Gudbrand thought that was both right and sensible. He took the cow and went straight to town to sell her. But in town nobody wanted to buy a cow.

Oh well, thought Gudbrand, then I’ll take her home again. She has her stall and her tether there, and it’s no farther back than it was coming. And off he ambled.

He hadn’t gone far when he met a man with a horse to sell. A horse is better than a cow, thought Gudbrand, and they traded.

A little farther on he met a man driving a fat pig along the road. A fat pig is better than a horse, thought Gudbrand, and he traded again.

Next came a man with a goat. A goat must be better than a pig, thought Gudbrand, and they swapped.

Then he walked a long way, until he met a man with a sheep. It’s always better to have a sheep than a goat, he thought, and traded again.

After a while longer he met a man with a goose, and the sheep went for the goose.

And when he had walked a long, long way, he met a man with a rooster under his arm. It’s always better to have a rooster than a goose, thought Gudbrand, and he traded with him too.

The animals kept getting smaller and the road kept getting longer. By late afternoon Gudbrand was hungry, so he sold the rooster for twelve shillings. He stood in the road for a while with the coins in his palm. It’s always better to save your life than to keep a rooster, thought Gudbrand-on-the-Slope, and he went and bought himself something to eat.

On the way home he came to the nearest neighbor’s farm, and stopped in.

“Well, how did it go in town?” the people there asked.

“Oh, so-so,” said Gudbrand. “I can’t praise my luck, and I can’t complain about it either.” And he told them the whole story from beginning to end.

“Well, you’re in for a fine welcome when you get home to your wife,” said the neighbor. “Heaven help you. I wouldn’t be in your shoes for anything.”

“I think it could have gone a lot worse,” said Gudbrand. “But good or bad, I’ve got such a kind wife that she never says a word, whatever I do.”

“Oh, I know that for a fact,” said the neighbor. “I just don’t believe it.”

“Want to bet?” said Gudbrand-on-the-Slope. “I’ve got a hundred dalers at the bottom of the chest at home. Do you dare put up the same?”

The neighbor dared, and they shook on it.

Gudbrand stayed there until evening. When it began to get dark, the two of them walked up to his farm together. A hundred dalers was a great deal of money, and the neighbor didn’t mean to miss a single word. He pressed himself against the wall beside the door, and Gudbrand went into the warm, lamplit room.

“Good evening,” said Gudbrand.

“Good evening,” said his wife. “Oh, thank heaven, there you are!”

And there he was.

Then she wanted to know how things had gone in town.

“Oh, so-so,” said Gudbrand. “Nothing to brag about. Nobody in town would buy the cow, so I traded her for a horse.”

“Well, thank you for that!” said his wife. “We’re as good as anybody. Why shouldn’t we drive to church like other people? If we can afford to keep a horse, we may as well have one. Run down and put the horse in, children!”

The children were already halfway to the door.

“Well, the thing is, I haven’t got the horse either,” said Gudbrand. “A bit farther on I traded it for a pig.”

The children sat down again.

“Why, that’s exactly what I’d have done myself!” cried his wife. “Thank you kindly! Now we’ll have bacon in the house, and something to offer people when they come calling. What would we want with a horse? People would only say we were too grand to walk to church now. Run down and put the pig in, children!”

This time the children looked at their father first.

“I haven’t got the pig either,” said Gudbrand. “A little way after that, I traded it for a milking goat.”

“Oh, how well you manage everything!” said his wife. “What would I want with a pig, now that I think about it? People would only say, ‘Look at them, eating up everything they own.’ No, with a goat I get milk and cheese, and I still have the goat. Let the goat in, children!”

“I haven’t got the goat either,” said Gudbrand. “Farther along the road I traded it for a fine sheep.”

“Why, you clever man!” cried his wife. “You’ve done it just the way I’d have wished, just the way I’d have done it if I’d been there myself! What would we want with a goat? I’d be scrambling up hill and down dale every evening to fetch it home. No, a sheep gives me wool and clothes for the house, and meat besides. Run down and let the sheep in, children!”

“I haven’t got the sheep anymore,” said Gudbrand. “After a while I traded it for a goose.”

“Thank you for that!” said his wife. “And thank you again! What would I want with a sheep? I haven’t even got a spinning wheel, and I don’t care to sit there spinning and weaving till my fingers ache. We can buy clothes, same as we always have. Now I’ll have roast goose, which I’ve been longing for, and down for my little pillow. Run down and let the goose in, children!”

“I haven’t got the goose either,” said Gudbrand. “A bit farther on I traded it for a rooster.”

“Heaven knows how you think of it all!” cried his wife. “It’s just what I’d have done myself. A rooster! Why, he’s as good as a clock on the wall, and he never needs winding. He’ll crow at four every morning, and we’ll be up in good time. What would we want with a goose? I don’t even know how to cook one, and I can stuff my pillow with sedge grass. Run out and let the rooster in, children!”

The children didn’t even look up.

“I haven’t got the rooster either,” said Gudbrand. “Farther on still, I got so hungry I had to sell it for twelve shillings, just to save my life.”

“Well, thank heaven you did!” she cried. “Whatever you do, you do it just the way I’d have wished. What would we want with a rooster? We’re our own masters. We can sleep in as long as we please! Thank heaven I’ve got you home again, you who manage everything so well. Then I need neither rooster nor goose, nor pigs, nor cows.”

Then Gudbrand swung the door wide open. There stood the neighbor in the dark, with his ear exactly where the door had been. Behind Gudbrand, his wife was still beaming.

“Have I won the hundred dalers now?” said Gudbrand-on-the-Slope.

And the neighbor had to admit that he had.
