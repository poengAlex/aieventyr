Once there was a farmer with three sons. He was poor, and old and worn out, and the sons would not put their hands to any work. A great fine forest belonged to the farm, and the father wanted the boys to go in there, cut timber, and earn something to pay down the debt.

At long last he got them moving, and the eldest was to go out and chop first. When he had gone deep into the wood and started swinging his axe at an old, bearded spruce, a huge, hulking troll came up to him.

“If you chop in my forest, I’ll kill you!” said the troll.

When the boy heard that, he flung down the axe and ran home as fast as he could. He came bursting in, out of breath, and told what had happened. But his father said he had a hare’s heart; trolls had never frightened him away from chopping when he was young, he said.

The next day the second son had to go, and it went exactly the same. As soon as he had struck a few blows into the spruce, the troll came to him too and said, “If you chop in my forest, I’ll kill you!”

The boy scarcely dared look at him. He threw down the axe and took to his heels like his brother—only faster. When he got home the father grew angry and said trolls had never scared him off when he was young.

On the third day Askeladden wanted to go.

“Yes, you,” said the two older brothers, “you’ll manage it, you who’ve never been out the door!”

Askeladden didn’t answer them. He only asked to be given a good stout packed meal. The mother had no butter, so she scraped the pot to coax out a little for him; he got that in his knapsack and set off.

He had been chopping only a little while when the troll came to him as well and said, “If you chop in my forest, I’ll kill you!”

But the boy was quick. He sprang aside into the trees for the cheese, and squeezed it so the whey spurted out. “If you don’t hush this instant,” he shouted at the troll, “I’ll squeeze you the way I squeeze the water out of this white stone!”

“No, dear, spare me,” said the troll. “I’ll help you chop.”

Well, on those terms the boy spared him, and the troll could chop like a demon, so that they felled and trimmed many great stacks of logs in a day.

When it drew toward evening, the troll said, “Now you can come home with me. It’s nearer to my place than to yours.”

So the boy went along. And when they reached the troll’s home, the troll was to make up the fire on the hearth while the boy was to go fetch water for the porridge pot. But there stood two iron buckets, so big and heavy he couldn’t even manage to lift them off the floor.

Then the boy said, “It’s not worth hauling these little thimble-pails along. I’ll go and fetch the whole well, I will.”

“No, dear me,” said the troll, “I can’t lose my well. You make up the fire, and I’ll go for the water.”

When the troll came back with the water, they cooked up a great, steaming cauldron of porridge.

“All the same,” said the boy, “if you want to do as I do, we’ll have a contest.”

“Oh yes!” answered the troll, for he always thought he’d come out ahead.

So they sat down at the table. But the boy, without the troll noticing, took his leather knapsack and tied it in front of him, and he ladled more into the bag than he ate himself. When the bag was full he drew his belt-knife and slit a long gash in it. The troll watched him, but said nothing.

After they had been eating a good while, the troll laid down his spoon. “No, I can’t manage another bite,” he said.

“You must eat!” the boy answered. “I’m scarcely half full yet. Do as I did, and cut a hole in your belly, then you can eat as much as you like.”

“But won’t that hurt dreadfully?” asked the troll.

“Oh, nothing worth speaking of,” said the boy.

So the troll did as the boy told him, and you can well know he lost his life. But the boy took all the silver and gold that were in the mountain and went home with it. With that he could always pay something on the debt.
