Once upon a time there was a farmer who had three sons. He was old and frail. Money he had none; debts he had plenty. His sons might have helped, but none of them would lift a finger.

The farm had a fine big forest, though, and the father wanted the boys to cut timber there and pay something off the debt. At long last he got them moving, and the eldest was to go first.

He walked deep into the forest until he found an old spruce with a long grey beard of lichen, and there he set to work. He hadn’t made many cuts in the trunk before something creaked among the trees, and out stepped a huge great troll.

“Chop in my forest and I’ll kill you!” said the troll.

The boy froze with his axe in the air. Then he let it drop among the roots and ran for his life. He got home so out of breath he could hardly tell them what had happened.

“Hare-heart!” said his father. “When I was young, no troll ever frightened me off my chopping.”

The next day it was the second son’s turn, and it went exactly the same way. He had barely struck a few blows at the spruce when there stood the troll.

“Chop in my forest and I’ll kill you!”

The boy hardly dared look at him. He flung down his axe and took to his heels like his brother, and a good deal faster. When he got home, his father was furious.

“Never!” he said. “When I was young, there wasn’t a troll in the whole forest that could frighten me!”

On the third day the youngest wanted to go. They called him Askeladden, the Ash Lad, because he liked to sit by the hearth and poke in the ashes.

“You?” said his brothers. “Oh, you’ll manage it, you who’ve never been further than the front door!”

Askeladden didn’t answer that. He only asked for plenty of food to take with him. His mother had nothing to put on his bread, so she hung the pot over the fire and made him a little cheese. He tucked it into his knapsack and set off.

He had been chopping only a short while, and the chips were still flying, when the troll came to him too.

“Chop in my forest and I’ll kill you!”

But Askeladden was quick. He darted over to his knapsack, snatched out the cheese and squeezed it till the whey spurted between his fingers.

“Hold your tongue,” he shouted at the troll, “or I’ll squeeze you the way I’m squeezing the water out of this white stone!”

The troll shrank back with his hands up. He had squeezed a great many stones in his time, and not one of them had ever given him a drop.

“Oh, please, spare me!” he said. “I’ll help you chop.”

On those terms Askeladden let him live. And the troll could chop. All afternoon the spruces came crashing down, and by evening the two of them had felled and trimmed dozens upon dozens.

As evening came on, the troll said, “Now you can come home with me. It’s nearer to my place than to yours.”

Askeladden couldn’t argue with that.

Inside the mountain, the troll was to build up the fire while Askeladden fetched water for the porridge pot. But by the door stood two iron buckets so huge and heavy that he couldn’t shift them an inch.

Askeladden looked at them and waved a hand.

“It’s not worth lugging these thimbles about,” he said. “I’ll go and fetch the whole well.”

The troll stared at him in alarm. “No, no, my dear fellow! I can’t lose my well. You see to the fire, and I’ll fetch the water.”

When the troll came back with the water, they boiled up a mighty pot of porridge.

“Tell you what,” said Askeladden. “If you’re game, we’ll have an eating match.”

“Oh, yes!” said the troll. At eating, nobody had ever beaten him yet.

They sat down at the table. But without the troll noticing, Askeladden took his leather knapsack and tied it in front of his belly, and from then on more porridge went into the knapsack than into Askeladden. When it was full, he took out his knife and slit a hole in it, and the porridge ran out under the table.

The troll saw it, but he said nothing.

They ate a good while longer. At last the troll put down his spoon.

“No,” he said, “I can’t manage another bite.”

“You must eat!” said Askeladden. “I’m barely half full. Do as I did and cut a hole in your belly. Then you can eat as much as you like.”

“But doesn’t that hurt terribly?” asked the troll.

“Oh, nothing worth mentioning,” said Askeladden.

So the troll did as Askeladden said, and you can guess how that ended. Askeladden took all the silver and gold he could find in the mountain and carried it home. With that, at least, he could pay a little off the debt.
