Once there was a king who had so many geese that he needed a girl just to mind them. Her name was Aase, and so they called her Aase the Goose-girl.

Now there was a king’s son from England who was setting out to go wooing, and Aase went and sat down right in his path.

“Are you sitting there, little Aase?” said the prince.

“Yes, here I sit, patch upon patch and piece upon piece; for I’m waiting for the king’s son from England today,” said little Aase.

“You can’t expect to get him,” said the prince.

“Oh yes—if I’m to have him, I’ll get him soon enough,” said little Aase.

So painters were sent out to all lands and kingdoms to take likenesses of the fairest princesses, and the prince would choose between them. One pleased him so well that he traveled to her and meant to marry her, and he was both glad and proud when he had won her as his sweetheart. But the prince had a stone with him, and he laid it before his bed; and that stone knew everything.

And when the princess arrived, Aase the Goose-girl said to her that if she had ever had any sweetheart before, or if there was anything she did not want the prince to know, then she must not step over the stone he had placed before his bed—“for it will tell him everything about you,” she said. When the princess heard that, grief came over her, you may be sure. But then she thought of asking Aase if she would go in her place and lie down with the prince in the evening; and when he had fallen asleep, they would change again, so that he would have the right one beside him when morning came.

And so they did.

When Aase the Goose-girl came and stepped over the stone, the prince asked, “Who is it that steps into my bed?”

“Pure and spotless maiden!” said the stone, and so they lay down and slept. But when the night had worn on, the princess came and lay down in Aase’s place.

In the morning, when they were to rise, the prince asked the stone again, “Who is it that steps out of my bed?”

“One who has had three sweethearts,” said the stone.

When the prince heard that, he would have nothing to do with her, you may be sure, and he sent her home again and took another sweetheart in her stead.

When he was on his way to visit the next one, little Aase the Goose-girl had sat down in his path again.

“Are you sitting there, little Aase the Goose-girl?” said the prince.

“Yes, here I sit, patch upon patch and piece upon piece, for I’m waiting for the king’s son from England today,” said Aase.

“You can’t expect to get him,” said the prince.

“Oh yes—if I’m to have him, I’ll get him soon enough,” said Aase.

And with that princess it went just the same as with the first, except that when she rose in the morning the stone said she had had six. Then the prince would not have her either, and he drove her away. But still he thought he ought to try once more, to see whether he could find one who was pure and spotless; so he searched far and wide through many lands until he found one he could truly like.

But when he was on his way to her, Aase the Goose-girl had sat down in his path again.

“Are you sitting there, little Aase the Goose-girl?” said the prince.

“Yes, here I sit, patch upon patch and piece upon piece, for I’m waiting for the king’s son from England today,” said Aase.

“You can’t expect to get him,” said the prince.

“Oh yes—if I’m to have him, I’ll get him soon enough,” said little Aase.

When the princess arrived, Aase the Goose-girl told her as she had told the other two: if she had ever had a sweetheart, or if there was anything else she did not want the prince to know, then she must not step on the stone the prince had set before his bed—“for it will tell him everything,” said Aase. The princess took it badly when she heard this; but she was just as sly as the others, and begged Aase to go in her place and lie down with the prince that evening, and when he had fallen asleep they would change, so that he would have the right one with him when it grew light.

And so they did.

When little Aase the Goose-girl came and stepped over the stone, the prince asked, “Who is it that steps into my bed?”

“Pure and spotless maiden!” said the stone, and so they lay down.

Out in the night the prince slipped a ring onto Aase’s finger, and it was so tight she could not pull it off again; for the prince could well see that something was not right, and he wanted a mark by which he could recognize the true one. When the prince had fallen asleep, the princess came, chased Aase off to the goose-path, and lay down in her place.

In the morning, when they were to rise, the prince asked, “Who is it that steps out of my bed?”

“One who has had nine,” said the stone; and when the prince heard that, he grew so angry that he drove her out at once. Then he asked the stone how it could be that these princesses, who had stepped over it, turned out as they did—for he could not make sense of it, he said. And the stone told him how it had all gone: how they had tricked him and sent Aase the Goose-girl in their stead.

The prince meant to get to the bottom of it. He went down to where she sat tending her geese, for he wanted to see whether she too had the ring; if she had it, then it would be best to take her as his queen, he thought. When he came down, he saw at once that she had tied a rag around one of her fingers, and he asked her why she had done that.

“Oh, I’ve cut myself so badly,” said little Aase the Goose-girl.

Then he wanted to see the finger, but Aase would not take off the rag. So the prince seized her finger, and Aase tried to pull it back; and the rag slipped off, and there he recognized his ring.

Then he took her with him to the king’s hall and gave her fine clothes and plenty of splendor, and afterward they held their wedding. And so little Aase the Goose-girl got the king’s son from England after all—she did, simply because she was meant to have him.
