Once upon a time there was a king with more geese than he could count, so many that he had to keep a girl just to herd them. Her name was Aase, and so everyone called her Aase the Goose-girl.

One day a king’s son from England came riding by, with a cloak and a retinue and everything that goes with them. He was out wooing. But in the middle of the road sat Aase, on purpose. Her skirt was more patch than skirt, her geese were milling behind her, and she did not budge an inch. The King’s son had to rein in his horse.

“Are you sitting there, little Aase?” said the King’s son.

“Yes, here I sit, putting darn on darn and patch on patch,” said little Aase. “I’m waiting for the King’s son from England today.”

“You can’t expect to get him,” said the King’s son, and he should know.

“Oh yes. If I’m to have him, I’ll get him all right,” said little Aase.

Then painters were sent to every land and kingdom to paint the loveliest princesses there were. The King’s son walked up and down in front of the portraits for days, and at last he found one he liked so well that he travelled to fetch her. He was as glad and proud as a man can be.

But the King’s son never went anywhere without a certain stone. At night he laid it in front of his bed, and that stone knew everything. It was called the Knowing Stone.

When the princess arrived, Aase the Goose-girl went up to her.

“If you’ve had a sweetheart before,” she said, “or there’s anything else you’d rather he didn’t know, don’t step over the stone in front of his bed. It tells him everything about you.”

The princess was so upset it was a pity to see, you may be sure. But then she had an idea.

“Dear Aase,” she said, “couldn’t you lie down beside him tonight in my place? Once he’s asleep, we’ll change, and he’ll have the right one when it gets light.”

And so they did.

That evening Aase stepped over the stone, and the King’s son asked into the dark, “Who is it that steps into my bed?”

“A maiden, pure and simple!” said the stone.

So they slept. But deep in the night the princess crept in, Aase crept out, and the princess lay down in her place.

In the morning the King’s son was up first, and as the princess stepped over the stone after him, he asked, “Who is it that steps out of my bed?”

“One who has had three sweethearts,” said the stone.

The princess stopped with one bare foot on the floor. The King’s son stood by the bedpost, and his face went hard. The stone said nothing more.

He didn’t want her after that, as you can well imagine. She was sent home the same day, and he found himself another sweetheart.

On his way to visit her, he had to rein in his horse again. There sat little Aase the Goose-girl.

“Are you sitting there, little Aase the Goose-girl?” said the King’s son.

“Yes, here I sit, putting darn on darn and patch on patch, for I’m waiting for the King’s son from England today,” said Aase.

“You can’t expect to get him,” said the King’s son.

“Oh yes. If I’m to have him, I’ll get him all right,” said Aase.

It went no better with the new princess. Aase warned her, the princess took fright, and Aase went in her place. In the evening the stone said, “A maiden, pure and simple!” just as before. The only thing that changed was the number. In the morning the stone said six.

So the King’s son didn’t want her either, and he sent her packing. But he meant to try once more. Somewhere there had to be a girl who was pure and simple. He searched again, land after land, until he found one he could like.

But when he set out to fetch her, there was Aase the Goose-girl, sitting in the road as straight as ever.

“Are you sitting there, little Aase the Goose-girl?” said the King’s son.

“Yes, here I sit, putting darn on darn and patch on patch, for I’m waiting for the King’s son from England today,” said Aase.

“You can’t expect to get him,” said the King’s son.

“Oh yes, I can. If I’m to have him, I’ll get him all right,” said little Aase.

When the third princess arrived, Aase found her alone in one of the fine rooms of the manor.

“If you’ve had a sweetheart, or there’s anything he mustn’t know, don’t tread on that stone,” said Aase. “It tells him everything.”

Through the open door they could both see it, lying by the bed as if it had all the time in the world.

The third princess went pale. But she was every bit as sly as the other two. She leaned in close to Aase’s ear.

“Go in for me tonight,” she whispered. “Only until he’s asleep. Then we’ll change, and he’ll have the right one when it gets light.”

Aase looked at her and didn’t so much as blink.

And so they did.

That evening Aase stepped over the stone, and the King’s son asked, “Who is it that steps into my bed?”

“A maiden, pure and simple!” said the stone, and they lay down.

But by now the King’s son had begun to wonder.

Late in the night, while Aase slept, he slipped a ring onto her finger, so tight she would never get it off again. Now he had a mark to know the right one by. The stone lay at the foot of the bed and said nothing, since nobody asked it.

When the King’s son had fallen asleep, the princess came and chased Aase out to the goose-path, and crept into the warm place she had left.

In the morning the King’s son asked, as he always did, “Who is it that steps out of my bed?”

“One who has had nine,” said the stone.

The King’s son was so angry that he drove her out on the spot. Then he sat down on the bed and stared at the stone. Three princesses had crossed it at night, pure and simple every one, and by morning they had had three and six and nine.

“How can this be?” he said.

So the stone told him everything. The princesses had tricked him, all three of them, sending Aase the Goose-girl in their place and changing back while he slept.

“And you tell me now?” said the King’s son. “Why didn’t you say so before?”

“You never asked,” said the stone.

The King’s son meant to get to the bottom of this. He went down to the goose-path, where Aase sat minding her geese. If she has the ring, he thought, I might as well make her my queen.

Even from a distance he could see she had tied a rag around one finger.

“What have you done to your finger?” he asked.

“Oh, I’ve cut myself something dreadful,” said little Aase the Goose-girl.

He crouched down beside her and asked to see, but Aase would not take the rag off for anything. So he caught hold of her hand. She pulled, and he held on, and the geese crowded round them with their necks stretched out, and the rag slipped off. There was his ring.

He took her home to the King’s manor and gave her all the finery and fine clothes she could wish for, and then they held the wedding. And that is how little Aase the Goose-girl got the King’s son from England after all, just because she was going to have him.
