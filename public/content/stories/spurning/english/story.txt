Once there was a King who had a daughter so sharp-tongued and contrary that no one could ever get the better of her in words. So he proclaimed that whoever could silence her—whoever could leave her with nothing to answer—should have the Princess for his wife, and half the kingdom besides. Three brothers who had heard of this wanted to go and try their luck as well. First the two eldest went, sure they were the cleverest; but they got nowhere with her, and were punished for it. Then Askeladden set off.

After he had walked a little way, he found a willow withy twisted into a loop; he picked it up. After walking a bit farther he came upon a shard of a bowl; he picked that up too. A little farther on he found a dead magpie, and not far from it a crooked goat’s horn; a bit later he found another just like it. And as he crossed the fields by the King’s manor, where manure had been spread, he found a worn-out shoe-sole. All these things he carried with him to the royal house, and then he went in to the Princess.

“Good day,” said he.

“Good day,” said she, twisting herself about and making a face.

“May I roast my magpie?” he asked.

“I’m afraid she’ll burst,” the Princess answered.

“Oh, that’s no trouble—I’ll bind her round with this,” said the lad, and pulled out the willow loop.

“The fat will run off her,” said the Princess.

“I’ll hold this underneath,” said the lad, and showed her the shard of the bowl.

“You’re so crooked in your words, you are,” said the Princess.

“No, I’m not crooked—but this is,” he said, and brought out the one goat’s horn.

“No, now I’ve never seen the like of that!” cried the Princess.

“Here you can see its like,” said the lad, and brought out the other horn.

“I think you’ve come here to put me to silence, have you?” said the Princess.

“No, I haven’t come out—but this has,” said the lad, and drew out the worn-out shoe-sole.

And so the Princess was silenced.

“Now you’re mine,” said the lad; and he got her, and half the land and kingdom into the bargain.
