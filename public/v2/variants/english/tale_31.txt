The Seven Foals

Once upon a time, there was a poor couple living in a miserable hut deep in the forest. They barely had enough to survive, but they had three sons, and the youngest of them was the Ash Lad, because all he did was lie in the ashes and dig around.

One day, the eldest boy wanted to go out and seek his fortune. He was given permission right away and set off into the world. He walked all day long, and as night approached, he arrived at a royal palace. The king was standing on the steps and asked where he was heading.

"Oh, I'm just looking for work, Father," the boy said.

"Would you like to work for me and tend my seven foals?" asked the king. "If you can tend them for a whole day and tell me in the evening what they eat and drink, then you shall have the princess and half the kingdom. But if you can't, I'll cut three red strips from your back."

The boy thought that sounded like an easy task; he was sure he could do it.

In the morning, as the sky began to lighten, the stable master let out the seven foals. They ran off, with the boy following behind, and it could be said that they went over hills and valleys, through bushes and thickets. After chasing them for a good while, the boy began to tire, and soon he became fed up with the whole task of herding. Just then, he came to a rocky outcrop where an old witch was sitting, spinning with a spindle. Upon seeing the boy running after the foals, sweating profusely, the witch called out, "Come here, come here, my dear son, let me comb your hair!" The boy was willing and sat down in the outcrop, resting his head in her lap, while she combed him all day long as he lazed around.

As evening drew near, the boy decided to leave. "I may as well head straight home," he said. "There's no point in returning to the royal palace."

"Stay until it's dark," said the witch. "The foals will pass by on their way to the king. You can run home with them. No one will know that you've been sitting here all day instead of tending the foals."

When the foals came, she gave the boy a water flask and a moss clump to show the king, saying that was what the seven foals ate and drank.

"Did you mind the foals faithfully all day?" asked the king when the boy appeared before him in the evening.

"Yes, I did," said the boy.

"Then can you tell me what my seven foals eat and drink?" asked the king.

The boy presented the water flask and moss clump he had received from the witch. "Here is their food, and here is their drink," said the boy.

The king realized how the boy had tended the foals, and he became so angry that he ordered the boy to be driven away immediately, but first, they cut three red strips from his back and salted the wounds.

When the boy returned home, you can imagine how he felt. "I went out once to find work," he said, "but I'll never do that again."

The next day, the second son said he wanted to try his luck in the world. The parents said no and urged him to look at his brother's back, but the boy insisted, and eventually, he was allowed to go on his way. After traveling all day, he too came to the royal palace. The king stood on the steps and asked where he was heading, and when the boy replied that he was looking for work, the king said he could work for him and tend to his seven foals. The king set the same punishment and reward as for the brother. The boy was immediately willing, confident he could manage the task and tell the king what the foals ate and drank.

At dawn the next morning, the stable master released the seven foals; they took off over hills and dales, with the boy trailing behind. But it went the same way as with his brother; after running for a long while, until he was both sweaty and tired, he passed a rocky outcrop where an old witch sat spinning with a spindle, and she called to the boy, "Come here, come here, my dear son, let me comb your hair!" The boy liked that well enough; he let the foals run on and sat down in the outcrop with the witch. He sat and lazed around all day.

When the foals returned towards evening, he also received a moss clump and a water flask from the witch to show the king. But when the king asked the boy, "Can you tell me what my seven foals eat and drink?" and he presented the moss clump and the water flask and said, "Here is their food, and here is their drink," the king became angry again and ordered that three red strips be cut from the boy's back and salt rubbed in, and to chase him home immediately. When the boy returned home, he also told how things had gone and said that he went out once to find work, but he certainly wouldn't do that again.

On the third day, the Ash Lad wanted to give it a try; he was interested in taking a shot at herding the seven foals as well, he said.

The others laughed and mocked him; "when it went like this with us, how will you manage it? — you who never do anything but lie in the ashes and dig around!" they said.

"Yes, but I still want to try," said the Ash Lad. "I'm set on it," and no matter how much the others laughed and the parents pleaded, it didn't help; the Ash Lad set off on his way.

After traveling all day, he also arrived at the royal palace at dusk. The king stood on the steps and asked where he was heading.

"I'm looking for work," said the Ash Lad.

"Where are you from?" asked the king, for he wanted a bit more information before employing anyone.

The Ash Lad told him where he was from and said he was the brother of the two who had tended to the king's seven foals, and then he asked if he could try herding them the next day.

"Oh well," said the king, quite displeased just at the thought of them, "if you're their brother, then you're probably no better; I've had enough of their sort!"

"Yes, but since I'm here, could I at least try?" said the Ash Lad.

"Oh very well, if you insist on getting your back flayed, that's fine by me," said the king.

"But I would rather have the princess," said the Ash Lad. At dawn the following morning, the stable master let the seven foals out again, and they set off over mountains and valleys, through bushes and thickets, with the Ash Lad chasing after.

After running for a while, he too came upon the rocky outcrop; the old witch was sitting there again, spinning with her spindle and called out to the Ash Lad, "Come here, come here, my dear son, let me comb your hair!" she said.

"Kiss my rear, kiss my rear!" said the Ash Lad as he hopped along and held onto the foals' tails, racing past her. Once he got past the outcrop, the youngest foal stopped and said, "Hop on my back, for we have a long way to go," and so he did. They traveled a long, long distance. "Do you see anything now?" said the foal.

"No," said the Ash Lad. They traveled another long stretch. "Do you see anything now?" asked the foal.

"Oh no," said the boy. After traveling a long, long way, the foal asked again, "Do you see anything now?"

"Yes, now I think I see something whitish," said the Ash Lad, "it looks like a big birch stump."

"Yes, that's where we need to go," said the foal.

When they reached the stump, the oldest foal broke it aside, revealing a door where the stump had stood. Inside, there was a small room, and in the room, there was nothing much but a small hearth and a couple of stools; behind the door hung a large rusty sword and a small jug.

"Can you swing this sword?" asked the foal.

The Ash Lad tried but couldn't manage it; so he had to take a sip from the jug, first once, then again, and once more, and then he could handle it with ease.

"Yes, now you must take the sword with you," said the foal; "with it, you will cut off our heads on your wedding day, so we can become princes again, as we once were. We are the brothers of the princess you will marry, once you can tell the king what we eat and drink; but a dreadful troll cast this spell on us. When you have cut off our heads, be sure to place each head near the tail of the body it was on, so the enchantment will no longer have power over us."

The Ash Lad promised this, and they traveled on.

After going a long, long way, the foal asked, "Do you see anything?"

"No," said the Ash Lad.

They traveled some distance further. "What about now?" asked the foal; "do you see anything?"

"Oh no," said the Ash Lad.

They traveled many, many miles more, over hills and valleys.

"Now?" said the foal, "do you see anything yet?"

"Yes," said the Ash Lad, "now I see a line of blue in the distance."

"That's a river," said the foal; "we need to cross it."

They crossed the river over a long, splendid bridge, and after crossing, they went a long, long way again; then the foal asked once more if the Ash Lad could see anything.

Yes, this time he saw something dark in the distance, like a church tower.

"Yes, we need to go in there," said the foal.

When the foals reached the churchyard, they turned into humans again, looking like princes, wearing clothes so fine that they glowed, and they entered the church where the priest, standing at the altar, gave them bread and wine. The Ash Lad also went in, and after the priest had laid hands on the princes and given his blessing, they left the church again, as did the Ash Lad, who took with him a flask of wine and a communion wafer. When the seven sons reached the churchyard, they turned back into foals, and the Ash Lad mounted the back of the youngest, and they returned the same way they had come, but much, much faster than before. They crossed the bridge first, then passed the stump, and then past the witch sitting in the outcrop spinning; they moved so quickly that the Ash Lad couldn't hear what the witch screamed after him, though he understood that she was very angry.

It was almost nightfall when they returned to the royal palace, and the king himself was waiting for them.

"Have you tended the foals faithfully all day?" the king asked the Ash Lad.

"I've done my best," replied the Ash Lad.

"Then can you tell me what my seven foals eat and drink?" inquired the king.

The Ash Lad presented the communion wafer and wine flask to the king. "Here is their food, and here is their drink," he said.

"Yes, you have tended them faithfully," said the king, "and you shall have the princess and half the kingdom."

So, preparations for the wedding were made, and it was intended to be so splendid and grand that people would both hear and speak of it, the king said. But as they sat at the wedding table, the groom rose and went down to the stable — he had forgotten something there, he said, and had to go and get it. When he got there, he did as the foals had instructed, cutting off all seven heads, first of the eldest, then of the others, according to their age, and he took care to place each head by the tail of the foal it belonged to; and as he did this, they turned back into princes. When he returned to the wedding feast with the seven princes, the king was so pleased that he both kissed and patted the Ash Lad; and his bride was even more delighted with him than she had been before. "You've received half the kingdom now," said the king, "and you'll get the other half when I pass away; for my sons can find their own lands and realms now that they are princes again." So, indeed, there was much joy and celebration at that wedding.

I was there too, but no one had time to pay attention to me; all I got was a sandwich, which I placed on the oven, and the bread burned, and the butter melted, and I never got a single morsel back.
