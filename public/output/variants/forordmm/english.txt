**Foreword to the Sixth Edition**

The work, of which the sixth edition is now presented, comprises the first comprehensive collection of our folk tales, the joint collection of Asbjørnsen and Moe, which was first published in 1842-1844. For practical reasons, this edition is divided into two volumes, like the first edition, while later printings have combined the two parts into one. The work also appears in a revised form. For the literary demands that must be made of a classic edition are in this case intersected by another consideration, namely, that Norwegian Folk Tales is largely a book "for the people and the youth." Both Asbjørnsen and Jørgen Moe placed great emphasis on this. From edition to edition, they made changes and improvements to the original form of the text, and with each printing, the book's Norwegian tone and folk color became clearer and more consistent. 

Jørgen Moe's career, however, early forced him to withdraw from the work on folk tales; from the third edition (1866), Asbjørnsen managed the new editions alone. Moe was limited to making alterations in his contributions whenever he had the opportunity, primarily relying on the suggestions of a linguistically savvy friend. With an eye toward the desirability of a more thorough revision, he felt this was inadequate, and as it became apparent he might not be able to complete the work himself, he reviewed some tales with me to determine the direction and scope of the changes. Asbjørnsen later did the same; I discussed language and narrative style with him in several tales, and he also left some written contributions to the revision. Both storytellers explicitly instructed me to guide future editions of this book by the growing appreciation of local flavor in phrases, expressions, and narrative style. I have attempted to carry out this challenging task as best I can, and if I perhaps have gone further than strictly necessary at some points, I believe I have generally erred on the side of caution: the literary considerations have possibly constrained me more than they should according to my assignment, especially in the first third of the work. In any event, my goal has been never to change anything contradictory to the tone or style of the narrative, and I have certainly not gone as far as Asbjørnsen did in his new collection (1871 and 1876), where the broad Eastlands' character is strongly pronounced.

Concerning spelling, the task was also very complex. The book has two authors, whose writing styles, while closely related, did not completely align. Additionally, the orthography of different editions varies significantly, even within the same edition. Furthermore, the spelling in the later editions was primarily determined by an external person, the same linguistically knowledgeable friend who assisted the authors in revising the new editions; thus the orthography here only partly reflects the two storytellers' writing style. Under these circumstances, I found it best to base it on Asbjørnsen’s and Moe’s later writing practices while taking into account the first editions where both authors worked together. This is particularly true for punctuation. Like several writers of their time, Asbjørnsen and Moe used commas as much—if not more—for reading ease as for grammatical separation (though not always achieving full consistency or clarity). Both authors found it difficult to accept the stricter grammatical punctuation that appeared in later printings, and Jørgen Moe often said that this principle of punctuation was not well-suited to folk narratives, with their complex structures and spoken qualities.

Regarding spelling and comma usage, I was perhaps overly bound to the editions initially; some inconsistencies stem from this, while others, likely more, arise from the authors’ vacillating or flexible usage. In two places, I have made content additions. One is a sentence omitted since the first edition, yet required by context (Vol. II, p. 126). In another place (Vol. II, p. 166), I have, in agreement with Asbjørnsen, included a small episode (first published in my edition of Asbjørnsen's illustrated Fairy Tale Book for Children, Vol. II, Copenhagen 1887). However, I did not consider it justifiable to include in this work the retelling of “The Riddle” which Asbjørnsen and I provided there under the title “The Princess Who Could Not be Won Over,” nor obviously to present new, more complete versions of tales only planned or unfinished by the two authors at their deaths (such as no. 6 and no. 11).

Finally, I refer to the Afterword in the second volume, where I have reprinted the original subscription invitation and prefaces for the various editions. In the second edition (1851, though dated 1852), Jørgen Moe’s Introduction is also present (pp. XI-LXVIII; reprinted in Moe’s Collected Writings II, pp. 19-83) along with annotations by both storytellers, with variants and information on foreign parallels (pp. 383-502).

November 1899.  
Moltke Moe

**Foreword to the Seventh Edition**

I can be brief about this new edition. As stated in the foreword to the sixth edition, I already believed then that literary considerations might have constrained me more than they should have, in light of the directive from Asbjørnsen and Moe to "let future editions of this popular book follow the growing sense for the native in language." Besides correcting certain inconsistencies that developed over the long interval between the publication of the previous printing’s first and second volumes, I have now, cautiously yet decisively, gone further in Norwegian direction—regarding style, language, and spelling. Some changes remain unimplemented. It wasn't advisable to do too much at once. Moreover, several changes not yet completed should ideally go hand in hand with a reorganization of spelling—when we eventually get another official norm.

Norwegian Folk Tales begins in the 1851 edition with a dedication, not included in subsequent printings; the two men to whom the book was dedicated had already passed before the next edition saw light. I have not reintroduced the dedication. Yet something important is linked to the work by the fact that in its first complete edition, the first page bore the names Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm, "creators of the Germanic Folktale Literature."

September 1904.  
Moltke Moe
