Once upon a time, there was a fisherman who lived near the castle and fished for the king's table. One day, while he was out fishing, he caught nothing; no matter what he tried—baiting and fishing, fishing and baiting—he couldn't catch a single fish. But as the day wore on, a head popped out of the water and said, "If I get what your wife carries under her belt, you will catch plenty of fish." The man immediately agreed, not knowing his wife was expecting a child. Afterwards, he caught as many fish as he wanted. When he came home that evening and told his wife how he had gotten all the fish, she began to weep and lament, praying that God would comfort her over the promise her husband had made, for she told him she was with child. Soon, word of the wife's sorrow reached the castle, and when the king heard of it, he promised to take the child under his care and ensure its safety.

Time passed, and when the time came, the fisherman's wife gave birth to a baby boy. The king took the boy in and raised him as his own son until the boy grew up. One day, the boy asked to accompany his father on a fishing trip; he had a deep longing to go, he said. The king was reluctant to allow it, but eventually, the boy was given permission. He went with his father, and everything went well until they reached the shore in the evening. The son realized he had forgotten his handkerchief on the boat and decided to run out and get it. But as soon as he stepped into the boat, it began to drift away with him, gliding on the water until he reached a distant white shore.

After disembarking and walking a bit, he met an old man with a long white beard. "What is this place called?" asked the boy. "White Land," replied the man, and he asked the boy where he came from and what he wanted, to which the boy explained his story. "Well," said the man, "as you follow the shore, you'll come across three princesses buried up to their heads. The first will cry out and plead for you to help her, as will the second, but you must not go to them. Rush past as if you neither saw nor heard them. Go to the third, and do what she asks; that will be your fortune."

The boy did as instructed, ignoring the pleas of the first and second princesses, and went to the third. "Will you do as I say, you can have whichever of us three you desire," said the princess. Gladly, he agreed, and she explained that three trolls had buried all three sisters there; before, they lived in the castle visible in the forest. "Now you must go into the castle and endure a night of beating from the trolls for each of us," she said. "If you withstand it, you will save us." "Yes," said the boy, "I'll try that."

"When you enter," said the princess again, "there are two lions at the gate, but if you walk between them, they will not harm you. Go straight into a small dark room; lay down there. The troll will come and beat you; there's a flask on the wall you must use to heal yourself. Then grab the sword hanging beside the flask and kill the troll." The boy did as the princess instructed, walking between the lions as if they weren't there, and into the little room, where he lay down.

The first night, a three-headed troll with three switches came and whipped him brutally, yet he endured till the troll was done. Then he took the flask, applied it to his wounds, and felt as good as new. Grabbing the sword, he killed the troll. In the morning, the princesses stood above ground up to their waists. The second night was similar; a six-headed troll with six switches beat him even harder. But when morning came, the princesses stood above ground to their knees. The third night, a nine-headed troll with nine switches whipped him so fiercely he fainted; the troll threw him against the wall, causing the jug to break and spill over him, instantly reviving him. Without delay, he seized the sword and slew the troll. On the third morning, the princesses stood fully above ground.

He took the youngest of them as his queen and lived happily for a long time. Eventually, he longed to visit home and see his parents. His queen was reluctant to let him leave, but seeing his great desire, she said, "Promise me one thing: do as your father asks, but not as your mother wishes," and he promised. She gave him a ring that would grant him two wishes. He wished to be home, and his parents marveled at his grand and regal appearance. After a few days, his mother urged him to visit the castle and show the king what a fine man he'd become. His father objected, wishing to enjoy his company a while longer. Nevertheless, the mother persisted until he went.

At the castle, he appeared even grander than the king himself, which displeased the king. "Yes, but now you can see my queen; I cannot see yours. I doubt you have such a fair queen," said the king. "If she stood here, you would see!" the young king replied, and immediately, she appeared, looking very sad. "Why didn't you listen to me and heed what your father said?" she asked. "Now I must return home, and you've used both your wishes." With that, she tied a ring in his hair, bearing her name, and wished herself away.

The young king was despondent and spent his days contemplating how to return to his queen. "I must find out where White Land is," he thought and set off on a journey. After some time, he came upon a mountain where he met a man who was lord over all the forest animals, calling them with a horn. The king inquired about White Land. "I don't know," the man replied, "but I will ask my animals." He blew the horn, calling them, but none knew the way.

The man then gave him a pair of skis. "These will take you to my brother, who is lord of all the birds," he said. "When you reach him, simply turn the skis, and they will return." The king did as instructed, traveling a hundred miles to the bird lord. Upon arrival, he turned the skis, which returned as promised. He asked about White Land again, and the man summoned all the birds, but none knew where it was. After some time, an old eagle arrived, which had been away for ten years, yet even she didn't know.

"Very well," said the man, "I'll lend you another pair of skis. These will take you to my brother, who is lord over all the fish. Don't forget to turn the skis." Thanking him, the king used the skis to reach the fish lord, turned them, and back they went. Again he asked about White Land. The man summoned the fish. In the end, an old pike, whom the man struggled to summon, responded, "Yes, I know it well; I’ve been cook there for ten years. Tomorrow I'm heading back, as the queen whose king disappeared is to wed another."

"Then I'll give you some advice," the man said. "In the marsh over yonder stand three brothers who have fought for a hundred years over a hat, a cloak, and a pair of boots. With these, one can become invisible and wish to travel anywhere instantly. Tell them you wish to try the items and will judge between them." Grateful, the king approached the brothers. "What are you fighting over for eternity?" he asked. "Let me try the things, and I will judge between you." They agreed. Once he had all three items, he said, "You’ll hear my judgment next time we meet," and wished himself away.

As he traveled through the air, he met the north wind. "Where are you headed?" asked the wind. "To White Land," replied the king, explaining his story. "You'll probably travel faster," said the north wind, "but I must blow and gust every nook. When you arrive, stand on the step by the door, and I will come rushing as if to blow down the whole castle. When the prince who is to marry your queen steps out to see what's happening, seize him by the scruff and throw him out. I'll see to it that he's carried far away."

The king did as told, positioning himself by the door. As the north wind came roaring, rattling the castle walls, out came the prince to inspect. At that moment, the king grabbed him by the neck, tossed him out, and the north wind whisked him away. With the prince gone, the king entered the castle, though initially the queen did not recognize him, weak and thin as he'd become from his long, sorrowful journey. But once he showed her the ring, she rejoiced, and they celebrated their true wedding, news of which spread far and wide.
