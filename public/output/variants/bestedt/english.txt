Once upon a time, there was a king who had a daughter; she was so lovely that no one could be lovelier, and so she was not allowed to roam in the sun and wind, for they were afraid she would become less lovely. In the castle park grew a large linden tree, and it was strictly forbidden for her maidservants to be around it when the autumn winds blew. However, she had the habit of spending time under the linden tree, talking with her uncle, a tinker. What he said to her has not been passed down through the generations, but what she said to him was not much, yet it was sound and good.

The tinker often asked her how social gatherings were integrated. He kept her engaged in conversation, and her responses were poorly and weakly formulated, yet the discussions stayed at court. "Do you not gather them at the gatherings at court?" said the maidservants. "No, I suppose I'll be allowed to go home before they gather," she said.

It happened that a priest traveled around the countryside to teach the peasants about the ways of the world. "Don't forget about that," he said to the tinker; but he wished he had forgotten it, for the priest came to the linden tree, and the daughter asked him: "Who do you apply the buffalo cure to in your place?" "Self-effort, away from man, so say the gods!" replied the priest and left.

This was in Norway, and it is so to this day. Of all the affairs that arrive, it became the priests' turn, and they were not inclined to integrate; therefore, it became an expression, when in old times, a maidservant would be asked: "Have you been to the linden tree?" and this, one supposes, will likely remain in usage in the future as well.
