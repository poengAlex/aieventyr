The first day the fox was in charge of the woman's goats, he ate them all; the second day he finished off all her sheep, and on the third day, he devoured all her cows. When he returned home in the evening, the woman asked where he had put all her livestock. "The head is in the river, and the body in the woods," said the fox. She was in the middle of churning butter, but she felt she had to check for herself, and while she was gone, the fox slipped down into the churn and ate up the cream. When the woman returned and saw what had happened, she became so angry that she took the little bit of cream left and threw it at the fox, splattering some on the tip of his tail; that's why foxes have white-tipped tails.

The Blacksmith Who Wasn't Allowed Into Hell. Once upon a time, when Our Lord and Saint Peter wandered the Earth, they came to a blacksmith. He had made a deal with the Devil that he would belong to him in seven years, in exchange for being the master of all blacksmiths during that time. They both signed their names to the contract. That's why he had put in large letters over his smithy door: "Here lives the Master of All Masters!" When Our Lord saw this, he went inside. "Who are you?" asked the blacksmith. "Read over the door," replied the blacksmith; "but maybe you can't read, so you'll have to wait till someone who can comes along."

Before Our Lord could answer him, a man came with a horse, asking the blacksmith to shoe it for him. "Could I give it a try?" asked Our Lord. "You can try," said the blacksmith; "you can't make a bigger mess of it than I can fix it again." Our Lord went out and removed one of the horse's legs, placed it in the forge, and made the shoe glowing hot; then he sharpened the hob nails, fastened the shoe, and put the leg back on the horse. When he was done with that, he took off the other front leg and did the same. After reattaching it, he did the same with the hind legs, first the right and then the left, always following the same method.

While this was happening, the blacksmith was observing. "You're not a bad blacksmith," he said. "Do you think so?" said Our Lord.

Afterward, the blacksmith's mother came by the smithy and asked him to come home and eat lunch; she was very old, terribly bent in the back, and wrinkled in the face and could barely walk. "Notice what you see," said Our Lord; he took the woman, put her in the forge, and forged her into a beautiful young maiden. "I stand by what I said," said the blacksmith, "you're certainly not a bad blacksmith; it says over my door: Here lives the Master of All Masters, but still, I admit: One learns as long as one lives," and with that, he went to the farmhouse for lunch.

When he returned to the forge, a man came riding, wanting his horse shod. "That'll soon be done," said the blacksmith, "I've just learned a new way to shoe; it's useful when the days are short;" and he began to cut and break until he had removed all the horse's legs. "For I don't see why one should fuss with one leg at a time," he said. He placed the legs in the forge, as he had seen Our Lord do, added plenty of coal, and had the smith boys work the bellows quickly; but as one might expect, the legs burned up, and the blacksmith had to pay for the horse.

He didn't take that too well; but just then, an old poor woman passed by, and he thought: if one thing doesn't work, maybe another will, so he took the woman and put her into the forge. Although she cried and pleaded for her life, it didn't help; "you don't know what's best for you, as old as you are," said the blacksmith; "now you'll become a young maiden in a moment, and I won't charge a penny for the forging." It went no better with the poor woman than with the horse legs.

"That was a bad deed," said Our Lord. "Oh, it's not as if many will miss her," replied the blacksmith; "but it's a shame with the Devil: he barely keeps his promises."

"If you could have three wishes from me," said Our Lord, "what would you wish for?" "Try me," replied the blacksmith, "then you'll find out."

Our Lord gave him the three wishes. "First and foremost, I wish that whoever I invite to climb the pear tree outside the smithy wall will stay there until I myself ask him to come down again," said the blacksmith; "secondly, I wish that whoever I invite to sit in the armchair inside the workshop will stay sitting until I myself ask him to stand up; and finally, I wish that whoever I invite to crawl into the steel wire purse I have in my pocket will stay there until I give them permission to crawl out again."

"You wished like a foolish man," said Saint Peter; "first and foremost, you should have wished for God’s grace and friendship." "I didn't dare aim that high," said the blacksmith.

Then Our Lord and Saint Peter bid him farewell and continued on their way.

Time passed, and when the seven years were up, the Devil came, as stated in the contract, to fetch the blacksmith. "Are you ready now?" he asked, sticking his nose through the blacksmith’s door. "Oh, I was just about to finish this nail," replied the blacksmith; "meanwhile, you can climb the pear tree and pick a pear to snack on; you might be both thirsty and hungry from your travels." The Devil thanked him for the kind offer and climbed the tree. "Yes, on second thought," said the blacksmith, "I might not finish this nail for the next four years, this iron is devilishly hard; you won't be able to come down in that time, but you'll have to sit and rest for now." The Devil pleaded and begged as pitifully as a halfpenny for permission to come down again, but it was no use. In the end, he had to promise, he wouldn’t come back for four years, as the blacksmith had said. "Yes, then you can come down again," said the blacksmith.

When the time was up, the Devil returned for the blacksmith. "Now you're ready," he said, "I believe you must have finished the nail head by now." "Yes, I've finished the head," replied the blacksmith, "but still, you came a little early because the point I've not sharpened yet; I've never forged iron so hard before. While I sharpen the point, you could sit in my armchair and rest, as you must be tired, I imagine." "Thank you," said the Devil and sat in the armchair; but no sooner had he settled in than the blacksmith said again, that when he considered everything well, he could not sharpen the point for another four years. The Devil first nicely requested to be released from the chair, then got angry and began to threaten; but the blacksmith excused himself as best he could and said it was the iron's fault because it was so devilishly hard, and consoled the Devil by saying he was sitting comfortably and would be let go in precisely four years.

There was no other remedy: the Devil had to promise he wouldn’t return; and then the blacksmith said: "Yes, then you can stand up again," and the Devil rushed off as fast as he could.

After four years, the Devil came to fetch the blacksmith once more. "Now you're finished, I know," said the Devil, sticking his nose through the blacksmith’s door. "All done," replied the blacksmith, "now we can go whenever you want. But there’s one thing I've been standing here thinking I’d like to ask you: is it true what they say, that the Devil can make himself as small as he chooses?" "Indeed, it's true," replied the Devil. "Oh, then you might as well do me the favor of crawling into this steel wire purse and checking if it's intact at the bottom," said the blacksmith; "I'm afraid I might lose my travel money." "By all means," said the Devil; he made himself small and crawled into the purse. But no sooner was the Devil inside than the blacksmith closed the purse. "Yes, it’s all intact and tight everywhere," said the Devil from within the purse. "Yes, it's well and good you say so," replied the blacksmith, "but better safe than sorry; I think I'll just weld the seams a little, just to be sure," and with that, he put the purse in the forge and made it glow. "Ouch! ouch! are you mad? Don't you know I'm inside the purse?" shouted the Devil. "I can't help you," said the blacksmith, "they say an old saying: strike while the iron is hot," and with that, he took the sledgehammer, placed the purse on the anvil, and pounded it with all his might. "Ouch, ouch, ouch!" screamed the Devil in the purse; "dear me! let me out, and I'll never return." "Oh yes, now I think the seams are fairly welded," said the blacksmith; "then you can come out again." With that, he opened the purse, and the Devil rushed off so fast he didn’t dare look back.

After some time, the blacksmith thought he might have acted poorly when he made enemies with the Devil; "for if I shouldn't enter the kingdom of God," he thought, "I might risk becoming homeless since I’ve antagonized the one who rules over Hell."

He decided it was best to try to get into either Hell or Heaven, sooner or later, to know his standing, and so he took his sledgehammer on his shoulder and set off. After quite a distance, he came to a crossroads where the path to Heaven diverges from the one leading to Hell; there, he caught up with a tailor's apprentice who was scurrying along with his pressing iron in his hand. "Good day," said the blacksmith, "where are you headed?" "To Heaven, if I can get in," replied the tailor; "what about you?" "Oh, we likely won’t travel far together," replied the blacksmith, "I've thought to try Hell first since I’m somewhat familiar with the Devil." So they bid each other farewell and went their separate ways; but the blacksmith, being a strong, robust man, walked much faster than the tailor, and it wasn’t long before he reached Hell’s gate. He had the guard announce that there was someone outside wanting to have a word with the Devil. "Go out and see who it is," said the Devil to the guard, and the guard did. "Tell the Devil it’s the blacksmith, who had that purse he knows," replied the man, "and kindly ask that I be let in immediately, for I’ve smithed all morning and walked a long way since."

When the Devil got the message, he instructed the guard to lock all nine locks on Hell's gate, "and add a padlock too," said the Devil, "if he comes in, he'll wreak havoc in all of Hell." "There’s no help for it here then," the blacksmith said to himself upon realizing they were securing the gate even more; "then I'll try Heaven."

With that, he turned back and went to the crossroads again; there, he took the path the tailor had walked. Now frustrated about the long journey he had made back and forth for no reason, he strode as quickly as he could and reached Heaven's gate just as Saint Peter nudged it open a bit, allowing the thin tailor apprentice to slip in. The blacksmith was still six or seven steps from the gate. It's best to hurry here, he thought, grabbed the sledgehammer and threw it through the gap just as the tailor slipped inside; but if he didn't get in through that opening, I don't know where he ended up.

The Hen and the Rooster. The hen: "You promised me shoes, year after year, year after year, and I get no shoes!" The rooster: "You shall have shoes!" The hen: "I lay eggs, and I do good deeds, and yet I must go barefoot!" The rooster: "Then take your eggs and go to town, buy yourself shoes, and walk no longer barefoot!"
