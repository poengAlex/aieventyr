Once upon a time, there was a widower who had a housekeeper named Giske. She wanted to marry him and constantly pestered him about marrying her. Eventually, the man grew so tired of her insistence that he didn't know what to do to get rid of her. Between haymaking and the harvest, when the hemp was ripe, they set out to pull it. Giske always thought she was so pretty, skillful, and clever, so she pulled hemp until she got dizzy from the strong smell and fell asleep in the hemp field. While she slept, the man came with scissors and cut her skirts off; then he smeared her with tallow and soot, making her look worse than the devil himself.

When Giske awoke and saw how awful she looked, she didn't recognize herself. "Can this be me?" she said. "No, it can't be me, for I have never been this ugly; it must be the devil himself."

Wanting to understand what was going on, she went to the man's door and asked, "Is your Giske home today, Father?" "Oh yes, my Giske is here," he replied. "Then I can't be his Giske," she thought and left, glad the man was rid of her.

After walking some distance, she reached a large forest and met two thieves. "I'll join them," she thought, "since I'm like the devil, it suits me to be with thieves." But the thieves didn't think so; when they saw Giske, they ran as fast as they could because they thought the devil was after them. It didn't help them much, for Giske was long-legged and quick, catching up before they knew it. "Are you going out to steal? I will join and help you," she said, "I'm familiar with the area."

Hearing this, the thieves thought she would be good company and were no longer afraid. They were planning to steal a sheep but didn't know where to find one. "Oh, that's easy," said Giske, "I served at a farm near the forest for a long time; I could find the sheep barn in total darkness." The thieves liked that, so Giske was to go into the barn and pass sheep to them.

The sheep barn was right next to the wall of the house where the man lay sleeping. So Giske tiptoed quietly into the sheep barn, but once inside, she yelled to the thieves, "Do you want a ram or a ewe? There are plenty to choose from!" "Shh, just take a fat one," said the thieves. "Yes, but do you want a ram or a ewe? Do you want a ram or a ewe? There are plenty to choose from," Giske insisted. "Shh now, just take a fat one, either ram or ewe," said the thieves.

In the meantime, the man in the house woke up from all the noise and came out to see what was happening. The thieves took off, and Giske went after them, knocking the man over. "Wait, men! Wait, men!" she screamed. The man, having seen nothing but the black figure, was so frightened he hardly dared get up, thinking it was the devil himself in the sheep barn. He didn't know what else to do, so he went in and woke everyone, setting them to reading and praying, as he had heard one could pray away the devil.

The second evening, the thieves planned to steal a fat goose, and Giske was to show them the way. When they reached the goose house, Giske was to go in and pass geese out. "Do you want a goose or a gander? There are plenty to choose from!" she shouted when inside. "Shh, just take a heavy one," said the thieves. "Yes, but do you want a goose or a gander? Do you want a goose or a gander? There are plenty to choose from," she yelled. "Shh, just take a heavy one, either goose or gander, and shut your mouth," they said.

While Giske and the thieves argued, one of the geese began to scream, then another, and soon they were all screaming. The man came out to see what was happening, and the thieves ran away as fast as they could, with Giske chasing them so quickly that the farmer thought it was the devil; she was long-legged, and her skirts didn't hamper her. "Wait a bit, men!" shouted Giske, "You could have had what you wanted, either goose or gander." But they didn't think they had time to stop, and at the farm where they had been, everyone began to read and pray, convinced the devil had been there.

By the third day's evening, both the thieves and Giske were so hungry that they didn't know what to do with themselves; they decided to head to the storeroom of a rich farmer living by the forest and steal some food. So, they went there, but the thieves didn't dare go in, so Giske was to enter the storeroom and pass things out.

Inside, there was plenty of everything—meat, pork, sausages, and pea bread. The thieves hushed her and begged her just to throw out some food, reminding her of what had happened the previous two evenings. But Giske persisted, "Do you want meat or pork or sausage or pea bread?" she shouted loudly; "You can have what you want, for there's plenty to choose from!" The man at the farm woke up from the commotion and came out to see what was happening. The thieves ran off as fast as they could. Before long, Giske came running too, black and hideous. "Wait a bit, wait a bit, men!" she called, "You can have what you want, for there's plenty to choose from."

When the farmer saw the ghastly sight, he too thought the devil was loose, as he had heard what had happened on the prior nights, so he began to read and pray, as did everyone in the entire village, believing that one could read the devil away.

Saturday night, the thieves intended to steal a fat goat for their Sunday feast since they had been starving for days, but they didn’t want Giske with them, saying she just caused trouble and made a racket with her mouth.

While Giske waited for them on Sunday morning, she became very hungry after not having much to eat in three days, so she wandered into a turnip field and pulled up some to eat. The farmer whose field it was felt uneasy and decided he must check on his turnip field that Sunday morning. He hastily dressed and headed down to the marsh below the hillside where the field lay. There, he saw something black moving around, pulling up turnips, and quickly assumed it was the devil. He rushed back as fast as he could, telling everyone that the devil was in the turnip field. They were terrified at the farm, but thought it best to call for the priest to bind the devil.

"No, we simply can't disturb the priest today," said the woman, "it's Sunday morning; he won’t come now, he’s likely not even up this early, and if he is, he’ll be busy reading over his sermon." "I’ll promise him a roast from a fat calf, and he’ll come," the farmer assured her.

He made his way to the parsonage, but when he arrived, the priest was still abed. The servant girl invited the man into the living room while she went upstairs to inform the priest of the visitor. Hearing that such a respectable man was there, the priest hurriedly pulled on his trousers and came down immediately, still in slippers and a nightcap.

The farmer explained his errand, saying that the devil was loose in his turnip field, and if the priest would help bind it, he'd send him a roast of a fat calf. The priest was agreeable to this, calling for his servant boy to saddle the horse while he dressed. "No, sir, that won’t do," insisted the man, "the devil might not stay long, and who knows where he’ll get to once he’s loose. You must come right away."

The priest followed as he was, in his nightcap and slippers. But when they reached the marsh, it was so soft that the priest couldn’t cross it in slippers. The farmer hoisted the priest onto his back to carry him across. He carefully stepped from stump to mound, but when they reached the middle, Giske spotted them, thinking it was the thieves bringing the goat. "Is it fat, is it fat, is it fat?" she shouted, echoing through the woods. "The devil knows if it’s fat or lean," the farmer retorted upon hearing her; "but if you want to know, come feel for yourself," he said, frightened so much that he flung the priest into the marsh and took off, and if the priest hasn't gotten out by now, he might still be there.
