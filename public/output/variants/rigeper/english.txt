Once upon a time, there was a man known as Rich Per the Peddler because he traveled with goods and amassed a lot of money, becoming a wealthy man. Rich Per had a daughter whom he held in high regard, so much so that all the suitors who came for her were rejected because he thought none were good enough. When this continued to happen, eventually no more suitors came, and as the years passed by, Per grew fearful that she might remain unmarried. "I wonder," he said to his wife, "why no one comes to propose to our daughter, who is so wealthy? It would be quite odd if no one wanted her, for she has money, and she will get more. I think I should travel to the astrologers and ask them who she will marry; since no one is coming to our door.” "How can the astrologers possibly know that?" asked his wife. "Oh, they read everything in the stars," Rich Per replied.

He took along plenty of money to the astrologers and asked them to look at the stars and tell him who his daughter would marry. The astrologers examined the stars but said they couldn't see the answer. However, Per pleaded with them to examine more closely and finally tell him, promising to pay them well for it. The astrologers looked again more closely and then said that his daughter would marry the miller’s child, who had just been born in the mill right below Per’s estate. Per paid the astrologers a hundred coins and returned home with the information he had obtained. He thought it was utterly absurd that his daughter should marry someone so lowly, and newly born at that. He shared this sentiment with his wife and said, "I wonder if they’d be willing to sell me the boy; that way, we can dispose of him." "I suppose they would," said his wife, "they're poor people, after all."

So Per went down to the mill and asked the miller’s wife if she would sell him her son; he would pay her handsomely. She refused outright. "I can't understand why you won't," said Per. "Poverty is all you have, and I doubt the boy will relieve you of that." But she was so fond of her son that she wouldn’t part with him. When the miller came in, Per offered the same proposition, promising six hundred coins for the boy so that they could buy a farm, stop milling for others, and avoid starvation when there was no water for the mill. The miller liked the idea, discussed it with his wife, and so Rich Per acquired the boy. The mother wept and lamented her misfortune, but Per comforted her by saying he would take good care of him; however, they had to promise not to ask about him, for he intended to send him far away to other lands to learn foreign languages.

Once Per was home with the boy, he had a small chest crafted, so neatly made that it was a joy to behold. He sealed it with pitch, placed the miller’s boy inside, closed the lid, and set it adrift in the river, letting the current take it away. "Now I'm rid of him," thought Per. But as the chest floated downstream, it entered a sluice leading to another mill and rammed against the mill’s float, causing it to stop. The miller went to see what blocked the mill, found the chest, and took it up. When he came home at midday, he said to his wife, "I wonder what could be inside this chest; it floated down the sluice and blocked the mill today." "We'll soon find out," said his wife, "the key is still in the lock; just open it." Inside lay the most beautiful child anyone had ever seen, and they were overjoyed to have him. They kept the boy, for they had no children themselves and were at an age where they didn’t expect to have any more.

Some time passed, and once again Rich Per began to wonder why no suitors came for his daughter, who remained so rich. Still, none came, so he returned to the astrologers, offering them more money if they could tell him who his daughter would marry. "We've already told you she will marry the miller’s boy down there," they said. "Yes, that may be," said Per, "but something bad must have befallen him, for he is out of the picture now; and if I could find out who my daughter is to marry, I would gladly pay you two hundred coins." The astrologers looked again into the stars and then became angry, saying, "She will have the same miller’s boy you cast adrift on the river, intending to do away with him, for he is still alive and is at the mill so and so much farther downstream."

Rich Per paid them two hundred coins for the prophecy and contemplated how he might rid himself of the miller’s boy again. The first thing Per did upon returning home was to travel down to the mill. By then, the boy was old enough to have been confirmed at church, and he was working in the mill, assisting with the tasks. He had grown into a handsome young man. "Couldn’t you let me have that boy?" said Per to the miller. "No, I cannot," he replied; "I have raised him as my own, and he conducts himself well, so I can now have help and use from him in the mill, as I am growing old and frail myself." "Yes, it’s the same with me," said Per, "and that’s why I would like someone I could train in the trade. If you let me have him, I'll give you six hundred coins, enabling you to buy a farm and live in peace during your elderly years." When the miller heard this, he let Per have the boy.

The two of them traveled far and wide with their wares, until they came to an inn at the edge of a large forest. There, Per sent the boy home with a letter to his wife—it wasn't far through the woods—and asked him to tell her to do whatever the letter instructed immediately. However, the letter instructed her to build a large bonfire and throw the miller's boy upon it, and if she failed to do so, she would be burned alive herself. The boy set off with the letter through the forest. As night fell, he came upon a farm deep in the woods and went inside. There were no people to be found, but in one room, there was a bed prepared just right, and he lay across it to sleep. The letter he tucked under his hatband, and he placed his hat over his face. When the robbers returned—twelve of them who lived there—and saw the boy lying on the bed, they wondered who he could be, and one of them took the letter, broke the seal, and read it. "Hmm," he said, "it's from Rich Per the Peddler; but now we shall play a trick on him, for it would be a shame if the old rogue were to have his way with such a young admirable boy." So, the robbers wrote another letter to Per’s wife, fastening it under the hatband while the boy slept. In that letter, they wrote that she should immediately arrange a wedding for their daughter and the miller’s boy, give them horses, livestock, and tools, and set them up on the farm under the hill; if it wasn't done by the time he returned, she would face punishment.

The next day, the robbers let the boy go, and upon returning home and delivering the letter, he relayed that Rich Per had instructed it be followed at once. "You must have done good service," said Per’s wife to the miller’s boy, "for him to write such now; for when he left, he was so angry with you that he didn’t know how he could rid himself of you." She immediately arranged the wedding and set them out with horses, livestock, and all necessary tools to the farm beneath the hill.

Not long after, Rich Per returned home, and the first thing he asked was whether she had done what the letter instructed. "Yes, I thought it strange enough, but I dared not do otherwise," she replied. Then Per asked where their daughter was. "Surely you know where she is," said his wife; "she's on the farm under the hill with him, as instructed in your letter." When Rich Per realized the state of things and saw the letter, he became so angry he was close to bursting, and immediately set off to the farm of the young couple. "It's well enough, my boy, that you have my daughter," he said to the miller’s boy, "but if you want to keep her, you must go to the dragon of Deepwind and get me three feathers from its tail, for whoever possesses them can have whatever he wishes."

"Where do I find it?" asked the miller’s boy. "That's for you to find out; I don't know," said Per. The boy confidently set off on his way, and after traveling a while, he arrived at a king's court. Here, I'll go in and ask for directions, he thought, for such folks are usually better informed about the world, and perhaps I can find the way here. The king asked where he came from and what his business was. "I am on my way to the dragon of Deepwind to get three feathers from its tail, if I could only find him," said the boy.

He needs luck for that, thought the king, "for I've never heard of anyone returning from there," he stated. "But if you meet him, could you also ask on my behalf why I cannot get clean water in my well, which I have dug repeatedly yet never find clean water?" "Yes, of course," said the boy.

At the king's court, he was well fed and given both provisions and money for his travels. By evening, he reached another king's court. Upon entering the kitchen, the king inquired where he was from and what mission brought him here. "I am going to the dragon of Deepwind to get three feathers from its tail," replied the boy. "You'll need luck for that," said the king, "as I've yet to hear of anyone returning from there. But should you find him, could you also ask about my daughter who disappeared many years ago? I've searched and sent out messages from all the churches, but no one can say anything about her.”

"I’ll do that," promised the boy.

At this king's court, he also lived well, receiving both provisions and money upon his departure. As evening approached again, he came to yet another king's court. This time, the queen came out and asked where he was from and why he was traveling. "I am seeking the dragon of Deepwind to obtain three feathers from its tail," said the boy. "Indeed, you will need luck to accomplish that," remarked the queen, "for I've never heard of anyone returning from there. But if you find him, could you ask on my behalf where I might find my lost gold keys?" "Certainly," agreed the boy.

After some time walking, he arrived at a wide, broad river. Standing bewildered, pondering how to cross or follow alongside it, an old hunchbacked man appeared, asking where he was going. "I'm trying to reach the dragon of Deepwind, if somebody could direct me to him," the boy explained. "I can indeed guide you," said the man, "as I help those needing passage across. He lives just beyond the hill; once you reach the top, you'll see his castle; and should you speak with him, please ask on my behalf how long I must continue ferrying people over." "I shall ask that," promised the boy. The man carried him over the river, and as he ascended the hill, the boy spotted the castle and walked inside.

Inside, the princess was alone. "Dear me! How can a Christian soul dare to come here?" she exclaimed. "No one of that faith has been here since I arrived, and it would be best for you to leave swiftly, for once the dragon returns, he will sense your presence and consume you instantly, and I too will face an ill fate.” "No," the boy declared, "I won't leave until I have obtained three tail feathers from the dragon." "You shall never get those," answered the princess. Still, the boy insisted on waiting for the dragon to gather the feathers and get answers to the questions. "Well, since you're so adamant, I suppose I should try to help you," she conceded. "Can you lift that sword hanging on the wall?" The boy couldn't budge it. "In that case, take a sip from this bottle," she instructed.

After resting a while, he attempted once more; he could barely move the sword. "Take another sip," suggested the princess, "and do tell me your mission." Taking a second sip, he explained that a king had asked him to inquire with the dragon on how he might get clean water in his well; another king wanted to know what had happened to his daughter, missing for years; a queen was looking for her lost gold keys; and finally, the ferryman wanted to know how long he must continue porting people over.

After some time, having taken yet another sip, the boy could swing the sword. "To escape being devoured instantly by the dragon," instructed the princess, "hide under the bed as night approaches, for he'll soon arrive home. You must remain extremely still so that he doesn't notice you. When we retire to bed, I'll ask him the questions; listen closely and remember his answers. Stay under the bed until all is quiet, and the dragon has fallen back to sleep; then sneak out cautiously, taking the sword. Once he rises, be prepared to decapitate him in a single stroke, and grab the tail feathers immediately, lest he rip them out himself to prevent others' benefit."

A while after the boy slid under the bed, the dragon entered. "I smell a Christian scent!" declared the dragon. "Oh, it must be a raven, flying with a human bone in its beak, just landing on the roof," suggested the princess, "that must be what you’re sensing." "So it is," agreed the dragon.

The princess served dinner, and after they ate, they went to bed. After lying for a while, the princess began to toss and turn restlessly until she finally sat up with a start. "Ouch!" she cried out. "What's wrong?" wondered the dragon. "Oh, my dreams are troubling," she explained, "and I had such a peculiar one." "What did you dream?" inquired the dragon. "I dreamt a king came here to ask how he might have clean water in his well," she said. "He should know how himself," the dragon grumbled. "Clear out the old rotten log at the bottom, and clean water he shall have. Now, rest peacefully and dream no more."

Not long thereafter, the princess grew restless again, causing her to wake with another startle: "Ouch!" "What now?" asked the dragon irritably. "Oh, I sleep so fitfully," she said, "and I've had another strange dream." "Dreaming again, are we? What was it this time?" "I dreamed a king asked about the whereabouts of his daughter, who disappeared years ago." "That’s you," the dragon retorted; "but he’ll see you no more. Now, please let me sleep, or I'll crack your ribs."

Hardly any time passed before the princess became restless yet again, waking with a start. "Ouch!" she gasped. Provoked, the dragon grumbled, threatening her if it continued. "Don't be cross," she implored, "but I had yet again another unusual dream." "Bah, more dreams!" growled the dragon. "Tell me this one." "A queen came asking where she might find her missing gold keys," told the princess. "She can search beneath the bushes where she lay that time; she’ll find them there," answered the dragon. "Let me sleep now."

Shortly after, she shifted and stirred uneasily once more, finally waking and exclaiming: "Ouch!" By now, the dragon was furious: "You won't be content until I break your neck. What is it now?" "Oh, please, don't be upset; it's beyond my control," she pleaded. "But that was an odd dream." "So many dreams!" cursed the dragon. "What was this one?" "I dreamt that the ferryman at the river asked how long he must keep ferrying over," shared the princess. "Stupid fool! He can stop immediately when a passenger comes; just cast him into the river, declaring, 'Now you take over until relieved!'" Retired from further dreaming, she allowed him finally to doze off.

Promptly, the boy crawled out as the dragon snored. Before dawn, the beast rose, not even finding his footing before the boy slashed off its head and snatched three feathers from its tail. Great joy filled them then, with the princess and boy taking all gold, silver, coins, and precious items they could carry. Reaching the river, the ferryman was so distracted with the load bestowed upon him that he forgot to ask the dragon’s counsel until the boy and princess were safely across. "You there!" he cried. "Did you ask the dragon what I wanted?” The boy answered affirmatively: "He said when a passenger arrives, throw him into the river, shouting: 'Now it's your turn to take over till you're released'—that’s how you'll be free." "Curses upon you!" lamented the ferryman. "Had you told me sooner, I’d have been free now!"

Arriving at the first king's court, the queen quickly inquired about her gold keys. "Indeed I asked," whispered the boy, responding, "The dragon said to search below the bushes where you laid that time, you'll find them there." "Shh, not another word," she cautioned, rewarding him with a hundred coins. At the subsequent court, he was asked whether he had queried about the missing daughter. "Indeed," responded the lad, "Here she is." In joyous disbelief, the king readily offered the miller’s boy his daughter and half his kingdom. But bound by marriage, two hundred coins were granted, along with horses, wagon, and as much gold and silver as he could carry. 

Traveling to the third kingdom, the king there queried about the well. "Yes," answered the young man, "You must clear the well and remove the old rotten log at the bottom to get clean water." The king gratefully gifted him three hundred coins.

Richly adorned in gold and silver, more prosperous than Rich Per himself now, the lad returned home. With the feathers in hand, Rich Per sanctioned the marriage without dispute. But upon witnessing the wealth, he anxiously asked, "Is there so much at the dragon’s lair?" "Indeed much more than one could carry, enough for many wagonloads, should you venture there," replied the boy. Eager, Rich Per resolved to travel, with specific directions to spare inquiries. "But leave your horses this side of the river, trusting the ferryman will aid you across."

Setting off with ample provisions and numerous steeds, Rich Per reached the riverbank, leaving behind the horses per the boy's advice. The ferryman hefted him onto his back, but once midway, he cast him into the depths, declaring: "Now you ferry until freed!" And if no one has released him since, Rich Per the Peddler remains there, helping travelers across the river to this very day.
