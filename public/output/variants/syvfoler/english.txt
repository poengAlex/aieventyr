The Seven Foals

Once upon a time, there were a poor couple living in a shabby hut deep in the forest. They barely scraped by, living from hand to mouth. However, they had three sons, and the youngest was known as Askeladden, for he did nothing but lie by the hearth, poking at the ashes. One day, the eldest son announced his intention to go seek his fortune, and his parents readily agreed, allowing him to set off into the world. He traveled the whole day and, as evening approached, he arrived at a royal castle. There, the king was standing on the steps and inquired about the boy's destination. "Oh, I'm just wandering about, looking for work, sire," said the boy. "Would you like to work for me and tend my seven foals?" the king asked. "If you can care for them all day and tell me what they eat and drink by evening, you'll win the princess and half my kingdom; if you fail, I'll cut three red stripes from your back." The boy thought the task sounded simple and was confident he could do it.

At dawn the next morning, the stablemaster released the seven foals; they dashed off, and the boy ran after them through hills and valleys, through bushes and thickets. After a considerable chase, he began to tire and soon became weary of the whole endeavor. Just then, he reached a rocky cleft where an old crone sat spinning with a spindle. Upon seeing the boy struggling to keep up with the foals, she called out, "Come here, come here, my dear son, and let me clean your hair of lice!" Eagerly, the boy accepted, sitting down at the cleft and laying his head in her lap while she picked through his hair the entire day as he rested. As evening neared, the boy prepared to depart. "I might as well go straight home," he said, "since there's no point in returning to the castle." "Stay a little longer until nightfall," advised the crone, "then the foals will pass by again on their way back to the king; you can return alongside them, and no one will know you spent the day here instead of tending the foals." When they came back, the crone handed the boy a water bottle and a piece of moss, instructing him to show the king and claim these were the foals' food and drink.

"Have you tended them faithfully all day?" the king inquired when the boy returned in the evening. "Yes, I certainly have," the boy claimed. "Then tell me, what do my seven foals eat and drink?" the king demanded. The boy displayed the water bottle and moss he received from the crone. "Here's their meal, and here's their drink," he declared. But the king quickly discerned how the boy had failed to tend the foals properly, becoming so enraged that he ordered three red stripes cut from the boy's back, rubbing salt into the wounds before sending him away immediately. 

Upon returning home in such a state, it's understandable how dejected he felt. He vowed never to seek work again. The next day, the second son announced his intention to try his luck in the world. Despite his parent's reservations and reminders of his brother's plight, he insisted on going, and eventually received permission to set out. After a day's journey, he too arrived at the castle where the king repeated the offer and warning given to his brother. The boy agreed, confident he could perform the task.

The same fate befell him; after chasing the foals till exhaustion, he met the same crone, who offered to rid him of lice. He spent the day lounging, and the crone later provided him with a water flask and moss. Predictably, the king again was incensed when the boy offered his explanation and showed the items. Three red stripes were carved into his back and salted, and he was promptly sent home.

Upon his return, he recounted his ordeal and resolved never to seek work again. On the third day, Askeladden expressed his desire to venture out and attempt to herd the seven foals despite the ridicule from his brothers. "If we couldn't do it, you certainly won't succeed, you who do nothing but wallow in ash," they taunted. But Askeladden remained determined, saying, "I can't shake this from my head," and set off despite their laughter and his parents' protests.

Finally arriving at the castle at dusk, the king, suspicious due to the prior incidents, inquired about Askeladden's intentions. After explaining who he was and requesting the same opportunity as his brothers, the king reluctantly consented, grumbling, "If you want to have your back flayed, so be it." "I'll aim for the princess instead," said Askeladden. At dawn, he followed the seven foals, refusing the crone's invitation to rest and running after the creatures relentlessly. When asked by the youngest foal if he saw anything after travelling a considerable distance, Askeladden said no. They traveled further until Askeladden saw something white, resembling a large birch stump. "That's where we're headed," said the foal.

Upon reaching the stump, the eldest foal shifted it aside to reveal a door to a small hut containing only a fireplace and some stools, with a rusty sword and a small jar behind the door. "Can you wield the sword?" asked the foal. Askeladden tried but could not, drinking from the jar thrice before managing it effortlessly. "Now take the sword," the foal instructed, "with which you'll sever all our heads on your wedding day, returning us to our princely forms, former as brothers to the princess you'll marry after revealing our diet to the king. Be sure each head is placed by the tail of its correct body, or the curse will persist."

Askeladden accepted these terms, and they journeyed onward until reaching a distant blue-looking stripe, which the foal identified as a river to cross. Using a fancy bridge, they continued until they saw what appeared to be a church tower. Upon entering the churchyard, the foals became princely again, lavishly dressed, as they convened with the priest who provided them bread and wine. Askeladden collected a flask of wine and a piece of altar bread before departing with the princes, reverting to foals, and retracing their path at an incredible speed back to the king’s court by evening.

The king awaited their arrival, querying Askeladden about his tending of the foals. Producing the altar bread and wine flask, Askeladden presented them as the foals' provisions. "Indeed, you've cared for them faithfully," said the king, awarding the princess and half the kingdom. And so, preparations for a grand, celebratory wedding commenced. During the festivities, Askeladden excused himself for a brief errand to the stable, where he fulfilled the foals' request, decapitating each as instructed and restoring them to their princely forms.

Presenting the seven princes at the table, the king was overjoyed, showering Askeladden with affection, and the princess's admiration for her groom grew immensely. "You've earned half the kingdom," the king conceded, "and upon my passing, you shall have the rest. My sons, now princes once again, can acquire lands and realms on their own." Betwixt the merriment and revelry that ensued, I was also present, though scarcely noticed amidst the commotion. All I received was a bit of bread, which I placed upon the oven, only to have it burn away, losing the butter entirely, never to be seen again.
