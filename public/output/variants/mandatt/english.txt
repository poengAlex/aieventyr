Once upon a time, there were two people who got married; each had a daughter. The stepmother's daughter was lazy and sluggish and never wanted to do anything, while the father's daughter was diligent and willing. However, no matter what she did, she could never please the mother, and both the stepmother and her daughter wanted to be rid of her. One day, they sat by the well to spin, where the stepmother's daughter would spin flax, but the father's daughter was only given bristles. "You're always so quick and clever," said the stepmother's daughter, "yet I'm not afraid to spin against you." So, they made an agreement that whoever's thread broke first would have to jump into the well. Naturally, the father's daughter's thread snapped first, and she had to go into the well. But when she reached the bottom, she didn't get hurt, and all around her was a beautiful green meadow. As she walked through it, she came upon a brushwood fence she had to cross. "Oh, don't step on me too hard, and I'll help you another time," said the fence. She stepped lightly, barely touching it. Walking further, she encountered a mottled cow with a milk pail on its horns, the cow was full of milk. "Oh, be so kind as to milk me," said the cow, "for I am about to burst; drink as much as you want, and pour the rest on my hooves, and I'll help you again." She did as the cow asked; just grabbing the teats made the milk flow into the pail; she drank until her thirst was quenched and poured the rest on the cow's hooves, then hung the pail back on the horns. Continuing on, she met a large sheep with wool so thick and long it dragged after it, and on one horn hung a large pair of scissors. "Please shear me," said the sheep, "for I am so laden with wool in this heat that I am nearly suffocating. Take as much as you like, and wrap the rest around my neck, and I'll help you again." She was quick to help, and the sheep laid itself on her lap, calm and still, while she clipped it neatly without a single scratch on its skin. She took as much wool as she wanted and wrapped the rest around the sheep's neck.

A bit further on, she reached an apple tree fully laden with apples, its branches bending toward the ground; by the trunk stood a little stick. "Please, pluck the apples off me so my branches can straighten, as it pains me to stay so crooked. Be gentle though, and don't damage me; eat as much as you wish, and place the rest by my roots, and I'll help you again." She picked what she could reach and used the stick to gently knock down the rest. After filling up on apples, she carefully laid the leftovers by the root. After walking a great distance, she arrived at a large farm where a witch and her daughter lived. She asked if she could enter service. "Oh, it’s no good," said the witch, "We’ve had many, but none were worth anything." Nevertheless, she pleaded so beautifully that they hired her. The witch gave her a sieve, asking her to fetch water with it. It seemed absurd to collect water in a sieve, but she went ahead anyway, and when she reached the well, the small birds sang: "Seal it with clay, plug it with straw! Seal it with clay, plug it with straw!" She did so, and was able to carry water in the sieve just fine. But when she returned with the water, and the witch saw the sieve, she said, "You didn't come up with that on your own!" Then, the witch told her to clean the cowshed and milk the cows, but the shovel was so large and heavy that she couldn't even lift it. With no idea how to proceed, the birds sang that she should take the broom and scatter a little, and the rest would fly out by itself. She did as they sang, and the shed was suddenly as neat as if it had been thoroughly cleaned. Then she had to milk the cows; they were restless, kicking and tossing about so much that she couldn’t milk them. But the birds sang outside: "A little spray, a little sup for the little birds!" She followed suit, sprinkling a little milk out to the birds, and all the cows stood still, allowing her to milk them without raising a hoof, kick, or push. When the witch saw her return with the milk, she said, "You didn’t come up with that on your own!" Then she told her to wash the black wool white. This she had no idea how to accomplish, for she had never seen anyone turn black wool white, but she said nothing, taking the wool out to the well. Here, the small birds sang that she should put the wool into the large tub standing there, and it would turn white. "No, no!" cried the witch when she returned with the wool. "It's no use having you; you can do everything; you'll just drive me crazy. It's best to let you go." So, the witch set out three boxes: a red one, a green one, and a blue one, telling her she could choose one for her reward. She didn’t know which to take, but the birds sang: "Don’t take the green, don’t take the red; choose the blue, marked with three crosses!" She chose the blue one, just as the birds sang. "Curse you and woe," said the witch, "you'll pay for it." When the father's daughter was about to leave, the witch threw a red-hot iron bar after her. But she ducked behind the door just in time, hiding, so it missed her; the little birds had told her how to manage it. She hurried on as fast as she could, but when she came near the apple tree, she heard a rumbling sound behind her; it was the witch and her daughter coming after her. She was so frightened she didn’t know where to hide. "Come here," said the apple tree, "I'll help you; hide under my branches and don’t let them catch you, or they’ll take your chest and tear you to pieces." She did as the tree advised, and soon enough, the witch and her daughter arrived. "Have you seen a girl pass by here?" asked the witch. "Oh yes," said the apple tree, "one ran past a while ago; but she's too far off now for you to catch her." The witch turned back and went home. The girl continued on her way, but at the sheep pen heard the same frightening sound as before. She didn’t know where to go, panic-stricken as she was, knowing well that it was the witch turning back. "Come here, and I'll help you," said the sheep; "hide under my wool so they don't see you; or they'll get your chest and tear you apart." Quickly enough, the witch came rushing by. "Have you seen a girl pass this way?" she asked the sheep. "Oh yes," said the sheep, "I saw someone earlier, but she ran so fast you'll never catch her." So, the witch turned and went home. By the time she reached the cow, the noise started anew. "Come here," said the cow, "and I’ll help you; hide under my udder and they won’t see you, or they'll seize your chest and tear you apart." It wasn’t long before they came along. "Have you seen a girl pass here?" asked the witch to the cow. "Yes, I did see one earlier, but she's far ahead now; she ran so fast you won't catch her," said the cow. The witch then turned back and went home. As the girl came near the brushwood fence, she heard the terrifying sound again and was filled with fear, as she knew it was the witch returning. "Come to me, and I'll help you," said the fence; "crawl beneath my twigs to prevent them from seeing you; otherwise, they'll rob you of your chest and tear you to pieces." So, she hurried under the twigs of the brushwood fence. "Have you seen any girl passing here?" asked the witch to the fence. "No, I haven’t seen any girl," replied the fence, stretching itself tall to make crossing impossible. The witch had no choice but to turn back and return home. Upon arriving home safely, both the stepmother and her daughter were even more envious of her than before, for now, she was even more beautiful, and so splendid to behold. She was not allowed indoors but was banished to the pigsty. There, she cleaned thoroughly and opened her chest to see what her reward was. As soon as she did, it contained so much gold, silver, and fine things that every wall and ceiling were adorned, finer than the grandest royal palace. When the stepmother and daughter saw this, they were beyond incensed, demanding to know how her service had gone. "Oh," she said, "you know well by now, given such a reward; they were such people and such a mistress to serve that none can compare." Eagerly, the stepmother's daughter insisted on serving too, seeking to gain a chest of gold herself. They sat to spin once more; this time, it was the stepmother's daughter spinning bristles, while the father's daughter spun flax, determined again that whoever's thread broke first must descend into the well. Predictably, the stepmother’s daughter’s thread snapped first, and they cast her into the well. The same events unfolded; she fell to the bottom without harm and ended up on a lovely green meadow. There she met the brushwood fence. "Step gently upon me, and I’ll help you later," it said. "Oh, what do I care for a pile of twigs," she said arrogantly, walking heavily on it until it creaked. After a while, she reached the cow swollen with milk. "Please milk me," said the cow, "and I’ll help you again; drink as much as you like but pour the remainder on my hooves." She milked the cow, drank as much as she desired, but left nothing for its hooves; she lobbed the pail carelessly away and continued. She soon encountered the sheep with its dragging wool. "Please shear me, and I’ll serve you again," said the sheep, "take as much wool as you like but wrap the rest around my neck." So she did, but her handling was so clumsy that she cut large holes in its fleece, taking all the wool with her. Shortly afterward, she came upon the apple tree, stooped under its weight of apples. "Please pluck the apples from me, so my branches can stand straighter as it’s such a strain," said the tree; "but handle with care not to damage me; eat as much as you and lay the rest neatly by my roots, and I will help you again." She picked the ones within reach, but carelessly beat down the rest with the stick, snapping large branches, gorging herself until she couldn't eat more, then threw the remains haphazardly under the tree. Eventually arriving at the witch's house, she asked for employment. The witch said she didn't need a servant, for either they were inadequate or too clever, cheating her out of her possessions. Nonetheless, the stepmother's daughter persisted, insisting upon taking the job, and the witch agreed, albeit reluctantly. Her first job was to fetch water in the sieve. She attempted to do so, but water spilled through just as fast as it was filled. The birds sang: "Seal with clay, stopper with straw! Seal with clay, stopper with straw!" yet she ignored them, flinging clay at the birds, which scattered away, leaving her to return with an empty sieve, earning her a scolding from the witch. Next, she was tasked with cleaning the cowshed and milking the cows, jobs she considered beneath her. Nevertheless, she entered the shed, but the shovel was unwieldy for her to manage. The birds sang the same tune as for the father's daughter, to pick up the broom and scatter some mess; the rest would follow. She tossed the broom at them before struggling with the task. As she attempted milking, the cows fussed, kicking over whatever little she managed to collect. The birds sang again, "A little spray, a little sup for all the little birds!" In response, she lashed out, throwing whatever she could find at the birds while flaring at the cows; she managed neither to clean nor to milk. Returning within, she received a rough scolding from the witch. Lastly, she was instructed to wash the black wool white; but failed there too. The witch found this completely intolerable, hence, offered her a choice among three caskets: a red, a green, and a blue one, declaring she didn't want a servant who was no good to her but could let her choose a reward. The birds sang: "Don’t take the green, don’t take the red; choose the blue, with three crosses on its head!" Unconcerned with their song, she picked the red, as it appeared the most striking. Off she went, departing with ease; no one chased her. Arriving home, her mother was overjoyed, they promptly placed the casket in the drawing room, convinced it contained gold and silver, expecting it to gild the walls and ceiling entirely; but when they opened it, only snakes and toads spilled forth, and when the stepmother’s daughter opened her mouth, more of the same swarmed forth, forcing them eventually to flee the house. This was her reward for serving the witch.
