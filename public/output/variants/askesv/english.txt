Once upon a time, there was a poor man who had three sons. When he died, the two eldest sons decided to go out into the world to seek their fortune; however, they refused to take the youngest with them. "You there!" they said, "you're good for nothing but to sit and dig in the ashes." "Well, then I'll go alone," said the youngest, known as Askeladden. The two set off and found jobs at the king's palace; one worked for the stable master, and the other for the gardener. Askeladden also set out, bringing with him a large kneading trough, the only thing they had inherited from their parents, which the other two brothers didn't care for. It was heavy to carry, but he wouldn't leave it behind.

After walking a while, he arrived at the king's palace as well and asked for work. They said they had no use for him, but he pleaded so earnestly that they eventually let him work in the kitchen, carrying wood and water for the cook. He was diligent and clever, and before long, everyone liked him very much, while the two others were lazy and received scoldings and little pay, becoming envious of Askeladden when they saw that he was doing better.

Across a body of water from the king's palace lived a troll with seven silver ducks that swam in the water, visible from the palace. The king often wished for them, so the two brothers told the stable master, "Our brother has claimed he can get the king the seven silver ducks if he wants to." You can imagine it didn't take long for the stable master to relay this to the king. The king then summoned Askeladden and said, "Your brothers tell me you can get me the silver ducks, and now you shall do it." "I've neither thought nor said so," the boy replied. But the king insisted: "You said it, and you shall do it," he said. "Well," said the boy, "if it can't be helped, then give me a quart of rye and a quart of wheat and I'll try." 

He received what he asked for and placed it in the kneading trough, which he took across the water. When he reached the other side, he began spreading the grains along the shore, and eventually managed to lure the ducks into the trough, rowing back as fast as he could. Just as he reached the middle, the troll came out and saw him. "Are you the one who has taken my seven silver ducks?" it shouted. "Yes, I am!" the boy replied. "Will you come again?" the troll asked. "That's quite possible," said the boy. 

When he returned to the king with the seven silver ducks, he became even more liked at the palace, and the king himself said it was a good deed; but his brothers became even more furious and envious of him. They then concocted a story for the stable master, claiming Askeladden had said he could obtain the troll's bedspread, which was made with alternating squares of silver and gold, for the king whenever he wished. The stable master was quick to tell this to the king, who said to the boy, "Your brothers have told me you've claimed you can acquire the troll's bedspread with the silver and gold squares, and now you must do it, or else lose your life." Askeladden responded that he had neither thought nor said such a thing, but it was of no use, and he requested three days to think it over.

At the end of three days, he rowed across again in his trough and watched and waited. Eventually, he saw the bedspread being hung out to air, and once it was safely inside again, Askeladden snatched it and rowed back as quickly as possible. When he was midway across, the troll came out and saw him. "Is it you who took my seven silver ducks?" the troll shouted. "Yes, I am!" answered the boy. "And have you taken my bedspread with the silver and gold squares as well?" "Yes, I have!" replied the boy. "Will you be returning?" "It's possible," the boy said.

Returning to the palace with the bedspread, adorned with gold and silver squares, everyone liked him even more than before, and he became a servant to the king himself. His two brothers grew even angrier and to avenge themselves, they went to the stable master and said, "Now our brother claims he can get the king the golden harp the troll has, which makes everyone happy just by hearing it, no matter how sorrowful they are." Naturally, the stable master once again told the king, and the king summoned the boy: "If you said it, then you must do it. If you manage, you shall have the princess and half the kingdom; but if not, you shall lose your life."

"I've neither thought nor said such a thing," responded Askeladden. "But since there seems to be no other choice, I will have to try. However, I would like six days to contemplate." This wish was granted, but after six days, he had to proceed. He took a nail, a birch twig, and a candle stub in his pocket and rowed across, wandering back and forth for a while.

Eventually, the troll came out and caught sight of him. "Is it you who took my seven silver ducks?" it shouted. "Yes," replied the boy. "And you who took my bedspread with silver and gold squares?" asked the troll. "Yes," said the boy. The troll seized him and dragged him into the mountain. "Now daughter," he said, "I've caught the one who took my silver ducks and my bedspread with the silver and gold squares; now put him in the goat pen, so we can fatten him for a feast." She willingly agreed and placed him there, where he stayed for eight days, during which he received all the finest food and drink he could wish for in abundance.

After eight days, the troll told his daughter to go down and cut him in the little finger to see if he was fat. She went to the goat pen. "Show me your little finger!" she demanded, but Askeladden stuck out the nail, which she pricked. "Oh no! He's as hard as iron still," said the troll's daughter when she returned to her father, "we can't eat him yet." Another eight days passed, with the same result, except this time Askeladden put forth the birch twig. "A little better," she said upon returning to the troll, "but he is still as tough as wood." After yet another eight days, the troll again instructed his daughter to check if he was fat.

"Show me your little finger!" the troll's daughter ordered upon reaching the goat pen that time; Askeladden put forward the candle stub. "He's suitable now," she declared. "Is that so?" said the troll; "then I'll go out and invite guests for the feast; meanwhile, you will slaughter him, roast half, and boil the other half." Once the troll had left, the daughter started sharpening a big long knife. "Are you going to use that to slaughter me?" asked the boy. "Yes," she said. "But it's not sharp," said the boy; "I think I should sharpen it, so you can take my life more easily." She let him have the knife, and he began to hone it.

"Let me try it on your hair braid," suggested the boy; "it seems sharp enough now." She allowed it, but as soon as he grabbed her braid, he bent her head backwards and sliced it off, then cooked half and roasted the other half, placing it on the table. He put on her clothes and sat down in the corner. When the troll returned with the guests, he asked his daughter—thinking it was her—to join them at the table. "No," said the boy, "I don't want any food; I feel so gloomy and downhearted."

"Oh, you know a cure for that," the troll suggested, "take the golden harp and play!" "Yes, where is it now?" asked Askeladden. "You know that—you used it last; it's hanging there over the door!" The boy didn't need to be told twice; he took it, then went out and started playing. Just as the trough had been pushed out, he rowed away, making the water swirl around it.

After some time, the troll began to think the daughter was taking too long outside, so he went to check on her. There he saw the boy in the trough, far, far out on the water. "Is it you who has taken my seven silver ducks?" the troll shouted. "Yes," said Askeladden. "Is it you who has taken my bedspread with the silver and gold squares, too?" "Yes," said Askeladden. "And have you now taken my golden harp with you?" screamed the troll. "Yes, indeed I have," replied the boy. "Didn't I eat you up, though?" "No, it was your daughter you ate," answered the boy. Upon hearing this, the troll became so furious he exploded, and Askeladden rowed back, bringing a heap of gold and silver, as much as the trough could carry.

When he returned to the king's castle with the golden harp, he received the king's daughter and half the kingdom, just as the king had promised. However, he was kind to his brothers, believing they had only meant well with what they had told him.
