Once upon a time, there was a cottager who had three sons. He had no inheritance to give them, nothing to set them to, nor did he know what to do with them; so he told them they could take up whatever they desired and travel wherever they wished, and he would gladly see them off on their journey. He accompanied them until they reached a place where the road split into three paths, and there they each took a different route; with that, the father said his farewells and returned home. What happened to the two eldest, I never heard, but the youngest went both far and long. One night, he was crossing a great forest when a terrible storm arose; it blew and snowed so fiercely that he could hardly keep his eyes open, and before he knew it, he had completely lost his way and couldn’t find neither road nor path. But as he wandered, he finally saw a light shining far away in the woods. He thought he must try to get there, and after much struggle, he did.

The light came from a large building where the fire was still burning brightly inside. He could tell they hadn’t gone to bed yet. He went in and found an old woman bustling about. "Good evening, Mother!" said the boy. "Good evening!" the old woman replied. "What dreadful weather it is out tonight," the boy remarked. "Indeed, it is," said the old woman. "May I stay here for the night?" the boy asked. "It’s not safe for you to stay here," the old woman said, "for if the folks find you when they return, they’ll kill both you and me." "What kind of people live here, then?" asked the boy. "Oh, it’s robbers and the like," she said. "They kidnapped me when I was a child and kept me here as their housekeeper," she explained. "I think I’ll stay here anyway," the boy said, "let happen what may, for I can't go out into such weather again at night."

"Well, suit yourself," the old woman said. The boy lay down on a bed but did not dare sleep, and before long, the robbers came. The old woman told them a stranger had arrived whom she couldn’t get to leave. "Did you see if he had any money?" the robbers asked. "Money, that poor soul!" the old woman replied. "He barely has clothes to wear." The robbers whispered among themselves, considering what to do with him, whether to kill him or otherwise.

In the meantime, the boy got up and engaged them in conversation, asking if they needed a servant boy, for he might like to serve them. "Yes," they said, "if you're willing to engage in the same work as us, then you can stay here." "What kind of work is that?" asked the boy, "For when I left home, my father allowed me to take up whatever I fancied." "Are you willing to steal?" the robbers asked. "Oh yes," said the boy; he thought that profession couldn't be too difficult to learn.

There was a man living a little distance away who owned three oxen; one of them he planned to take to the town to sell. The robbers knew this and told the boy that if he could steal the ox from him on his way without the man noticing and without harming him, he would be allowed to become their servant. The boy took a fine shoe with a silver buckle that he found there and set off. He placed it on the road where the man would pass with his ox and then hid under a bush in the forest.

When the man came by, he immediately spotted the shoe. "What a splendid shoe," he said, "If I had its mate, I'd take it home, and maybe once for all I'd see my wife in a good mood!"—for he had a wife so wicked and mean that it wasn't long between the beatings he received. But he thought having just one shoe would be useless, so he left it and went on. The boy seized the shoe and hurried through the forest to get ahead of the man, placing the shoe on the road again. When the man with his ox reached it, he regretted leaving the mate behind and not taking it with him. "I suppose I better go back and fetch the other," he said to himself and tied the ox to the fence. "At least then I'll have a good pair of shoes to calm the wife down for a while."

He searched high and low for the shoe, but he couldn't find it, and eventually, he had to return with the one he had. While he did this, the boy took the ox and went away. When the man returned and saw the ox was gone, he began to wail and lament, fearing that his wife would kill him when she found out. Then it occurred to him to go home, take another ox, and head to town with it without letting his wife know, to get a good price for it. He did just that, without his wife knowing, and the robbers knew it well, for they had their spies out. They told the boy that if he could steal this one too without the man noticing and without causing harm, he would be their equal.

The boy thought it would be easy. This time, he took a rope and hanged himself under his arms in the middle of the road. When the man came with his ox and saw this apparition hanging there, he felt uneasy. "What a poor soul to hang yourself there," he thought. "Well, you can hang for all I care, I can't revive you," he said and went on with his ox. The boy climbed down from the tree, took a shortcut, got ahead of the man, and hung himself in the road again. "Did you truly think so hard that you hung yourself there, or is it just an illusion for me?" the man wondered. "Well, hang for all I care, whether you're a ghost or not." The boy repeated his trick a third time, hanging himself in the man's path once more.

When the man saw him again, he said to himself, "This is strange! Have they all really thought so hard to hang themselves, these three? No, I can't believe it's anything but a trick." Determined, he tied his ox and ran back to see if the others were truly hanging. While he searched the trees, the boy came down and took his ox away. When the man returned and saw the ox was gone, he was terribly distressed and lamented bitterly, but finally resigned himself to it. He thought the only thing to do was to go home, take the third ox, and sell it for more than it’s worth to ensure he got a good price for it.

The man did just that, without his wife noticing. The robbers, however, were well aware of this, and they told the boy that if he could steal the third ox in the same way, he would be the master of them all. The boy headed to the woods, and when the man came with the ox, he began to roar like a large ox deep in the forest. When the man heard this, he was overjoyed because he recognized the roar of Big Bull, thought he would find both of them again, and secured the third one, venturing into the woods in pursuit. Meanwhile, the boy took that ox as well.

When the man returned to find the third ox also missing, he was so distraught he didn’t dare go home for several days, fearing his wife would kill him. The robbers were not pleased that they had to acknowledge the boy as their superior. They pondered how to challenge his abilities and set out on a journey, leaving him alone at home.

As soon as they had gone, the boy drove the oxen onto the road so they returned to the man he had stolen them from, and the farmer was overjoyed, as one can imagine. Then the boy gathered all the horses the robbers possessed, loaded them with all the best things he found, such as gold, silver, clothes, and other fine items, and left a message for the old woman. He told her to send his regards to the robbers and thank them; now he was leaving, and it would be difficult for them to catch him. With that, he was off.

After traveling some distance, he eventually came upon the path he had taken to reach the robbers. When he arrived at his father's home, he dressed in an outfit he had found among the robber's possessions, looking like a general, and drove onto the estate as a grand figure. Then he went in and asked if he could take lodging there. No, he could not. "How am I to lend lodging to such a grand lord, I?" said the man, "I hardly have clothes to sleep on myself, and they are poor indeed."

"You've always been hard," the boy said, "and it seems you still are, since you won't lend lodging to your own son." "Are you my son?" said the man. "Don't you recognize me?" asked the boy. Yes, then he knew him. "But what have you done, to become such a man in such a short time?" asked the father. "Well, I'll tell you," said the boy. "You said I could take up whatever I wanted, so I apprenticed with some thieves and robbers, and now I've finished my training and become the master thief," said he.

Near the house where his father lived, there was a Judge who had such a large estate and so much money that he could hardly count it, and a daughter who was beautiful, fair, good, and kind. The master thief wished to have her, so he told his father to go ask the Judge for his daughter's hand on his behalf. "If he asks what profession I engage in, you can say I'm a master thief," said the boy.

"I think you're mad," said the father, "for you can't be sane to think of such folly." But the boy insisted that he must insist or he would use force. Finally, his father tearfully went to the Judge's door. "Well, my man, what troubles you?" asked the Judge. The father explained that he had three sons who had left one day, and he had promised they could travel where they wished and do whatever they pleased. "Now the youngest has come home and forced me to come to you and ask for your daughter's hand for him, claiming he is a master thief," cried the father.

"Rest easy, my good man," said the Judge, laughing, "and tell him from me that he must prove himself first. If he can steal the roast from the spit in the kitchen on Sunday while everyone is watching, he shall have my daughter; tell him that!" The father did so, and the boy thought the task would be easy. He procured three live hares, put them in a sack, and donned some rags to look as ragged and shabby as possible. He sneaked into the hallway looking like a ragged schoolboy, carrying his sack, one Sunday morning.

The Judge himself and all the household were in the kitchen, keeping watch over the roast. Just then, the boy released a hare, and it darted off around the yard. "Look at that hare!" they all shouted and went out to catch it. Yes, the Judge saw it too. "Oh, let it run," he said, "there's no point trying to catch that hare on the fly." Soon after, the boy released the second hare, and they saw it again, thinking it was the same one, so they all wanted to go and catch it, but the Judge insisted it would be futile.

After a short while, the boy released the third hare. That, too, they saw, again assuming it was the same one already running loose, and they hurried to catch it. "It sure is a strange hare, too, running like that," said the Judge, "let's see if we can catch it." He went out, and everyone followed, chasing the hare, with no good outcome. Meanwhile, the master thief took the roast and made off with it, and where the Judge found another roast for dinner, I do not know, but hare roast he certainly did not have, even though he ran himself both sweaty and tired.

At dinner, the Pastor came over, and when the Judge told him about the trick the master thief played on him, he ridiculed the Judge to no end. "I can't imagine how such a thing could happen to me," said the Pastor. "Well, watch out," replied the Judge, "perhaps he'll visit you before you know it." But the Pastor remained confident and mocked the Judge for letting himself be tricked by such a man.

Later in the afternoon, the master thief returned, wanting the Judge's daughter as promised. "You'll need to accomplish another task first," the Judge sweet-talked him, "because what you did today wasn't much. Couldn't you do a good trick on the Pastor? He's inside, making fun of me because I've been fooled by someone like you." The master thief said he could do that easily. He disguised himself as a bird, donned a large white sheet, broke off goose wings and attached them to his back, and climbed up into a large maple tree in the Pastor's garden.

When the Pastor returned home that evening, the boy began to cry out, "Mr. Lars! Mr. Lars!"—as the Pastor's name was Mr. Lars. "Who is calling me?" the Pastor inquired. "I am an angel sent from Heaven to announce that you shall be taken up alive to the Kingdom of God due to your piety," the master thief declared. "Next Monday evening, prepare to embark, for I shall fetch you in a sack. All your gold, silver, and the vain things of this world shall you assemble in a heap in your living room."

The Pastor thanked and knelt before the angel. The following Sunday, he delivered a farewell sermon, elaborating on the news that an angel from God had appeared in the large maple tree in his garden, announcing he would be taken alive to the Kingdom of God due to his piety. He preached so fervently that all in the church, young and old, wept bitterly. On Monday, the master thief returned as an angel, and the Pastor knelt once more, thanking before being put into the sack.

Once inside, the master thief dragged him along both over obstacles and stones. "Oh! Ow!" cried the Pastor in the sack, "where am I being taken?" "This is the steep path leading to the Kingdom of Heaven," said the master thief, tugging him along, almost beating him to death. At last, he tossed him into the Judge’s goose house, where the geese nipped and pinched him, almost killing him.

"Ouch, ouch, ouch! Where am I now?" asked the Pastor. "Now you are in purgatory, being cleansed and purified for eternal life," replied the master thief, took all the gold, silver, and fine things the Pastor had amassed in his living room, and left.

The following morning, when the maid came to release the geese, she heard the Pastor lamenting in the sack inside the goose house. "In Jesus' name, who is it, and what ails you?" she asked. "Oh, are you an angel from Heaven? Let me out and allow me to return to Earth, for this place is worse than Hell; the little demons are pinching me with tongs," the Pastor replied.

"I’m no angel, heaven forbid!" said the girl, helping the Pastor out of the sack, "I'm just the Judge's goose keeper, and it must be those little demons who pinched you, Father." "Oh, that was the master thief! Oh, my gold and my silver and the fine clothes!" the Pastor cried, despaired, and ran home, leaving the girl thinking he had lost both wit and reason.

When the Judge heard what had happened to the Pastor, he had both been in purgatory and on the steep path, he laughed so hard he was near his wits’ end. But when the master thief came and claimed the Judge’s daughter, as promised, he sweet-talked him and said, "You must perform another piece of artistry for real proof of your skill. I have twelve horses in the stable," he explained, "and I will place twelve men on them, one on each. If you can steal the horses out from under them, then I will see what I can do."

"Yes, that might be possible," said the master thief, "if I indeed receive your daughter." "Yes, if you accomplish that, I will do my best," said the Judge.

The master thief went to a shop and bought brandy, pouring a sleeping potion into one flask but real brandy in another. He hired eleven men to sit behind the Judge's barn at night. For fair words and payment, he borrowed a ragged old jacket and a coat from a local hag, took a staff in hand and a bag on his back, and hobbled towards the Judge's stable as evening approached.

When he arrived, the stablemen were watering the horses for the night, deeply engrossed in their work. "What the devil do you want?" one of the stable boys asked the crone. "Oh dear me! It’s so cold!" said the old woman, shivering and fussing. "Oh dear me! It’s so cold that one might freeze to death!" she chattered, her teeth clattering hard. "Oh Lord, may I sit within the stable door?" she pleaded.

"You'll meet the devil sooner! Get lost, if the Judge sees you here, we'll get our dance," one said. "Oh, the poor old crook!" said another who had some pity for her, "the old hag can surely sit there; she won't do any harm." The others argued among themselves, but she gradually crept further into the stable, eventually sitting behind the door, and thus no one paid her any mind.

As night wore on, a little chill set in for the men perched on horseback. "Oh dear! It’s the devil’s own cold," said one, flapping his arms. "Yes, I’m freezing like I’m shaking," another said. "If only we had some tobacco!" someone suggested. One had a bit of a plug, which they shared; not much for one, but they chewed and spat. It helped a bit, but eventually, they were all cold again.

"Oh dear," said one of them, shivering. "Oh dear," said the old woman, clacking her teeth so it echoed in her mouth, and took out a bottle of plain brandy. She shook so much that the fluid swirled inside the flask, and she drank such a gulp that it sounded in her throat. "What is in your flask, old woman?" one stableman asked.

"Oh, it's just a drop of brandy, father," she replied. "Brandy? What kind? Let me have some! Let me have some!" they all cried in unison. "Oh, it’s so little I have," complained the old woman, "you won’t even get wet in the mouth from it." But they demanded it adamantly, she handed out the sleeping potion, carefully not shaking any longer while administering it, ensuring each had his fill, the twelfth scarcely drank before the first was snoring.

The master thief threw off the tramp rags and lifted each stableman, as light as a feather, onto the beams. He called for his eleven men and galloped away with the Judge’s horses. When the Judge came out to check on the stablemen at dawn, they were starting to wake: they kicked the beams so hard the splinters flew, some toppled down, while others remained seated there like fools. 

"Yes," said the Judge, "I can see at a glance who's been here. What a bunch of useless men you are, sitting here letting the master thief steal the horses right from under you!" And they got a scolding for not watching properly. Later in the day, the master thief returned, told him how it had been done, and demanded the Judge's daughter as promised. The Judge gave him a hundred dollars and said he would need to perform a still greater feat.

"Do you think you could steal the horse from under me while I ride it?" the Judge asked. "Ah, it might be done," replied the master thief, "if indeed I receive your daughter." The Judge told him he would ride out to a large exercising ground one day. The master thief acquired an old worn-out nag, concocted a harness from twigs and broom-knots, purchased an aged rattletrap cart with a large barrel, and persuaded a penniless old hag to lie inside the barrel with her mouth over the tap-hole; he placed his finger in—assuring no harm would come—and promised her ten dollars for each extraction beyond the first.

Disguised in tatters, with soot smudged on his face, wearing a wig and a bushy beard made from goat hair, he headed for the field where the Judge had been riding for some time. Upon arrival, the cart crept along, moving so slowly it hardly budged. It waddled bit by bit until it stood completely still, then shifted slightly again, and so moved drearily that the Judge could never suspect it was the master thief. The Judge rode to him and asked if he'd seen anyone lurking around the woods. 

"No," said the apparent old man, "I haven't seen a soul." "Listen," said the Judge, "if you ride into the woods and look closely for anyone lurking, I’ll lend you my horse, and a good drink coin for your trouble." “No, I can’t do that, sir,” said the figure, “I’m headed to a wedding with this mead barrel, but the tap fell out on the way, and now I have to hold my finger in the hole.”

“Ride on,” said the Judge, “I’ll take care of the horse and barrel.” Reluctantly, the old figure agreed, but he urged the Judge to be swift in placing his finger over the tap hole as soon as his was withdrawn. The Judge promised his best efforts, and with that, the master thief mounted the horse, rode away, and time wore on and wore on, with no sign of return.

Eventually, the Judge grew weary of holding his finger plugged in the tap hole, so he pulled it out. "Now I demand ten more dollars!" screamed the woman within the barrel. The Judge realized the trick and walked home; but as he trekked, they met him with his horse, for the master thief had already been there.

The next day, the master thief went to the Judge to claim his daughter. The Judge sweet-talked again, dished out two hundred dollars, and said he had one final test. He claimed: “Can you manage to steal the sheet from our bed and my wife's nightgown?”

"That might not be impossible," said the master thief, "if only I could have your daughter assuredly." As night fell, the master thief went out, cut down a hanged thief from the gallows, hoisted him up over his shoulder, acquired a tall ladder, which he placed against the Judge's bedroom window, climbed up, and nudged the dead figure up and down, like a glare from outside.

“There’s the master thief, mother!” the Judge nudged his wife. “Now I’ll shoot him,” declared the Judge, picking up a rifle he placed before the bed. “Oh no, you shan’t,” pleaded the wife, “you’ve called him here yourself.” "Yes, I’ll shoot, mother," he replied, lying there aiming and aiming. Sometimes the head appeared, giving him a sliver of sight, other times disappearing again; at last, he took a steady shot, the dead dropping with a thud upon the earth.

The master thief descended briskly. "Well, I am indeed the high authority," the judge mused, "but tongues wag fast in others’ mouths, it would be awkward should anyone glimpse this body; best I dig a grave." "Do as you see fit, father," said the woman. The Judge rose, trod down, and the moment he stepped through the door, the master thief slipped inside and dashed up to the woman. “Well now father,” she voiced — assuming it was her husband —, “are you finished so soon?”

"Oh yes, I put him in a pit," he replied, "heaped a little earth upon, just to cloak him; such weather it is outside; I’ll do a better job later. Lend me the sheet to dry off, I’m all bloody muddled handling him.” She obliged with the sheet. "I’ll need your gown as well," he continued, "since the sheet just won’t reach, you see."
