Rich Per Trader

Once upon a time, there was a man called Rich Per Trader because he had traveled around with goods and amassed a lot of money, becoming a wealthy man. This Rich Per had a daughter, and he held her in such high regard that all the suitors who came were turned away, as he believed none were good enough for her. As this happened with everyone, eventually no one came, and as the years passed, Per feared she would never marry. "I really wonder," he said to his wife, "why no one comes to court our daughter, who is so wealthy? It would be quite strange if no one wanted her, for she has money, and she'll inherit more. I think I should travel to the astrologers and ask them who she is supposed to marry, for no one here seems to have a clue." "How can the astrologers know that?" asked his wife. "Oh, they read everything in the stars," said Rich Per.

He took a lot of money with him to the astrologers and asked if they would look at the stars and tell him who his daughter should marry. The astrologers looked at the stars, but they said they couldn't see it. However, Per urged them to look more closely and finally reveal it to him, promising to pay them well. The astrologers then examined the stars more carefully and said his daughter was destined to marry the miller's child who had just been born at the mill located right below Rich Per's farm.

Per gave the astrologers a hundred dollars and returned home with the news. He thought it was too absurd that his daughter should marry someone so newly born and of such low status. He also mentioned this to his wife, saying, "I wonder if they would sell me the boy so we can get rid of him." "Yes, I think they would," said the wife, "they are poor people."

Per went down to the mill and asked the miller's wife if she would sell him her son; she'd receive a hefty sum for him. She refused outright. "I don't see why you won't," said Rich Per; "you live in poverty, and the boy won't change that, I assume," but she was so fond of her son that she wouldn't part with him.

When the miller came in, Per proposed the same deal to him and promised him six hundred dollars for the boy, allowing them to buy a farm and avoid grinding for others and going hungry when there was no water for the mill. The proposition greatly appealed to the miller, and after discussing it with his wife, Rich Per acquired the boy. The mother cried and grieved, but Per comforted her by promising the boy would be well cared for. They had to promise not to inquire about his whereabouts, as he planned to send him far away to foreign lands to learn new languages.

Once Per Trader returned home with the boy, he had a small chest crafted, so finely made it was a delight to behold. He sealed it with pitch, placed the miller's boy inside, locked it securely, and set it adrift on the river, thinking, "Now I am rid of him." However, as the chest sailed far down the river, it ended up in the water intake of another mill further downstream, halting the millstones.

The miller went to see what had stopped the mill and found the chest, taking it upstairs. When he brought it home at noon, he said to his wife, "I wonder what's inside this chest; it came rushing down the chute and stopped the mill today." "We'll soon find out," said the wife, "the key is in the lock; just open it." When they opened it, there lay the most beautiful child one could see, and they were delighted and decided to keep the boy, for they had no children of their own and were too old to expect any.

As time passed, Per Trader began to wonder again if any suitors would come for his daughter, who was so rich and had so much money. But none came, so he traveled again to the astrologers and offered them a large sum if they could tell him who his daughter should marry. "We already told you she would marry the miller's boy from down there," the astrologers said. "Yes, that's all well and good," said Per Trader, "but he met with a mishap and no longer lives; if I could just find out who my daughter should marry, I'd gladly give you two hundred dollars."

The astrologers looked at the stars once more but grew angry this time, saying, "She will indeed marry the miller's boy whom you set adrift on the river and tried to destroy, for he still lives and is at the mill located so and so far downstream." Per Trader gave them two hundred dollars for the prophecy and thought about how he might finally rid himself of the miller's boy.

The first thing Per Trader did upon returning home was to visit the mill. By then, the boy had grown enough to stand on the church floor, and he worked in the mill, helping out. He had turned into a handsome lad. "Couldn't you let me have that boy of yours?" Per Trader asked the miller. "No, I can't," the miller replied; "I have raised him as my own, and he has been of great assistance, so I can now have help and benefit from him in the mill, for I am growing old and feeble myself." "Yes, it's the same with me," said Per Trader, "and that's why I would like someone I could train to trade. If you let me take him, I'll give you six hundred dollars, so you could buy a farm and live in peace in your old age." When the miller heard this, he let Per Trader take the boy.

Together they traveled far and wide dealing in goods until they came to an inn by the edge of a large forest. From there, Per sent the boy home with a letter to his wife—it wasn't far across the forest—and instructed him to tell her to follow the instructions in the letter as soon as possible.

But in the letter, it stated she should immediately build a large fire and throw the miller's boy on it, and if she didn't, she herself would be burned alive.

The boy set off through the forest with the letter. As evening approached, he reached a distant farm in the woods and went inside; he found no one there, but there was a made-up bed in one room, which he lay across. He placed the letter under his hatband, laying his hat over his face.

When the robbers—twelve of them inhabited the farm—came home and saw the boy lying on the bed, they wondered who he might be. One of them took the letter, opened, and read it. "Hm, hm," he said, "it's Per Trader who's in the field; now let's play him a trick, for it would be a pity if the old scoundrel managed to end such a young handsome lad." So the robbers wrote another letter to Per Trader's wife, attaching it under the hatband while the boy slept, instructing her to immediately hold a wedding for the daughter and the miller's boy, give them horses, cattle, and tools, and set them up properly on the farm in the hills. If it wasn't done by the time he returned, she would face punishment for it.

The next day the robbers let the boy go, and when he returned home and gave her the letter, he said he was to give greetings from Per Trader and tell her to do as instructed in the letter forthwith. "You must have served him well," said Trader's wife to the miller's boy, "since he now writes like this; for when he left, he was so angry with you, he didn't know how to get rid of you." She promptly arranged the wedding and set them up with horses, cattle, and various tools on the farm up in the hills.

Not long after, Per Trader returned home, and the first thing he asked was if she had done what he had written in his letter. "Yes, I thought it odd, but I dared not do otherwise," she said. Per then inquired where their daughter was. "You surely know where she is," said his wife; "she's with him on the farm up in the hills, as it said in the letter." Per Trader became so furious upon hearing how it had all happened, and upon seeing the letter, he was ready to burst, and immediately set off up to the farm to see the young couple. "Well, young man, you've got my daughter," he said to the miller's boy, "but if you want to keep her, you'll have to go to the Dragon of Dybenfart and get me three feathers from his tail, for the one who possesses them can have whatever they wish." "But where can I find him?" his son-in-law asked. "That’s your problem; I don't know," said Per Trader.

The boy set forth bravely, and after traveling for some time, he came to a king's court. I’ll go in and ask for directions here, he thought, for such people know the world better than others, and perhaps here I can find directions. The king asked where he was from and what his purpose was. "I am going to the Dragon of Dybenfart to get three feathers from his tail, if only I could find him," said the boy. "That will take some luck," the king thought, "as I've never heard of anyone returning from him," he said. "But if you do meet him, could you ask him why I can't get clean water in my well, which I've dug over and over, yet I never get clean water in it?" "Yes, I'll do that," said the boy.

At the king's court, he lived well and received both provisions and money when he left. As evening approached, he arrived at another king's court. When he entered the kitchen, the king came out and asked where he was from and what his mission was. "I am headed to the Dragon of Dybenfart to get three feathers from his tail," said the boy. "That will require some luck," said the king, "for I've yet to hear of anyone coming back from him. But should you find him, could you ask on my behalf where my daughter is, who disappeared many years ago," he asked. "I've searched and inquired about her from all churches, yet no one has been able to tell me anything." "I'll do my best," said the boy. At the king's court, he lived well, and when he left, he received both provisions and money.

As evening approached again, he arrived at yet another king's court. Here the queen came out to the kitchen and asked where he was from and what his mission was. "I am going to the Dragon of Dybenfart to get three feathers from his tail," he said. "There will certainly need to be some luck for that," said the queen, "as I've never heard of anyone coming back from him. But should you find him, could you ask on my behalf where I can find my golden keys, which I have lost?" "I'll do that," said the boy.

After walking some more, he came to a large, wide river. As he wondered how he could cross it or if he should follow it along, an old stooped man approached and asked where he was headed. "I am indeed going to the Dragon of Dybenfart if only someone could tell me where to find him." "I can tell you that," said the man, "for I ferry those who are headed to him; he lives right above here; once you climb the hill, you’ll see his castle, and if you get a chance to speak with him, could you ask on my behalf how long I'll be ferrying here?" "I'll be sure to do that," said the boy.

The man carried him across the river on his back, and when he reached the hilltop, he saw the castle and went inside. The princess was home alone. "Dear me, do Christian folk dare to come here?" she said. "No one has been here since I came, and it's best you hurry along, as soon as you can, for when the dragon comes home, he'll smell you, gobble you up on the spot, and make me unhappy too." "No," said the boy, "I can’t leave until I've got three feathers from his tail." "You'll never get those," said the princess.

But the boy refused to leave; he wanted to wait for the dragon, get the feathers from his tail, and answers to his questions. "Alright, since you're so insistent, I'll see if I can help you," said the princess. "Try lifting the sword that hangs on the wall over there." No, the boy couldn't even budge it. "Well, then you need a drink from this flask," said the princess.

After sitting for a while, he had to try again; this time, he could just about move the sword.

"You need another drink," said the princess, "and then you can tell me your business." So, he took another drink and explained that the king had asked him to inquire, why he couldn't get clean water from his well, another king had asked where his daughter had gone, who had disappeared many years prior, and the queen had requested he ask the dragon where her golden keys had disappeared to, and finally, the ferryman had asked how long he should keep ferrying folks across.

When he took hold of the sword again, he could lift it, and after another drink, he could swing it. "To avoid immediate danger from the dragon, you must hide under the bed," said the princess as evening drew near, "for he will soon be returning; you must stay there so quietly that he doesn't notice you. Once we've gone to bed, I'll ask him the questions, but you must listen carefully and mark his answers well; don't come out until everything is quiet, and the dragon falls back to sleep; then quietly crawl out and take the sword with you, and as soon as he gets up, make sure to chop off his head in one blow, and quickly snatch the three feathers; otherwise, he'll tear them out himself, so no one else can benefit from them."

A little while after the boy had crawled under the bed, the dragon arrived home. "I smell Christian man's bones!" said the dragon. "Ah, yes, a raven flew by with a man's bone in its beak and sat on the roof," said the princess, "that must be what you're smelling." "Oh, I see," said the dragon. The princess then set out the meal, and once they'd eaten, they retired to bed. But after resting for a while, the princess began tossing and turning and suddenly shrieked. "Oh, dear!" she exclaimed.

"What ails you?" asked the dragon. "Oh, I'm sleeping so restlessly," said the princess, "and I had such a strange dream." "What did you dream about?" asked the dragon. "I dreamed a king came here and asked you how to get clean water in his well," said the princess. "Oh, he should know that himself," said the dragon; "he needs to dig around the well and remove the old rotting log at the bottom, then he'll have clean water again; but now just lie still and don’t dream anymore."

After a while, the princess began tossing and turning again and suddenly woke with a start: "Oh, dear!" "What now? What's happening again?" said the dragon. "Oh, I'm sleeping so restlessly, and I had such a strange dream," said the princess. "You dreamt a lot," said the dragon; "what did you dream about now?" "I dreamed a king came here and asked you where his daughter was, the one who disappeared many years ago," said the princess. "That’s you," said the dragon, "but he'll never see you again. Now I beg you to let me have a peaceful night and stop dreaming, or I'll break your ribs."

The princess hadn’t been lying still long before she once again tossed and turned and suddenly shrieked: "Oh, dear!" "Now, is it happening again? What's the matter now?" said the dragon, by now so wild and sleep-deprived he was near fury. "Oh, don't be angry," said the princess; "but I had such a strange dream." "That’s a lot of dreaming! What did you dream now?" asked the dragon. "I dreamed a queen came here and asked if you could tell her where she might find her golden keys, which she lost." "Oh, she can look among the bushes where she lay back when she knows," said the dragon. "Now leave me be for another dream."

Finally, they slept for a time, but soon the princess began to toss and turn again and suddenly shrieked: "Oh dear!" "You won't get better until I’ve cracked your skull," the dragon retorted; by then, he was so furious he was spitting sparks. "What’s happening again now?" "Oh, don’t be angry with me; I can't help it," said the princess, "but I had such a strange dream." "There’s no end to this dreaming either," said the dragon, "what did you dream about now?" "I dreamed that the ferryman by the river down here asked you how long he should be ferrying across," said the princess. "The foolish beast can put that to rest," said the dragon, "when someone comes wanting to cross, throw them into the river and say: You ferry now until relieved! But let me have peace from your dreams now, else there’ll be another dance."

The princess let him sleep peacefully. But as soon as everything settled and the boy heard the dragon snoring, he crawled out. Before daylight, the dragon rose, but he was barely on his feet before the boy lopped off his head and snatched three feathers from his tail. There was much joy, and both the boy and the princess took as much silver, gold, money, and other treasures as they could carry. When they reached the river, they distracted the ferryman with so many goods to ferry across that he forgot to ask about what the dragon had said until everything, and the boy and the princess, were across.

"That's right, you," he said as they were about to leave, "did you ask the dragon about what I asked you?" "Yes," said the boy, "he said when someone comes to cross, toss them in the river and say: You ferry now until relieved! — then you'll be free." "Oh, curse you!" said the ferryman, "had you said that before, you could have freed me."

When they arrived at the first king's court, the queen asked if he had asked the dragon about her golden keys. "Yes," replied the boy, whispering to the queen, "he said you should look among the bushes where you lay back when you know." "Hush, hush, say nothing," said the queen, and gave the boy a hundred dollars.

When he reached the second king’s court, the king asked if he had talked to the dragon about what he requested. "Yes," said the boy, "I did, and here is your daughter." The king was so joyful that he would gladly have given the miller’s boy the princess and half the kingdom. But since he was already married, he received two hundred dollars, horses, a carriage, and as much gold and silver as he could carry.

Arriving at the third king’s court, the king came out and asked if he inquired with the dragon about what he asked. "Yes," the boy said, "he stated that you should dig up the well and remove the old rotten log at the bottom, then you’ll have clean water." The king gave him three hundred dollars.

From there, he traveled straight home, and he was so adorned in gold and silver, and so splendid that he shone and sparkled; now he was much richer than Per Trader. When Per received the feathers, he had nothing against the marriage; but when he saw all the wealth, he asked if there was so much at the dragon’s place. "Yes," said the boy, "there was much more than I could bring back, enough for many cartloads; and if you wish to travel there, there should certainly be enough for you too." Indeed, Per Trader wanted to travel there.

The boy gave him directions so well that he didn’t have to ask along the way; "but the horses," said he, "you should leave on this side of the river, for the old ferryman will surely help you across."

Per set off, taking a vast supply of provisions with him and many horses; but he left them behind at the river, just as the boy had suggested. The ferryman carried him on his back, and when they were halfway across, he threw him into the middle of the river and said: "Now you can ferry here until relieved!" And if no one has relieved him, Rich Per Trader is still there ferrying to this very day.
