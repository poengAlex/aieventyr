HERREPER

There was once a poor couple who had nothing except for three sons. The two oldest, I do not know their names, but the youngest was called Per.

When their parents died, the children were to inherit from them, but there was nothing else to receive apart from a pot, a flat griddle, and a cat. The oldest, who was supposed to have the best share, took the pot. “When I lend out the pot,” he said, “I always get permission to scrape it.” The second one took the flat griddle. “Because when I lend out the griddle,” he said, “I always get a sample of flatbread.” But the youngest had no choice; if he wanted anything, it had to be the cat. “If I lend out the cat, I won’t get anything in return,” he said. “If the cat gets a splash of milk, she wants it all to herself. But I’ll bring her with me anyway; it would be a shame to leave her behind.”

So the brothers went out into the world to seek their fortune, each taking his own path.

After the youngest had been walking a while, the cat said to him: “You will be repaid for not leaving me behind in the old house to fend for myself. Now I shall go into the forest and catch some remarkable animals, and then you will go up to the King’s castle that you see over there, to the King, and say that you are bringing him a small gift. When he asks who sent it, you must say it is from ‘Herreper.’”

Indeed, Per had not waited long before the cat came with a reindeer from the woods. She had leapt onto the reindeer’s head and, after sitting herself between its antlers, said: “If you don’t go straight to the King’s castle now, I’ll scratch out your eyes.” The reindeer did not dare do otherwise.

When Per arrived at the castle, he went into the kitchen with the reindeer and said, “I’m here with a little gift for the King, if he will not consider it beneath him.”

The King came out to the kitchen and, seeing the fine reindeer, was quite pleased. “But my dear friend! Who sends me such a grand present?” asked the King.

“Oh, it is from Herreper,” said the boy.

“Herreper?” said the King. “Now where should I say he lives?” For he thought it a shame he did not know such a distinguished man.

But the boy wouldn’t tell him; he said he didn’t dare, for his master had forbidden him. So the King gave Per a good sum of money as a tip and asked him to send his thanks and greetings back home for the wonderful gift.

The next day, the cat went again into the woods, jumped onto the head of a deer, sat between its eyes, and threatened it into going to the King’s castle. Once more, Per went into the kitchen and said he was bringing the King a little gift, if it pleased him to accept it. The King was even more delighted with this deer than with the reindeer, and again asked who could possibly send such a grand present. “It is from Herreper,” said the boy. But when the King asked where Herreper lived, he got the same response as the day before. This time, the King gave Per even more money as a tip.

On the third day, the cat arrived with a moose. When Per stepped into the royal kitchen, he said he had yet another little gift for the King, if he would be so good as to accept it. The King came straight into the kitchen, and upon seeing the magnificent moose, he became so happy he did not know which foot to stand on. That day, he gave Per many, many more coins—surely a hundred silver pieces. The King dearly wanted to know where Herreper lived and asked all sorts of questions, but the boy said he did not dare give that information, for his master had strictly forbidden it.

“Then ask Herreper to visit me,” said the King.

“Yes, I shall do so,” said the boy.

When he came out of the castle and met the cat again, Per said: “Well, you’ve gotten me into quite a predicament. Now the King wants me to visit, but I have only these rags on my back!”

“Ah, don’t be afraid of that,” said the cat. “In three days, you shall have horses and a carriage, and clothes so fine that gold will drip from you. Then you can certainly visit the King. But everything you see at the King’s place, you must say that you have far grander and finer things at home. Do not forget that.”

No, Per would remember, he thought.

When the three days had passed, the cat came with a carriage, horses, clothes, and all that Per needed—all of it so splendid that no one had seen the like before. Then they traveled on, and the cat jumped along with him. The King received him most cordially, but whatever the King showed him, Per said it might be nice enough there, but he had even finer and grander things at home. The King did not care much for these claims, but Per stuck to his story. Finally, the King became so angry he could hardly keep his temper. “Now I shall go home with you,” said the King, “to see if it is truly so much better and grander. But if you’re lying, God help you—I’ll say no more!”

“You’ve really landed me in trouble now,” Per said to the cat. “The King wants to come home with me, and it won’t be easy to find my home.”

“Don’t worry about that,” said the cat. “Just follow after me, where I run ahead.”

So first Per drove after the cat, then came the King with all his retinue.

After they had driven a good stretch, they came to a huge flock of beautiful sheep, with wool so long that it nearly touched the ground.

“If you will say that this flock of sheep belongs to Herreper when the King asks,” the cat said to the shepherd, “you shall receive this silver spoon.” (She had taken the spoon from the King’s castle.)

Yes, he would gladly do that.

When the King arrived, he said to the shepherd boy, “I have never seen such a large and splendid flock of sheep! Who owns them, my little lad?”

“They belong to Herreper,” said the boy.

A little farther on, they came to a huge herd of beautiful spotted cows; they were so well fed that they shone in the sunlight.

“If you will say that this herd belongs to Herreper when the King asks,” the cat said to the herding girl, “you will receive this silver ladle.” (She had also taken this from the King’s castle.)

“Yes, indeed,” said the girl.

When the King arrived, he was amazed by this fine herd, saying he had never seen such a lovely collection of cattle before. He asked the girl who tended them to whom these spotted cows belonged.

“Oh, they belong to Herreper,” said the girl.

They traveled on a bit more and came to an enormous herd of horses. They were the finest looking horses one could imagine, big and strong, with six of each color—red, dun, and blue.

“If you will say that this herd of horses belongs to Herreper when the King asks,” the cat said to the stable boy, “you shall have this silver measuring cup.” (She had also taken it from the King’s castle.)

Yes, he would do that, said the boy.

When the King arrived, he was astonished by this splendid herd—he had never seen anything like it, he said. Then he asked the stable boy who owned the red, dun, and blue horses.

“They belong to Herreper,” the boy answered.

They drove on a good distance more and came to a castle. First there was a gate of bronze, then one of silver, and then one of gold. The castle itself was made of silver and so shiny that it dazzled the eyes, for the sun shone upon it just as they arrived. They went inside, and the cat told Per to say that this was where he lived. Inside, the castle was even more splendid than it was outside. Everything was made of gold—chairs, tables, and benches. Once the King had gone around and seen everything, high and low, he felt utterly humbled. “Yes, Herreper does have it grander than I. No point denying that,” he said, and he wanted to leave at once. But Per asked him to stay and have supper, which the King did, though he was surly and sour the whole time.

While they were seated at the table, the Troll who owned the castle came and pounded on the gate.

“Who is it that eats my food and drinks my mead like pigs in there?” the Troll roared.

The moment the cat heard him, she ran out to the gate.

“Wait a moment, and I will tell you how the farmer handles his winter rye,” the cat said, and she began explaining at length how it was done. “First, the farmer plows his field,” she said. “Then he fertilizes it. Then he plows it again.”

Just then the sun rose.

“Look behind you, and you will see a beautiful maiden,” said the cat to the Troll.

So the Troll turned around, and as soon as he caught sight of the sun, he burst apart.

“Now all of this is yours,” the cat said to Herreper. “Now you must chop off my head. That is the only thing I ask of you in return for what I have done for you.”

“No,” said Herreper. “I won’t do that at all.”

“Yes,” said the cat, “if you do not, I will scratch out your eyes.”

So Herreper had to do it, no matter how unwilling he was. He cut off the cat’s head, and at once she turned into the loveliest Princess anyone had ever seen, so beautiful that Herreper fell head over heels for her.

“Yes, this splendor was mine long ago,” said the Princess. “But that Troll put a curse on me so that I had to live as a cat with your parents. Now you may do what you will—whether you want me to be your Queen or not, for now you are King of this entire kingdom,” said the Princess.

Oh, there was no question that Herreper wanted her for his Queen. So there was a wedding feast for eight days, and after that I was no longer there with Herreper and his Queen.
