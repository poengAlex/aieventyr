Fugl Dam

Once there was a King who had twelve Daughters, and he was so fond of them that they always had to stay by his side. But every midday, when the King slept, the Princesses went out for a walk. One day, as the King took his midday nap and the Princesses went for their usual stroll, they suddenly disappeared and did not come back. There was great sorrow throughout the whole country, and the King was the saddest of all. He sent out messengers both in his own kingdom and in foreign lands, and he had notices read out at all churches and all the bells rung across the country. But the King’s daughters were gone and stayed gone, and no one knew what had happened to them—so they understood they must have been taken into the mountain by magical means.

It was not long before this was heard far and wide, in both towns and villages, yes, even across many regions and foreign lands. And so the rumor reached a distant King in another land, who had twelve Sons. When they heard about the twelve Princesses, they begged their father for permission to travel out and search for them. He was reluctant to let them go—he was afraid he would never see them again—but they bowed to him and pleaded for so long that he had no choice but to let them depart. He fitted out a ship for them and chose Sir Red as the captain, for he was well acquainted with sailing.

They sailed around for a long time, and went ashore in every land they reached, searching and asking after the Princesses. But they neither heard nor learned anything about them. Then only a few days remained and they would have been sailing for seven years. One day there came a fierce storm, the kind of weather that made them believe they would never reach land again, and everyone had to work so hard that they had no sleep while the storm lasted. But when it came to the third day, the wind died down, and suddenly it was completely calm. They were all so exhausted from their work and from the harsh weather that they immediately fell asleep—except for the youngest Prince, who had no peace and could not sleep at all.

While he paced to and fro on the deck, the ship came near a small island, and on that island ran a little dog, barking and growling at the ship, as though it wanted to come aboard. The Prince stood on deck, calling and whistling to the dog; the more he called, the more it barked and growled. He felt sorry for the poor thing, believing it might have come from a ship that had been wrecked in the storm. Yet he thought he could not rescue it alone, for he believed he could not manage the boat by himself, and everyone else was sleeping so soundly he did not wish to wake them because of a dog. But the weather was calm and clear, so he thought, “I will go ashore anyway, and save the dog.” He tried lowering the boat—this turned out to be easier than he had expected. He rowed ashore and went over to the dog. But each time he tried to grab it, the dog jumped aside, and in this way it led him on until he found himself inside a grand castle, before he even realized it. There, the dog turned into a beautiful Princess, and on a bench nearby sat a man so large and ugly that the Prince was quite frightened.

“Don’t be afraid,” said the man—though the Prince became more afraid still, when he heard the man’s voice—“for I know well what you want: there are twelve Princes in your group, and you are searching for the twelve Princesses who disappeared. I know where they are; they are with my master. Each of them sits on her own golden chair, brushing his hair, because he has twelve heads. You have already sailed for seven years, but you must sail for seven more before you will find them. You could easily stay here,” he added, “and marry my daughter. But first you must kill him. He is a harsh master to us all, and we all long to be rid of him. Once he is dead, I will be King in his place. But test now whether you can swing that sword,” said the Troll.

The Prince reached for an old rusty sword hanging on the wall, but he could hardly move it.  
“Then take a swig from this flask,” said the Troll.

After doing so, he could shift the sword. When he took another swig, he could lift it. And after yet another, he could swing it as easily as if it were a baking spatula.

“When you now go back aboard,” said the Troll Prince, “You must hide the sword well in your berth, so that Sir Red does not see it. He isn’t strong enough to swing it, but he will hate you for having it and will seek to take your life. Then, when another seven years have passed, and only three days remain,” he went on, “the same thing will happen as now: a fierce storm will come upon you, and when it passes, you will all grow sleepy. At that moment, you must take the sword and row ashore. You will come to a castle guarded by all kinds of creatures—wolves, bears, and lions—but do not be afraid; they will all bow down before you. Once inside, you will find him sitting in a magnificent chamber, splendid and well-dressed, but with twelve heads. The Princesses will be sitting on their own golden chairs, each brushing one of his heads—and you can well guess they don’t enjoy that work. You must hurry and chop off each head before he wakes. If he sees you, he will swallow you alive.”

The Prince returned to the ship with the sword, and remembered well all that he had been told. The others were still asleep, and he hid the sword in his berth so that neither Sir Red nor any of the others saw it. Soon the wind rose again, and he woke the others, saying it would not do for them to sleep any longer now that there was fair wind for sailing. None of them noticed that he had been gone.

When the seven years were ended, short of three days, all came about as the Troll had said. A powerful storm blew for three days, and when it had passed, they all grew sleepy from their efforts and lay down. But the youngest Prince rowed ashore, and all the guards bowed to him, so he came to the castle. Entering the chamber, he found the Great Troll asleep, just as the Troll Prince had said, and the twelve Princesses sitting on their chairs, each brushing one of his heads. The Prince signaled for the Princesses to move aside. They pointed at the Troll, signaling the Prince to go away or leave them be. But he kept gesturing that they should move aside, so finally they understood he was there to save them. They quietly slipped away one by one while he swiftly chopped off the Troll King’s heads, so that, in the end, blood flowed like a large stream.

Once the Troll was slain, the Prince returned aboard with his sword. He felt he had done enough by himself, but he could not deal with removing the body alone—so he woke the others and told them it was a shame that they were sleeping while he had discovered the Princesses and saved them from the Troll. The others laughed at him and said that he must have slept just as soundly as they had and only dreamed that he had done such heroic deeds; if anyone had rescued the Princesses, it was far more likely to be one of them. But the youngest Prince explained everything that had happened, and when they followed him ashore, they saw first the stream of blood, then the castle, the Troll, the twelve heads, and the Princesses—and so they realized the truth of his words. Now they helped him drag the body and heads into the sea. Everyone rejoiced at having the Princesses safe, and no one was happier than the King’s daughters themselves, who no longer had to sit all day brushing the Troll’s hair. From all the gold, silver, and costly items in the castle, they took as much as the ship could carry. Then they all went aboard—both Princes and Princesses.

After they had sailed a while, the Princesses remembered in their joy that they had forgotten their golden crowns in a cupboard at the castle. They asked if someone would fetch them, but no one volunteered except for the youngest Prince, who said: “Ah well, I have risked my life often enough, so I can go and get the golden crowns too, if you will just lower the sails and wait for me to return.” This they agreed to do. But as soon as he was far enough away that they could not see him, Sir Red—who wanted to have the highest rank and to marry the youngest Princess himself—said that there was no use waiting around, for it was obvious that the youngest Prince would never return. He also reminded them that the King had given him, Sir Red, full power and command, so off they must sail, and they should all declare that he was the one who had rescued the Princesses. If anyone said otherwise, that person would pay with his life. The other Princes did not dare disobey, so they set sail.

Meanwhile, the youngest Prince rowed ashore, went up to the castle, found the cupboard with the golden crowns, and struggled and strained until he got it down into his boat. But when he came to where he could see the ship, it was gone. Since he could not spot it anywhere, he understood what had happened. There was no use rowing after it, so he had to turn around and land onshore again. He was afraid to spend the night alone in the castle, but there was nowhere else to stay, so he gathered his courage, locked all the doors and gates, and lay down in a room where there was a bed made up for sleeping. He was indeed frightened, and grew even more frightened after lying there for a while, for the walls and roof started creaking and cracking as if the entire castle might split apart. Suddenly, something as heavy as a whole load of hay dropped next to his bed. Then it became quiet again; but he heard a voice that told him not to be afraid and said:

“I am Fugl Dam,  
and I will help you on your way.  
But when you wake tomorrow morning,  
you must go to the storehouse for four barrels of rye for me to eat for breakfast,  
or I cannot do anything.”

When he woke up, he saw an unbelievably large bird, with a feather in its neck as thick as a young sapling. The Prince went to the storehouse and fetched four barrels of rye for Fugl Dam. Once the bird had eaten them, it asked the Prince to hang the cupboard with the golden crowns on one side of its neck, then take as much gold and silver as would balance that weight and hang it on the other side. The bird then asked him to climb on its back and hold on tightly to the great feather in its neck.

And off they flew, rushing through the air. Before long, they passed the ship. The Prince had wanted to go aboard and fetch his sword, fearing someone might see it—and the troll had warned that must not happen—but Fugl Dam said they should leave it be. “Sir Red will likely never see it,” said the bird, “but if you go aboard, he’ll try to take your life, for he wants the youngest Princess for himself. But you need not worry about her, for each night she lays a bare sword in front of her in bed.”

Eventually, they came to the Troll Prince, who received the Prince so well that it was beyond measure, for the Prince had slain his master and made him King in his place. The Troll Prince would gladly have given him his daughter and half the kingdom. But the Prince longed so greatly for the youngest of the twelve Princesses that he found no rest, wanting time and again to be off and away. The Troll urged him to be patient a little longer, saying they still had nearly seven years of sailing to go before they would return home. About the Princess, the Troll said the same as Fugl Dam: “You need not worry for her; she places a bare sword in front of her in bed each night. And if you doubt me,” said the Troll, “go aboard when they pass by here, look for yourself, and fetch the sword; it must come back to me anyway.”

So, when the ship sailed past once more, they again had foul weather, and when the Prince came aboard, everyone was asleep. Each Princess lay with her own Prince, but the youngest lay alone with a bare sword in front of her in bed, and on the floor in front of the bed lay Sir Red. The Prince took the sword and rowed back to shore without anyone noticing he had been aboard.

Still the Prince could not rest, and he wanted more than ever to leave. At last, when scarcely three weeks remained of the seven years, the Troll King said: “Now, you can prepare for your homeward journey, since you do not wish to stay with us. I will loan you my iron boat, which sails by itself once you say, ‘Boat, go forward!’ (Inside the boat is an iron club. You must lift that iron club when you see the ship straight ahead, and they will receive such a gust of wind that they’ll forget to watch for you. When you come alongside the ship, lift the iron club again, and there will be such a storm that they’ll have other concerns than looking for you. After you pass them, lift the club a third time. Be sure always to set it down gently, or the storm will be so terrible that it will swallow both you and them. Once you reach the shore, you need do no more with the boat than push it out, turn it around, and say, ‘Boat, go the way back that you came!’).”

When he departed, he received so much gold and silver, and such fine clothes and linens—sewn by the Troll Princess during his long stay—that he was far richer than any of his brothers. He had barely sat down in the boat and said “Boat, go forward,” before it set off. And when he saw the ship dead ahead, he lifted the club. Then they got such a powerful following wind that it never crossed their minds to look for him. Once he was alongside the ship, he lifted the iron club again, and this time there arose such a gale and towering waves that foam whirled around the ship and the sea washed over the deck. They had more than enough to keep them busy, so they paid no attention to him. And once he was safely beyond, he lifted the iron club for the third time, and they were so overwhelmed that they hardly had time even to think. He reached land far, far ahead of the ship. After taking all his belongings off the boat, he pushed it back out, turned it around, and said, “Boat, go the way back that you came!” And away the boat went.

He then dressed himself like a sailor and went up to a wretched little hut where an old woman lived. He told her he was a poor sailor, that his large ship had been wrecked, and that he was the only survivor. He asked if she would let him stay there with the few possessions he had rescued.

“Heavens, help me,” said the old woman, “I can’t house anyone; you see how it is here. I don’t even have enough bedding for myself, let alone for someone else.”

“That’s all right,” said the sailor. As long as he had a roof over his head, he did not care how he slept. She could not refuse him that, when he was willing to make do as best he could.

That evening, he moved in his belongings, and no sooner had he done so than the old woman, who wanted news she could gossip about, began to ask him what he was doing there, where he came from, where he had been, where he was going, what those things he had brought might be, what his business was, and whether he had heard anything about the twelve Princesses who had disappeared so many years ago—and a lot more besides, which she had plenty of time to ask. But he told her he was feeling weak and had such a headache from the awful weather that he could not recall a single fact. She would have to leave him in peace for several days, until he recovered from all the work he had done, and then she would learn all she wanted and more. The next day, the old woman again began to question him, but still the sailor complained of such a bad headache that he could not give her any clear answers. Yet every so often, he let slip a few words suggesting that he knew a little about the King’s daughters. At once, she dashed off to tell every gossip in the area what she had heard; and then they came flocking, one after another, wanting to know news about the Princesses: had he seen them, were they coming soon, were they on their way, and more besides. He claimed again that he was still in pain from the storm, so he could not be sure of all the details. But he did say that, unless they had gone down in the fierce weather, they would arrive in about fourteen days or maybe sooner—though he was not certain they were even still alive, for while he had indeed seen them, they might well have sunk since then.

One of these women ran to the royal palace and told the King that there was a sailor in the old woman’s hut who had seen the Princesses, and that they would likely arrive in about fourteen days—maybe in as few as eight. When the King heard this, he sent for the sailor, commanding him to come and relate the story himself.

“But I’m not fit to appear before him,” said the sailor. “I don’t have suitable clothes to wear in his presence.” However, the King’s messenger insisted he must come nonetheless. The King would and must speak with him, no matter what he looked like, for no one else had been able to give any clue about the Princesses.

So, he went to the palace and was brought before the King, who asked whether it was true that he had seen the Princesses.

“Yes, I did see them,” answered the sailor. “But I cannot say for sure if they still live, for the weather was so fierce that we were shipwrecked. If they are indeed still alive, they will be here in about fourteen days—maybe sooner.”

Upon hearing this, the King was almost overcome with joy. When it grew close to the time the sailor had named, the King journeyed to the shore in all his finery, and joy spread throughout the land as the ship arrived carrying the Princesses, the Princes, and Sir Red. No one was happier than the old King, who had gotten his daughters back. All the eleven older Princesses were lively and content, but the youngest—who was to be wed to Sir Red—weeped and was continually sorrowful. The King strongly disapproved of this and asked why she was not as cheerful as her sisters. She had nothing to be sad about now that she was free of the Troll and was to marry such a man as Sir Red. But she dared not say anything, for Sir Red had threatened to take the life of anyone who revealed what had truly happened.

One day, while they sat sewing on their wedding clothing, someone entered in a large sailor’s coat, carrying a peddler’s chest on his back, and asked if the Princesses wanted to buy any finery for the wedding—he had so many rare and precious things of gold and silver. Indeed, it was likely they did. They looked at his wares, and they looked at him, for they thought they recognized both him and several of the items he displayed.

“He who brings so many fine things,” said the youngest Princess, “surely has something even finer—perhaps something that might suit us even better.”

“That might well be,” said the peddler.

But the others hushed her and reminded her of Sir Red’s threats.

Some time later, as the Princesses sat by the window, they saw once more the Prince in his large sailor’s coat, carrying a chest on his back as he walked up to the palace. When he entered the great hall, he opened the chest for the Princesses to see. And when they recognized their own golden crowns, the youngest Princess said, “I think it’s right that the one who truly rescued us gets the reward he deserves—and that is not Sir Red, but rather the man who has brought us these golden crowns. He is the one who saved us.” Upon that, the Prince threw off his sailor’s coat and stood before them, more splendid than any of the others. The old King then had Sir Red put to death. Only then was there the greatest joy in the royal palace: one sister took her rescuing Prince, and another took hers, and so on, until they were all wed. And their wedding festivities were reported far and wide, so that they were known across twelve kingdoms.
