The Twelve Wild Ducks

There was once a Queen who went out driving in wintertime, right after fresh snow had fallen. After traveling a ways, she started to get a nosebleed and had to step out of the sleigh. While standing by the fence, looking at the red blood on the white snow, she remembered that she had twelve sons and no daughter, and so she said to herself, “If only I had a daughter as white as snow and as red as blood, then it might as well be the same to me what happens to my sons.” Hardly had she spoken those words before a troll witch appeared by her side. “A daughter you shall have,” said the troll witch, “and she will be as white as snow and as red as blood. But in return, your sons shall be mine—although you may keep them at home until the child is christened.”

When the time came, the Queen gave birth to a daughter who was indeed as white as snow and as red as blood, just as the troll witch had promised. Therefore they named her Snow White and Rose Red. There was great joy in the royal palace, and the Queen was so happy that nothing could match it. But she remembered her promise to the troll witch, so she had a silversmith make twelve silver spoons—one for each prince—and then had him make one more, which she gave to Snow White and Rose Red.

As soon as the Princess was christened, the Princes were turned into twelve wild ducks and flew away, never to be seen again. They were gone, and they stayed gone. The Princess grew up tall and beautiful, but she was often so strange and sad. No one could understand what was wrong with her.

One evening, the Queen herself was also sad, for it weighed heavily on her mind whenever she thought of her sons. She said to Snow White and Rose Red, “Why are you so sad, my child? If something is wrong, speak up! If there is anything you want, you shall have it.”

“Ah, I just feel so lonely,” said Snow White and Rose Red, “because everyone else has siblings, but I’m all alone. That’s why I am sad.”

“You’ve had siblings too, my child,” said the Queen. “I once had twelve sons who were your brothers, but I gave them all away in order to have you,” she said, and then she told the whole story.

Upon hearing this, the Princess found no peace. No matter how much the Queen cried or pleaded, it was no use; she had to go, and she would go, to look for her brothers, for she felt responsible for everything that had happened. Finally, she left the royal palace. She walked and she walked, through the wide world, farther than anyone would have thought such a fine young lady could manage on foot.

After a long walk through a vast forest, on one particular day she grew tired and sat down on a grassy mound, where she fell asleep. She dreamed that she walked even deeper into the forest and came upon a small timber cabin, and there she found her brothers. As soon as she awoke, she saw a narrow path in the green moss right in front of her, leading deeper into the woods. She followed it, and after a good while she arrived at a little wooden house just like in her dream.

When she stepped inside, there was no one there. Yet there were twelve beds, twelve chairs, twelve spoons, and twelve of everything else. On seeing this, she became so happy—happier than she had been in many years—for she could tell at once that her brothers lived there, and that these must be their beds, chairs, and spoons. She set to work warming the room, sweeping, making the beds, cooking food, tending and decorating everything as best she could. Once she had cooked and prepared a meal for all of them, she ate herself, but left her own silver spoon on the table. Then she crept under the youngest brother’s bed and lay down there.

No sooner had she done this than she heard a rushing and whistling through the air, and in flew all twelve wild ducks. But the moment they crossed the threshold, they turned into Princes.

“Oh, how warm and cozy it is in here!” they said. “God bless whoever lit the fire and cooked such a fine meal for us!” Then each one took a silver spoon, ready to eat.

But once they each had taken a spoon, there was still one left on the table, exactly like the others, so much so that they couldn’t tell it apart. They looked at one another in wonder. “This must be our sister’s spoon,” they said. “If her spoon is here, she cannot be far away.”

“If it’s our sister’s spoon, and she’s somewhere about, then she must be put to death, because she’s the reason we suffer this curse,” said the eldest Prince, and the Princess heard this from beneath the bed.

“No,” said the youngest Prince, “it would be wrong to kill her for that. She can’t help the hardships we suffer—and if anyone is to blame, it must be our own mother.”

So they began searching for her high and low, and at last they looked under all the beds. When they came to the youngest Prince’s bed, they found her and pulled her out.

The eldest Prince again insisted she should be put to death, but she wept and pleaded for her life: “Oh dear Lord, do not kill me,” she said. “I’ve roamed for years looking for you, and if there’s any way I can save you, I would gladly give my own life.”

“Well, if you can save us,” they said, “you shall live. If you only will it, you certainly can.”

“Yes, just tell me how it can be done,” said the Princess, “and I shall do whatever it takes.”

“You must gather cottongrass,” said the Princes. “You must card it, spin it, and weave it into cloth. When that’s done, you must cut and sew twelve caps, twelve shirts, and twelve cloths—one set for each of us. While you do this, you may not speak, laugh, or cry. If you manage to do that, we shall be freed.”

“But where am I to gather enough cottongrass for so many caps, shirts, and cloths?” asked Snow White and Rose Red.

“We’ll show you,” said the Princes, and they led her to a vast marsh where cottongrass grew as far as the eye could see, swaying in the wind and shimmering in the sun like snow from a great distance away.

Never had the Princess seen such a huge amount of cottongrass before. She began picking and gathering it as fast and as thoroughly as she could, and when she came home in the evening, she carded and spun it into yarn. Time passed in this manner; she kept gathering and carding cottongrass and also taking care of the Princes: cooking their meals and making their beds. In the evening they would return home like wild ducks, but at night they were Princes again, and in the morning they flew off, wild ducks once more through the day.

Then one day, while she was out on the marsh gathering cottongrass—if she was not mistaken, it was likely the last time she needed to go there—the young King who ruled that land rode by on a hunting trip. He stopped, amazed to see such a lovely maiden in the marsh gathering cottongrass, and he asked her who she was. But receiving no reply, he wondered even more at her. He felt so drawn to her that he decided to bring her back to his castle to marry her. So he ordered his servants to pick her up and place her on his horse. Snow White and Rose Red wrung her hands and made signs to them, pointing at the sacks containing all her work. Realizing that she wanted them brought along, the King told his servants to gather up the sacks too. Once that was done, the Princess reconciled herself to the idea, because the King was both kind and handsome, and he was gentle and friendly with her in all ways.

But when they reached the castle and the King’s stepmother, the old Queen, saw Snow White and Rose Red, she grew furious and jealous at her beauty. So she said to the King, “Can’t you see that this person you’ve brought home and intend to marry is a witch? She neither speaks, laughs, nor cries.”

However, the King paid no attention to such remarks and held the wedding, marrying Snow White and Rose Red, and they lived in great happiness and splendor. Still, she never forgot to sew on the shirts for her brothers.

Before a year passed, Snow White and Rose Red gave birth to a baby Prince. The old Queen grew even more enraged and jealous. So late one night, she sneaked into Snow White and Rose Red’s chamber as she slept, took the baby, and threw it into the snake pit. Then she cut the Queen’s finger, smeared the blood around her mouth, and went to the King.

“Come and see,” she said. “Look what sort of person you’ve made your Queen. She has eaten her own child.”

The King was so shocked he nearly broke down in tears and said, “Well, it must be true, since I see it with my own eyes. But let us hope she won’t do such a thing again. I’ll spare her this time.”

Before the end of the next year, Snow White and Rose Red gave birth to another son. Exactly the same thing happened. The King’s stepmother became even angrier and more jealous. She sneaked in again at night, took the child, and threw him into the snake pit. She cut the Queen’s finger and smeared blood around her mouth, then told the King that she had eaten this second child too. The King was utterly distraught and said, “Well, I must believe it, seeing it with my own eyes. But she surely won’t do it again, and I will spare her this time as well.”

Before another year had passed, Snow White and Rose Red gave birth to a daughter, and the old Queen did precisely the same thing—she tossed the baby into the snake pit while its mother slept, cut the mother’s finger, and smeared blood around her mouth. Then she told the King, “Now you can finally see that what I’ve been saying is true—that she is a witch, for now she’s devoured even her third child.”

This caused the King such grief that there was no end to it. He could no longer spare her but ordered that she be burned alive at the stake. When the fire was blazing, and she was about to be cast on it, she made signs for them to place twelve wooden planks around the fire, and on these she laid the cloths, the caps, and the shirts for her brothers. However, in the youngest brother’s shirt the left sleeve was missing—there hadn’t been enough time to finish it. No sooner had she done this than there was a rush and roar in the air, and twelve wild ducks came flying over the forest. Each one seized its clothing in its bill and flew off with it.

“Now do you see?” said the wicked old Queen to the King. “This proves she’s a witch. Hurry and burn her before the fire dies down!”

“Oh,” said the King, “we have plenty of firewood—we have a whole forest to take from. I’d like to wait a bit, for I want to see how this turns out.”

At that moment, the twelve Princes came riding in, splendid and tall as could be—but the youngest Prince had a duck’s wing instead of a left arm.

“What is going on here?” asked the Princes.

“My Queen is to be burned, charged with being a witch who has eaten her own children,” said the King.

“She hasn’t eaten her children,” said the Princes. “Speak now, Sister, for you have freed us; now free yourself!”

So Snow White and Rose Red spoke up and told everything that had truly happened: that every time she lay in childbirth, the King’s stepmother had sneaked in at night, taken the child away, cut her finger, and smeared the blood around her mouth. The Princes then took the King to the snake pit, where the three children were found playing happily among snakes and toads—healthier, lovelier children could not be imagined.

The King picked up the children and brought them to his stepmother, asking her what she believed should be the punishment for anyone so cruel as to betray an innocent Queen and three such blessed children.

“That person should be torn apart between twelve wild horses, so that each horse takes its share,” replied the old Queen.

“You have pronounced your own sentence,” said the King, “and so it shall be done to you.” And then the wicked Queen was tied between twelve untamed horses, each tearing away its portion of her.

Afterward, the King took Snow White and Rose Red, their three children, and the twelve Princes, and they all journeyed home to her parents. They told them everything that had happened, and there was joyous celebration throughout the kingdom, for at last the Princess was saved and had also saved her twelve brothers.
