THE SEVEN COLTS

Once upon a time there was a poor couple who lived in a shabby little hut far out in the forest. They barely had enough to live from one day to the next, and they had three sons. The youngest did nothing but lie around, digging in the ashes, and so people called him the Ash Lad.

One day, the oldest boy said he wanted to go out and look for a job; his parents gave him permission at once, and off he went into the world. He traveled and traveled all day. Toward evening, he came to the Royal Manor. There stood the King on the steps, asking where he was headed.

“Ah, I’m just out looking for work, Your Majesty,” said the boy.

“Will you work for me and tend my seven colts?” asked the King. “If you can watch them for a whole day and then tell me in the evening what they eat and drink, you shall have the Princess and half the kingdom. But if you fail, I’ll cut three red stripes from your back.”

The boy thought this sounded like an easy task—certainly something he could manage.

At dawn the next morning, the stable master let out the seven colts. They sprang away, and the boy ran after them. You can well imagine how they went, across mountains and valleys, through thickets and brush. Before long, the boy became tired, and after running a while more, he’d had quite enough of chasing colts. Just then, he came to a cleft in the mountain where an old woman sat spinning on a hand spindle. When she saw how the sweat poured off him as he ran, she called out: “Come here, my sweet boy, so I can comb and comfort you!” The boy was glad to do so; he sat down beside the old woman, laid his head in her lap, and she tended him the whole day while he lazed around.

Toward evening, the boy got ready to leave. “I may as well head home,” he said. “There’s no point going to the King’s palace now.”

“Wait here until dusk,” said the old woman. “The colts will come galloping back this way, and you can run home behind them. No one will ever know you’ve lazed about here all day instead of tending the colts.”

When they came by, she gave the boy a flask of water and a clump of moss. “Show these to the King,” she said, “and tell him these are what the seven colts eat and drink.”

“Have you tended the colts well and faithfully all day?” asked the King that evening when the boy returned.

“Yes, indeed,” said the boy.

“Then surely you can tell me what my seven colts eat and drink?” the King asked.

The boy showed him the water flask and the moss he had gotten from the old woman. “Here is their food, and here is their drink,” the boy said.

At that, the King understood full well how the boy had been ‘tending’ the colts. He became so angry that he ordered his men to drive the boy out immediately—but first, they had to cut three red stripes from his back and rub salt in the wounds.

When the boy came home, you can imagine how he felt. He said that was the first and last time he’d ever go looking for work.

The next day, the second son said he wanted to try his luck in the world. His parents told him no and pointed to his brother’s back, but he insisted, and at length they allowed him to go. Like his brother, he traveled until evening, arriving at the King’s palace, where he found the King on the steps asking where he was headed. When the boy replied that he was out looking for work, the King told him he could come and tend the seven colts under the same conditions and for the same reward—and risk—as his brother. The boy was quite willing; he was sure he could watch the colts and tell the King what they ate and drank.

Early in the morning, at daybreak, the stable master let the seven colts out again. Off they went across mountains and valleys, and the boy followed behind. But things went exactly as with his brother. After chasing the colts for a long time, sweating and growing tired, he passed that same mountain cleft where the old woman sat spinning on her hand spindle. “Come here, my sweet boy, so I can comb and comfort you!” she shouted. The boy was only too happy to oblige, letting the colts run on as they pleased, lying around in the cleft all day.

When the colts returned toward evening, the old woman once again gave him a clump of moss and a flask of water to show the King. But when the King asked, “Can you tell me what my seven colts eat and drink?” and the boy held out the moss and the flask, saying, “Here is their food, and here is their drink,” the King became furious. He ordered his men to cut three red stripes from the boy’s back, rub salt in the wounds, and drive him out at once. When he got home, he told everyone how things had gone and vowed he would never again venture out for work.

On the third day, the Ash Lad wanted to leave and try his hand at tending the seven colts as well. Everyone laughed at him. “Things went badly enough for us,” they said, “and you think you’ll do any better, you who do nothing but lie in the ashes and dig?”

“Well, I’m going anyway,” said the Ash Lad. “I’ve made up my mind,”—and no matter how much they laughed and his parents pleaded, he wouldn’t change it. Off he went.

After traveling all day, he, too, arrived at the King’s palace at dusk. Out on the steps stood the King, asking where he was from and where he was headed. The Ash Lad told him where he came from and that he was the brother of the two who had tried to tend the colts. He then asked if he might have a turn at it.

“Ugh!” the King exclaimed angrily, for just thinking about those brothers annoyed him. “If you’re their brother, you can’t be much better than they were. I’ve had enough of your kind!”

“All the same, since I’m here, I’d like to try too,” said the Ash Lad.

“Well, if you’re so eager to have the skin flayed from your back, be my guest,” said the King.

“I’d rather have the Princess,” replied the Ash Lad.

First thing at dawn, the stable master let the seven colts out again—off they went, over mountains and valleys, through thickets and brush, with the Ash Lad running after. After quite a while, he came to the mountain cleft where the same old woman sat spinning on her hand spindle. Again, she called out, “Come here, my sweet boy, so I can comb and comfort you!”

“Kiss my behind! Kiss my behind!” shouted the Ash Lad, hopping and running while keeping hold of the last colt’s tail.

Once he was safely past the cleft, the youngest colt said, “Climb on my back, for we still have a long way to go,” and so he did.

They traveled a great distance. “Do you see anything now?” asked the colt. “No,” said the Ash Lad. They went on some more. “Do you see anything now?” asked the colt again. “No, not yet,” answered the Ash Lad. After they had gone a very long way, the colt asked once more, “Do you see anything now?” “Yes,” said the Ash Lad, “now I can see something white—it looks like a giant birch stump.” “That’s where we’re going,” said the colt.

When they came to the stump, the oldest colt broke it aside, and there was a door where the stump had stood. Inside was a small room with little more than a tiny fireplace and a couple of stools. Behind the door hung a large rusty sword and a little jug.

“Can you swing that sword?” asked the colt.

The Ash Lad tried, but he couldn’t. So he took a sip from the jug—once, then again, and then yet another time—and afterward, he could handle the sword as though it weighed nothing at all.

“Now, take this sword with you,” said the colt. “On your wedding day, you must use it to cut off all our heads, and then we’ll become princes again, as we once were. We are brothers to the Princess you shall have, once you tell the King what we eat and drink. It was a terrible troll that cast this spell on us. When you cut off our heads, be sure to place each one by the tail of the body it belonged to—that way the spell will lose its power over us.”

The Ash Lad promised to do so, and they set off again.

They traveled farther still. “Do you see anything now?” asked the colt. “No,” said the Ash Lad. They went on a while. “How about now; do you see anything?” “No,” said the Ash Lad again. They went many, many miles across mountains and valleys. Then the colt asked once more, “What about now—do you see anything yet?” “Yes,” answered the Ash Lad, “now I see something blue in the distance, like a long strip.” “That’s a river,” said the colt, “and we must cross it.”

They crossed the water by way of a long, fine bridge. Once on the other side, they traveled another long distance. Finally, the colt asked again whether the Ash Lad saw anything. This time he saw something dark in the distance, like a church tower. “Yes,” said the colt, “we’re going in there.”

When they arrived at the churchyard, the colts turned into human beings again, looking like royal princes, in fine clothes that shone brightly. They went into the church and received bread and wine from the priest who stood before the altar. The Ash Lad went inside too, and after the priest had laid his hands on the princes and blessed them, they left the church again, and so did the Ash Lad. But on his way out, he took with him a bottle of wine and a piece of communion bread. As soon as the seven princes stepped into the churchyard, they turned into colts again. The Ash Lad climbed onto the youngest colt’s back, and off they went the same way they had come, but much faster this time—over the bridge, past the hidden stump, and past the old woman spinning in the mountain cleft. They galloped so fast that the Ash Lad couldn’t hear the curses she shouted after him, but he caught enough to know she was furious.

It was almost dark by the time they returned to the King’s palace that evening, and the King himself stood in the courtyard to meet them.

“Have you tended my colts well and faithfully all day?” asked the King.  
“I’ve done my best,” replied the Ash Lad.

“Then you can surely tell me what my seven colts eat and drink,” the King said.

The Ash Lad brought out the communion bread and the wine bottle. “Here is their food, and here is their drink,” he said.

“Well done,” said the King. “You’ve tended them properly, so you shall have the Princess and half my kingdom.”

Then a wedding was arranged, and it was to be so grand that people both near and far would hear about it, the King said. During the wedding feast, as they sat at the bridal table, the groom stood up and left for the stable—there was something he had forgotten, he said, and needed to fetch. Down there, he did exactly as the colts had told him: He took his sword and cut off their heads, one after another, from the oldest colt to the youngest, making sure to place each head by the tail of its own body. As soon as he did, the colts turned back into princes. When he returned to the wedding hall with these seven princes at his side, the King was so happy that he both kissed and embraced the Ash Lad, and the bride loved him even more than before.

“You already have half the kingdom,” the King said, “and you shall have the other half when I die. My own sons can find lands and kingdoms for themselves—now that they are true princes again.”

So you can well imagine there was joy and revelry at that wedding!

I was there too myself, but no one had time to notice me. All I got was a sandwich, which I put on the oven, and the bread burned and the butter melted away, and I never got back so much as a crumb.
