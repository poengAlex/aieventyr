The Virgin Mary as Godmother

Far, far away in a large forest, there once lived a poor couple. The wife gave birth to a beautiful daughter, but because they were poor, they did not know how they could afford to get their child baptized. One day, the husband went out to look for godparents who could pay for the baptism themselves. He walked the entire day, to one person and then another, but though many were willing to be godparents, none felt they had enough money to offer for the ceremony. As he was heading home in the evening, he met a beautiful lady dressed in fine clothes, who looked so tender and kind. She offered to arrange for the child’s baptism, but afterward she wished to keep the child. The husband answered that he would first have to ask his wife what she thought; but when he got home and told her, she said plainly, “No.”

The next day, the man went out again, but just as before, no one would be the godparent if they had to pay for everything themselves, and all his pleading did no good. As he walked home again in the evening, he met the beautiful lady once more—she looked just as sweet—and she made the same offer once again. He told his wife what had happened, and she said that if he still could not find godparents for the child the next day, then they would have to let the lady have it, since she seemed so good and kind.

On the third day, the man went out and again found no godparents. When he met the lady that evening, he promised to let her have the child, provided she made sure it received both baptism and Christian upbringing.

In the morning, she came to where the husband lived, accompanied by two men, took the child, and went to the church with it, where the child was baptized. Then she brought the girl home with her, and the little one lived there for several years. The foster mother was always kind and good to her.

When the girl grew old enough to understand things, the foster mother prepared to travel. “You may walk about in every room you like,” she said to the girl, “just don’t go into the three chambers I’m about to show you.” And then she left. But the girl could not resist taking a little peek into one of the chamber doors, and—whoosh!—out flew a Star. When the foster mother came back and saw what the girl had done, she became angry and threatened to send her away. But the child wept and begged for forgiveness, and so at last she was allowed to stay.

Some time later, the foster mother had to travel again, and she forbade the girl once more to enter the two chambers where she had not yet been. The girl promised to be careful, but after some time spent alone, wondering and fretting about what might be in the second chamber, she could not resist glancing through the door to see inside, and—whoosh!—out flew the Moon. When the foster mother returned and saw that the Moon was gone, she became very sad and told the girl she could no longer keep her there, that she must leave. But the girl cried so bitterly and pleaded so beautifully for forgiveness that she was allowed to stay this time as well.

After a while, the foster mother needed to travel yet again, and this time she warned the girl, who was now half-grown, most seriously not to try to enter—or even peek into—that third chamber. But once the foster mother had been gone a while, and the girl had wandered about alone and become very bored, she thought, “No, it would be so exciting to see even a little bit of what’s inside that third chamber!” At first, she decided she would not do it, out of respect for her foster mother. But when she felt the temptation a second time, she could no longer resist—she felt she simply must look inside. She cracked open the door, and—whoosh!—out flew the Sun. When the foster mother came back and saw that the Sun had escaped, she was grief-stricken and said that now the girl could no longer remain with her. The foster daughter cried and begged even more beautifully than before, but it was no use.

“No, now I must punish you,” said the foster mother. “But you may choose: either you will become the most beautiful woman of all yet be unable to speak, or you will become the ugliest and be able to speak.” Still, she had to leave. The girl replied, “Then I would rather be beautiful.” And so she was, but from that moment on, she was mute.

Once she had left her foster mother, she walked and wandered through a vast, vast forest, which seemed to have no end. As evening drew near, she climbed a tall tree standing over a well and settled herself there to sleep the night. Nearby lay a castle, and early next morning, a maid came to fetch water from the well for the Prince’s tea. She saw the lovely face reflected in the water and thought it was her own. She then tossed aside her bucket, ran home, and patted herself on the back, proclaiming, “If I’m that beautiful, I am surely too good to go carrying water!” Another maid was sent out, but the same thing happened. She too returned home, saying she was too beautiful and too good to fetch the Prince’s water. So, finally, the Prince himself went, for he was curious to see what was going on. When he came to the well, he too saw the reflection and looked up at once. There, he spotted the beautiful young woman sitting in the tree. He coaxed her down and took her home with him, and insisted on making her his queen, simply because she was so lovely. But his mother, who was still alive, protested. “She cannot speak,” she said, “and she may very well be some sort of witch!” But the Prince would not rest until he could have her.

They lived together for a while, and she became pregnant. When it was time for her to give birth, the Prince set a strong guard around her. Yet at the very moment of birth, they all fell asleep. The foster mother appeared, cut the newborn’s little finger, smeared blood on the Queen’s mouth and fingers, and said to her, “Now you shall be as grieved as I was when you let the Star escape.” And then she disappeared with the child. When the guards awakened, they believed the Queen had eaten her own baby. The Prince’s mother wanted to have her burned, but the Prince loved her deeply and managed—just barely—to spare her from punishment.

The second time the young Queen was about to give birth, he set twice as many guards as before. Yet the exact same thing happened, except the foster mother said, “Now you shall be as grieved as I was when you let the Moon escape.” The Queen cried and pleaded—for whenever the foster mother arrived, she could speak—but it did no good. Now the Prince’s mother was more certain than ever that she should be burned, but again the Prince managed to spare her.

When the Queen was about to give birth for the third time, thrice as many guards surrounded her. Still, the same thing happened: while the guards slept, the foster mother came, took the child, cut its little finger, smeared blood on the Queen’s mouth, and said, now the Queen should be as sad as the foster mother had been when the Sun escaped.

No one could save the Queen this time; she had to be burned. But just as they led her to the pyre, the foster mother appeared, bringing all three children. She led two of them by the hand, carrying the third in her arms. She went to the young Queen and said, “Here are your children; now you shall have them back. I am the Virgin Mary, and just as sorrowful as you have been, I once was when you let the Sun, the Moon, and the Star escape. Now you have suffered enough for what you did, and from this time forward, you shall be able to speak again.”

One can easily imagine—but no one can truly describe—how overjoyed the Queen and the Prince were. They lived happily ever after from that day on, and the Prince’s mother also grew very fond of her new daughter-in-law.
