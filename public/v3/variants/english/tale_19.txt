Kari Trestak

Once upon a time, there was a King who had become a widower. After his Queen, he had a daughter who was so sweet and so lovely that no one could be kinder or more beautiful. He spent a long time grieving for the Queen, whom he had loved dearly, but eventually he grew weary of being alone and married a widow Queen, who also had a daughter. However, her daughter was as ugly and mean as the King’s daughter was kind and beautiful. The stepmother and her daughter were jealous of the Princess because she was so lovely; but as long as the King was around, they dared not harm her, for he cared greatly for his child.

Time passed, and war broke out with another kingdom. The King was called away to fight. As soon as he was gone, the Queen decided she could do whatever she pleased. She both starved and beat the Princess, and chased her from room to room. Finally, she thought everything in the house was too good for the Princess, so she set her to tending the livestock. Day after day, the Princess wandered the forests and the mountains, herding the animals. She had little or nothing to eat, growing pale and thin, and she almost always wept because she was so miserable.

In the herd, there was a large blue Bull that stayed shiny and sleek. Often, it came up to the Princess and let her stroke it. One day, as she sat again weeping sadly, the Bull came over and asked why she was so sorrowful. She said nothing but kept on weeping. “Yes,” said the Bull, “I know why, even if you won’t tell me. You cry because the Queen is cruel to you and wants to starve you. But you needn’t worry about food. In my left ear, you’ll find a cloth, and when you spread it out, you can have as many dishes as you wish.” So she did exactly that, and when she laid the cloth on the grass, it produced the most wonderful meal anyone could hope for: there was wine, mead, and sweet pastries. She soon grew healthy again, round and rosy-cheeked, which made the Queen and her scrawny daughter turn both blue and pale with anger. The Queen couldn’t understand how her stepdaughter could look so well on such poor rations, so she told one of her servants to follow the Princess into the forest and see how it all happened, for she suspected that someone among the staff might be feeding her.

The servant girl went into the forest and kept watch. She saw the Princess take the cloth out of the Bull’s ear, spread it on the ground, and enjoy all the wonderful dishes. Then the servant hurried home to tell the Queen.

About this time, the King returned victorious from the war with the other ruler. The whole palace was filled with joy, and no one was happier than the King’s daughter. But the Queen pretended to be sick and bribed the Royal Physician with a great sum of money to say that she could never recover unless she was given the flesh of the blue Bull to eat. Both the Princess and the palace servants begged the doctor to find something else that might help, for everyone was fond of the Bull, saying there was none like it in all the land. But it was no use—the Bull had to be slaughtered. There was no other remedy. When the Princess heard about this, she became very upset and went down to the barn to see the Bull. There it stood with its head hanging low, looking so mournful that she began to weep for it.

“Why are you crying?” asked the Bull.

She explained that her father had come home, that the Queen was ill, and that she had bribed the doctor to declare that the only cure was the flesh of the blue Bull. Now they were going to slaughter it.

“If they manage to kill me,” said the Bull, “it won’t be long before they kill you too. If you feel as I do, we’ll run away tonight.”

The Princess felt it was truly heartbreaking to leave her father, but living in the same house as the Queen was worse, so she promised the Bull that she would come.

That evening, when everyone else was asleep, she tiptoed down to the barn. The Bull took her upon its back and rushed away as fast as it could. Early the next morning, when the servants came to slaughter the Bull, it was nowhere to be found; and when the King woke and looked for his daughter, she too had vanished. He sent messengers in every direction, proclaiming from every churchyard that they should be found, but no one had seen them.

Meanwhile, the Bull traveled through many lands with the Princess on its back. At length, they came to a vast Copper Forest, where the trees, branches, leaves, and flowers were all made of copper. Before heading in, the Bull warned the Princess: “When we enter this forest, be very careful not to touch even a single leaf, or it’s the end of both of us, for it belongs to a troll with three heads.” She promised to take great care and not touch a thing.

Cautiously, she bent and twisted to avoid every branch, using her hands to push them aside, for the forest was so dense there was barely any path. But in spite of all her efforts, she accidentally broke off one small leaf, which remained in her hand. “Oh no! What have you done?” said the Bull. “Now we’ll have to fight for our lives. But hold on to that leaf.”

Just then, they reached the end of the forest—and out came a three-headed troll in a rage. “Who dares touch my forest?” roared the troll.  
“It is as much mine as yours,” said the Bull.  
“We’ll fight for it!” screamed the troll.  
“So be it,” said the Bull.

Then they charged each other and did battle. The Bull gored and kicked with all its might, while the troll struck just as fiercely. It took them the entire day before the Bull at last overcame the troll. By that time, the Bull was so cut and wounded that it could barely move. They had to rest for a whole day. Then the Bull told the Princess to take the healing ointment horn from the troll’s belt and rub it over its wounds. Once she did, the Bull was well again, and the next day, they set off once more.

They traveled many, many days until they finally came to a Silver Forest, where the trees, branches, leaves, and flowers were all made of silver. Before they entered, the Bull once again warned the Princess: “Whatever you do, don’t touch a single thing in this forest; it belongs to a troll with six heads, and I can hardly stand up to such a one.” The Princess promised to be extremely careful, saying she would not touch anything against his wishes.

But the forest was so thick and cramped that they could barely make any headway. She tried to be as gentle and cautious as possible, bending sideways for the branches and pushing them away with her hands. Yet every moment, the branches lashed back at her face, and try as she might, she broke off another leaf without noticing. “Oh no! What have you done now?” exclaimed the Bull. “We must fight for our lives once again. This troll has six heads and is twice as strong as the first. Keep the leaf and hold on tight.”

Sure enough, the six-headed troll soon appeared. “Who dares touch my forest?” it roared.  
“It is as much mine as yours,” said the Bull.  
“We’ll fight for it!” screamed the troll.  
“So be it,” answered the Bull.

They clashed so violently that the Princess nearly fainted from fright. The Bull gored the troll’s eyes out and drove its horns right through it, so that its guts spilled out, yet the troll kept striking back. It took three whole days of constant fighting before the Bull finally killed it. But by then, the Bull was so battered and exhausted it could hardly move, covered in so many wounds that blood poured from them. So it asked the Princess to take the healing ointment horn from the troll’s belt and rub it over its wounds. She did so, and the Bull recovered. They had to rest for a week before it was strong enough to go further.

When at last they journeyed on, the Bull was still weak, so they couldn’t go quickly at first. The Princess wanted to walk on foot to spare it, but the Bull would not allow it, insisting she remain upon its back.

They traveled onward, through countless lands, until they finally reached a Gold Forest where the trees, branches, leaves, and flowers were all made of pure gold. The Bull warned her, “For your life, don’t touch anything in this forest either, for it belongs to a troll with nine heads—far stronger than both of the others combined. I doubt even I can handle this one.” The Princess vowed she would be careful and wouldn’t touch a thing. Yet this forest was even thicker and more cramped than the last, so that every step became more difficult. The farther they went, the more unbearable it grew, until she thought there was no way forward. She was so afraid of breaking anything that she huddled down, bending left and right, pushing branches aside with her hands. But with the branches swirling so close to her face, she accidentally plucked a golden apple without meaning to. Terrified, she wept and wanted to throw it away, but the Bull told her to keep it and comforted her as best as it could, though it truly feared disaster this time.

Sure enough, out stormed the nine-headed troll, so frightening that the Princess hardly dared look at it. “Who dares touch my forest?” it roared.  
“It is as much mine as yours,” said the Bull.  
“We’ll fight for it!” screamed the troll.  
“So be it,” said the Bull.

They battled fiercely. The Princess could scarcely bear to watch. The Bull ripped out the troll’s eyes and drove its horns deeply into it, so its insides fell out, but whenever one head was destroyed, the others breathed life back into it. Their struggle lasted a whole week before the Bull was finally able to slay the creature. By then, the Bull was so wounded and weak that it couldn’t move at all, covered in cuts from head to hoof. It was in such pain that it could not even speak to tell the Princess to fetch the troll’s healing horn. But she did so on her own, rubbed the Bull’s wounds, and at last it revived. Still, they had to rest for three weeks before the Bull could stand and travel once more.

They then set out slowly. The Bull said they would go a bit farther yet. Together they crossed many high ridges covered in dense forest. After some time, they came up into the mountains.

“Do you see anything?” asked the Bull.  
“No, I see only sky and the wild mountains,” said the Princess.

As they climbed higher, the landscape flattened, and they could see farther.

“Do you see anything now?” the Bull asked.  
“Yes, I see a small manor, far, far away,” said the Princess.  
“That manor isn’t so little,” replied the Bull.

Eventually, they came to a large ridge with a steep cliff face.

“Do you see anything now?” asked the Bull.  
“Yes,” said the Princess. “I see the manor clearly. It looks bigger—much, much bigger.”  
“That’s where you must go,” said the Bull. “Right below that manor, you’ll find a pigsty where you can stay. You will discover a rough wooden cloak there—put it on, go up to the royal manor, say your name is Kari Trestak, and ask for work. Then you must do one more thing: Take your small knife and cut off my head, then skin me and bundle up the hide. Lay it under that cliff face, and inside the hide place the copper leaf, the silver leaf, and the golden apple. By the rock face, you’ll see a stick. Whenever you need me, just knock on the cliff wall with it.”

At first she refused. But when the Bull said it was the only thing he wanted in return for all he’d done for her, she couldn’t refuse him. It broke her heart, yet she did as he asked: she used the knife on that huge creature, cutting off its head and removing its hide. Then she folded up the hide beneath the cliff face, placing the copper leaf, the silver leaf, and the golden apple inside.

When she had finished, she walked away toward the pigsty, weeping and grieving with every step. There, she found the rough wooden cloak and put it on. Next, she went straight to the King’s manor, went into the kitchen, and asked for work, giving her name as Kari Trestak.  
“Well,” said the cook, “you can scrape the dishes, if you like. The one who did it before has just left. When you get fed up with that, I guess you’ll run away too.”  
“No,” said Kari, “I won’t.”

She was quite decent at washing up. One Sunday, the household was expecting guests, and Kari asked if she might carry the water basin to the Prince. The other kitchen workers laughed at her. “What good would that do? Do you think the Prince wants to be served by someone who looks like you?” they scoffed. But she wouldn’t give up and kept asking, so at last they let her go.

As she clattered up the stairs in her wooden cloak, the Prince emerged.  
“Who are you?” he asked.  
“I’m just here to bring you wash water,” said Kari.  
“Do you really think I want wash water served by you?” asked the Prince, and he threw the water over her.  
So she had to take it back. Then she asked for permission to go to church, since it was nearby. They agreed, though they wondered why she insisted. First, she ran to the cliff face and knocked on it with the stick, just as the Bull had instructed. At once, a man came out and asked what she wanted. Kari (who was really the Princess) said that she now had permission to attend church but no clothes to wear. So he brought out a gown that gleamed like the Copper Forest, along with a horse and saddle.

When she arrived at the church, she looked so radiant that everyone stared in wonder, hardly listening to the pastor. The Prince himself was so taken with her that he never took his eyes off her. As she left, he rushed after her, swiftly drawing shut the church door, keeping one of her gloves in his hand. When she got outside and mounted her horse, the Prince ran up again, asking where she was from.  
“I’m from Washwater Land,” answered Kari.  
When he tried to give her glove back, she said:

“Light before and dark behind,  
so the Prince won’t see the path where I ride!”

In a flash, she vanished, and no one could tell where she had gone. The Prince traveled far and wide, searching for the land the proud lady had named, but no one had ever heard of it.

The next Sunday, someone needed to take a towel up to the Prince. “May I do it?” asked Kari.  
“What’s the point of that?” said the others in the kitchen. “You saw how it went last time.”  
Still, she kept asking until they let her. She clattered up the stairs in her wooden cloak, the Prince came out, snatched the towel, and threw it right in her face. “Get out of here, you ugly nuisance!” he said. “Do you think I want a towel that’s been touched by your filthy hands?”

Afterwards, the Prince headed to church, and Kari asked again if she might go. They asked what point there was in her going, dressed all in that wooden cloak, so grimy and rough. But Kari insisted she longed to hear the sermon, and finally, they let her go.

Again, she ran to the cliff and knocked. The same man came out and gave her a dress even more splendid than before, embroidered with silver so that it gleamed like the Silver Forest. He also provided a magnificent horse with a saddle draped in silver cloth and a silver bridle.

When she arrived at church, everyone was outside on the slope, and they were even more amazed at her finery. The Prince rushed toward her to hold her horse, but she jumped lightly down. “No need,” she said. “My horse is so well trained, it stands when I tell it to and comes when I call.” Everyone went inside, but none of them heard a word of the sermon because they couldn’t stop staring at her. The Prince was even more captivated than the week before. When the service ended and she left, he followed her once again, asking where she was from.  
“I’m from Towel Land,” said the Princess, letting her riding crop drop to the ground. As the Prince stooped to pick it up, she whispered:

“Light before and dark behind,  
so the Prince won’t see the path where I ride!”

Then she was gone. The Prince searched the land far and wide, asking about this place called Towel Land, but no one had ever heard of it. He was forced to wait in frustration.

On the third Sunday, someone had to bring a comb to the Prince, and Kari asked them to let her go, too. They warned her of how it had gone before and scolded her for trying to show herself to the Prince—so dirty and ragged in her wooden cloak. But Kari kept pleading until they gave in. She clattered up the stairs, and the Prince came out, took the comb, threw it after her, and told her to be gone.

He then left for church, and Kari asked for permission to go as well. Again, they scoffed at the notion, but she insisted, and in the end they gave in. She ran to the cliff and knocked. Out came the same man, who gave her a gown more dazzling still, embroidered with gold and diamonds—nearly all gold—and a fine horse with golden trappings and a gold bridle.

When she arrived at church, the pastor and the people were waiting on the slope, hoping to see her once more. The Prince darted forward to hold her horse, but she leaped off gracefully. “No thank you,” she said. “My horse is so well trained that it stays still when I tell it to.” Then they all hurried inside, the pastor to the pulpit, but again nobody listened. Everyone’s eyes and thoughts were on the mysterious lady, and the Prince was more besotted than ever, seeing nothing but her.

When the service was over, and she went out of the church, the Prince had poured a jug of raw tar in the porch, thinking to help her across and thereby learn who she was. But she ignored the tar, stepping squarely into it and hopping over it. Off came one of her golden shoes, which stuck in the tar. She leaped onto her horse, and the Prince rushed out, calling for her, asking again where she was from.  
“I’m from Comb Land,” said Kari.

When he offered her the golden shoe, she answered:

“Light before and dark behind,  
so the Prince won’t see the path where I ride!”

And she vanished once more. The Prince spent a long time traveling the world, asking about this place called Comb Land, but no one knew such a kingdom.

So the Prince made a proclamation that whoever could fit into the golden shoe would become his wife. Suitors both fair and foul flocked from every corner, but no one’s foot was small enough to fit the golden shoe. After a long while, the cruel stepmother from Kari Trestak’s old life arrived with her daughter, and the shoe did fit that daughter’s foot. Yet she was so ugly and unpleasant that the Prince grudgingly prepared for the wedding, because he had promised.

They decorated everything for the celebration, and the bride was dressed, but on the way to church, a little bird sat in a tree and sang:

“A piece of heel  
and a piece of toe;  
Kari Trestak’s shoe  
is covered in blood!”

Sure enough, the bird spoke true, for blood was oozing out of the shoe. So everyone, from the servant girls to the court ladies, had to try on the shoe, but it would fit no one.

“But where is Kari Trestak?” asked the Prince, after all the rest had tried. He recalled the bird’s song and understood its meaning.  
“Oh, her?” they said. “There’s no point in bringing her forward. She’s got feet as big as a horse’s.”  
“Be that as it may,” said the Prince, “since everyone else has tried, Kari shall try too.”

“Kari!” he called through the doorway. Kari came up the stairs in her wooden cloak, clattering as if a whole regiment were charging in. “Now you must see if this golden shoe fits. Perhaps you’ll be a Princess, too,” the other girls teased, laughing at her. Kari picked up the shoe and slipped her foot in as easily as could be. She threw off the wooden cloak and revealed herself in the gleaming golden gown, wearing the matching shoe on the other foot. The Prince recognized her at once and was so overjoyed that he ran over, put his arms around her, and kissed her. When he discovered that she was truly a Princess, he was all the happier, and their wedding was soon held.  
Snip, snap, snout—so ends the tale!
