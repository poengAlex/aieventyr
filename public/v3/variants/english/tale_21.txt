THE BLACKSMITH THEY DIDN’T DARE LET INTO HELL

Once upon a time, in those days when the Lord and St. Peter walked around on Earth, they came to a Blacksmith. He had made a deal with the Devil that for seven years he would belong to him, on the condition that in that period the Blacksmith would be the master of all masters in the art of smithing. Both he and the Devil had signed this agreement. That is why he had written in large letters above the smithy door: “Here Lives the Master of All Masters!”

When the Lord came along and saw it, He went inside.  
“Who are you?” He asked the Blacksmith.  
“Read what’s written above the door,” the Blacksmith replied. “But maybe you can’t read, so you’ll have to wait for someone who can help you.”

Before the Lord could answer, a man arrived with a horse and asked the Blacksmith to put new shoes on it.  
“Could I have a chance to shoe it?” asked the Lord.  
“Go ahead and try,” said the Blacksmith. “You can’t possibly mess it up any worse than I can fix afterward.”

So the Lord went out, took one of the horse’s legs clean off, laid it in the forge’s fire, and made the horseshoe glow red. Then He sharpened and shaped the nails, bent them, and put the leg back onto the horse—good as new. When He was finished with that one, He took off the other front leg and did the same. And once He’d put that back on, He took the hind legs, first the right then the left, laid them each in the forge, heated the shoes red, sharpened them, bent the nails, and set those legs back on the horse. Meanwhile, the Blacksmith stood there watching.  
“You’re not such a bad Blacksmith, after all,” he said.  
“You think so?” said the Lord.

A little while later, the Blacksmith’s mother came to the smithy and asked him to come home and eat lunch. She was very old, terribly bent in her back and wrinkled in her face, and she could barely walk.  
“Pay attention to what you see,” said the Lord, and He took the old woman, laid her in the forge’s fire, and hammered out of her a young, beautiful woman.  
“Well, I stick by what I said,” said the Blacksmith, “you’re not a bad Blacksmith at all. It does say above my door: ‘Here Lives the Master of All Masters,’ but still, I’ll freely admit: We keep learning our entire lives.” With that, he went to the house and had his lunch.

After he had returned to the smithy, another man came riding up who wanted his horse shod.  
“That’ll be done in a jiffy,” said the Blacksmith. “I’ve just learned a brand-new way of shoeing. It’s useful when the day is short.” And so he started cutting and pulling until he got all four legs off the horse. “I don’t see the point in fussing with one leg at a time,” he said. He placed the legs in the fire, just like he had watched the Lord do, piled on lots of coal, and had his apprentices work the bellows hard. But just as one might have expected, the legs burned to ashes, and the Blacksmith had to pay for the horse. He wasn’t too happy about that. And right then, a poor old woman came walking by, so he thought, “If the one didn’t work, maybe the other will.” He grabbed hold of the old lady and laid her into the forge, and no matter how much she cried and begged for her life, it didn’t help.  
“You don’t realize what’s good for you at your age,” said the Blacksmith. “In just a moment, you’ll be turned into a young woman again, and I’m not even charging a single coin for my blacksmith work!” But it went no better for that poor woman than for the horse legs.  
“That was wickedly done,” said the Lord.  
“Oh, I doubt many will miss her,” the Blacksmith replied. “But it’s a shame about the Devil—he barely keeps the promise on my door.”

“If you could have three wishes from Me,” said the Lord, “what would you wish for?”  
“Try me,” answered the Blacksmith, “and you’ll see.”  
So the Lord gave him the three wishes.  
“First of all,” the Blacksmith said, “I’d like that whoever I ask to climb up into that pear tree outside by the smithy wall has to stay there until I ask them to come down. Second, I’d like that whoever I ask to sit in the armchair inside my workshop has to remain sitting there until I ask them to get up. And last, I’d like that whoever I invite to crawl into this wire purse I have in my pocket has to stay in there until I give them permission to crawl out.”  
“You make wishes like a fool,” said St. Peter. “First of all, you should have wished for God’s mercy and friendship.”  
“I didn’t dare aim that high,” said the Blacksmith.  
Then the Lord and St. Peter said farewell and went on their way.

Time passed, and when the seven years were up, the Devil—as stated in the contract—came to get the Blacksmith.  
“Are you ready now?” said the Devil, peeking his nose in through the smithy door.  
“Well, I really need to hammer the head onto this nail first,” answered the Blacksmith. “In the meantime, why don’t you crawl up in that pear tree and pick yourself a pear to snack on? You might be thirsty and hungry after your journey.”

The Devil thanked him for the good offer and climbed up the tree.  
“Now that I think about it,” said the Blacksmith, “I won’t be able to hammer this nail’s head on for the next four years, because this metal is awfully hard. You can’t come down in the meantime, so you’ll just have to sit there and rest.”  
The Devil pleaded, begging as pathetically as could be, to be let down, but it didn’t help. Finally, he had to promise not to come back for another four years, as the Blacksmith had said.  
“All right then, you can come down,” said the Blacksmith.

When that time was up, the Devil came back again to fetch him.  
“Now you’re ready, I hope,” he said. “Surely you’ve had time to hammer that nail’s head on by now.”  
“Yes, I’ve got the head on it,” replied the Blacksmith, “but even so, you’re still a bit early, because I haven’t sharpened the point yet. I’ve never forged metal so infernally tough before. While I’m sharpening, you might as well sit in my armchair and rest—I can guess you’re tired.”

“Thanks for that,” said the Devil, and sat down in the armchair. But he’d barely settled in before the Blacksmith added that on second thought, he really couldn’t get the point sharpened for another four years. The Devil started out politely begging to be let up, then he got angry and threatened. But the Blacksmith came up with every excuse he could, blaming the iron for being so terribly hard, and reassuring the Devil that the armchair was soft and comfy, so in four years he would be freed exactly on the dot. With no other option, the Devil had to promise that he wouldn’t come for the Blacksmith until four more years had passed. Then the Blacksmith said, “All right then, you can stand up,” and off the Devil went as fast as he could.

Another four years later, the Devil came once more to take the Blacksmith.  
“I’m sure you’re finished now,” said the Devil, sticking his nose in through the door of the smithy.  
“All set,” the Blacksmith replied. “We can go whenever you’d like. But there’s one thing I’ve been meaning to ask you: is it true what they say, that the Devil can make himself as small as he wants?”  
“Believe me, it’s true,” said the Devil.  
“Well then, could you do me a favor and crawl into this little wire purse to check that it’s solid at the bottom?” asked the Blacksmith. “I’m worried I might lose my traveling money.”  
“No problem,” said the Devil. He shrank himself down and crept inside the purse. But no sooner had he gone in than the Blacksmith snapped it shut.  
“Well, it’s solid and tight all around,” said the Devil from inside the purse.  
“Yes, I’m sure you say so,” answered the Blacksmith, “but better safe than sorry. I’ll just do a bit of welding on the seams, just in case.” And with that, he put the purse back into the forge and heated it until it glowed.  
“Ow! Ow! Are you out of your mind? Don’t you know I’m inside the purse?” yelled the Devil.  
“That’s nothing I can do anything about,” said the Blacksmith. “As the old saying goes: ‘You’ve got to strike while the iron is hot.’” Then he grabbed his big sledgehammer, laid the purse on the anvil, and hammered away at it with all his might.  
“Ow, ow, ow!” screeched the Devil inside. “Please, for goodness’ sake, just let me out and I promise I’ll never come back!”  
“All right, I think the seams are welded well enough now,” said the Blacksmith. “Out you come.” Then he opened the purse, and the Devil ran off so fast he didn’t dare look back even once.

After a while, the Blacksmith began to realize that maybe he’d made a mistake by making an enemy of the Devil. “Because if I can’t get into the Kingdom of God,” he thought, “then I might have nowhere to go, seeing as I’m at odds with the one who rules Hell.” So it seemed best to try somewhere—either Hell or Heaven—sooner rather than later, so he could find out where he stood. And with that, he slung his sledgehammer over his shoulder and headed out on the road.

He walked a good distance until he reached the crossroads where the path to Heaven branches off from the path that leads to Hell. There he caught up with a tailor’s apprentice hurrying along with his iron in his hand.  
“Good day,” said the Blacksmith. “Where are you headed?”  
“To Heaven, if they’ll let me in,” the tailor answered. “How about you?”  
“Well, I don’t think we’ll be traveling together,” said the Blacksmith. “I figured I’d first try down in Hell—I know the Devil a bit already.”

They said their goodbyes and went their separate ways, but the Blacksmith was a strong, sturdy man and traveled far faster than the tailor. Before long, he was at the gates of Hell. He told the guard to go announce that someone outside wanted a word with the Devil.  
“Go out and see who it is,” the Devil told the guard, so the guard did.  
“Tell the Devil it’s the Blacksmith—the one with that purse he knows all too well,” replied the man. “And please ask him politely to let me in right away, because first I was hammering all morning, and after that I’ve walked a long road.”  
When the Devil got that message, he ordered the guard to lock all nine bolts on Hell’s gate, “and add a padlock on top of that,” said the Devil, “because if he comes in, he’ll cause chaos in all of Hell.”

“There’s no chance of getting in here, I see,” said the Blacksmith to himself, noticing how firmly they bolted the door. “So I might as well try for Heaven.” Then he turned around and went back to the crossroads again, choosing the path the tailor had taken.

Because he was vexed at having walked such a distance back and forth for nothing, he stretched his stride as far as he could and reached Heaven’s gate just as St. Peter opened it a crack—just enough for that skinny tailor’s apprentice to slip inside. The Blacksmith himself was still six or seven paces away. “I’d better hurry!” he thought. He grabbed his sledgehammer and tossed it right through the little gap in the door, at the exact moment the tailor squeezed in. But whether the Blacksmith made it through that opening himself, well…I don’t know where on Earth he ended up!
