SORIA MORIA CASTLE

Once upon a time there were a man and a woman who had a son named Halvor. Ever since he was a little boy, he never wanted to do anything—he just sat and raked in the ashes. His parents sent him away to learn all sorts of things, but Halvor never stayed anywhere for long: after a few days, he would slip away, run back home, and sit at the hearth to dig in the ashes again.

But one day a ship captain came along and asked Halvor if he wouldn’t like to join him at sea and see foreign lands. Yes, Halvor was all for that, and he was ready in no time.

I don’t know how long they sailed, but after a very long while a fierce storm arose. When it finally ceased and the weather calmed again, they no longer knew where they were; they had been driven ashore on a strange coast, unknown to any of them.

Since there wasn’t the faintest breeze, they lay there at anchor. Halvor asked the captain if he could go ashore to have a look around—it was better to walk about than to lie around sleeping. “You think you can show yourself before people?” asked the captain. “You’ve got nothing on but those rags you stand in.” But Halvor kept insisting; at last he was allowed to go, though he was told to come back when the wind picked up.

So off he went, and it was a lovely land: wherever he passed, there were wide fields and meadows—but not a soul did he see. Then it began to blow, but Halvor still felt he hadn’t seen enough. He wanted to walk on a bit farther to see if he might find any people. After a while he came to a wide road, so smooth one could roll an egg along it. Halvor followed this road, and around evening he saw a large castle far off, its windows all aglow with light. He had traveled the whole day with hardly any food to sustain him, and was terribly hungry; but the nearer he came to the castle, the more afraid he grew.

Inside the castle, a fire burned in the hearth. Halvor went into the kitchen, which was so splendid that he had never in his life seen a kitchen its equal: there were cooking pots of both gold and silver, but not a person to be seen. After standing there a while with no one coming out, Halvor went over and opened a door. In that room sat a princess, spinning at a spinning wheel.

“Oh no, oh no!” she cried out. “Do Christian folk dare come here? You had better leave again, otherwise the troll with three heads will swallow you alive!”

“Well, I don’t care if he has four heads—I’d still like to see that fellow,” said the boy. “And I’m not leaving; I’ve done no harm. But you must give me something to eat, for I’m terribly hungry.”

When Halvor had eaten his fill, the princess told him to try wielding the sword hanging on the wall. No, he couldn’t swing it—he couldn’t even lift it.

“All right,” said the princess, “then take a sip from that flask hanging beside it. That’s what the troll does when he goes out with his sword.”

Halvor took a sip, and straight away he could swing the sword as though it were nothing. Now, he was certain he’d best be ready for the troll.

Sure enough, before long, the troll came rushing in, and Halvor stood behind the door. “Phew! It smells like Christian blood in here,” bellowed the troll, thrusting one of his heads in through the doorway. “Oh, you’ll find out soon enough,” said Halvor, and struck off all the troll’s heads.

The princess was so glad to be saved that she both danced and sang. But then she remembered her sisters. “Oh, if only my sisters were saved too!” she said.

“Where are they?” asked Halvor.

Then she told him: one was held captive by a troll in a castle six leagues away, and the other was held in a castle nine leagues beyond that.

“But for now,” she said, “you must first help me get rid of this carcass here.”

Halvor was strong, and in no time at all he dragged the whole lot away and left everything neat and tidy.

So they lived well and in comfort, and the next morning at dawn Halvor set off. He was restless and could find no peace. He walked and he ran the entire day. When he caught sight of the next castle, he felt a bit afraid again; it was far more splendid than the first, yet again no people were to be seen. Halvor went into the kitchen but didn’t stop there either—he went straight in.

“Oh no, do Christian folk dare come here?” cried the princess. “I don’t know how long I’ve been here, but in all that time I’ve seen no Christian person at all! You’d best be off again, for a troll with six heads lives here.”

“No, I’m not leaving,” said Halvor, “even if he had six more.”

“He’ll swallow you whole,” said the princess.

But it didn’t help—Halvor wouldn’t go. He wasn’t afraid of the troll, but he did want food and drink, for he was hungry after the journey.

Yes, she gave him all he wanted, but then urged him to go. “No,” said Halvor, “I’m not leaving, because I’ve done nothing wrong and I have no reason to be afraid.”

“That won’t matter to him,” said the princess. “He’ll take you without so much as a ‘by your leave.’ But since you won’t go, then try swinging the sword the troll uses in battle.”

He couldn’t swing the sword either. But then the princess said he should take a sip from the flask hanging next to it, and after he had done so, he could indeed swing it.

Before long, the troll came; it was so huge and stout it had to walk sideways to get in through the door. When the first head came in, it roared, “Phew! I smell Christian blood in here!” At that very moment Halvor cut off the first head, and then the rest. The princess was so happy she didn’t know where to stand—but then she thought of her sisters, and so wished that they too were saved. Halvor figured it might be managed, and wanted to set off straight away, but first he had to help her drag the troll’s carcass aside. After that, he was on his way the following morning.

It was a long distance to the third castle, and he both walked and ran to arrive in time. By evening he saw the castle, and it was even more magnificent than the other two. By now he was hardly afraid at all, so he went through the kitchen and straight inside. There, a princess sat—so beautiful you could hardly believe your eyes.

Just as before, she said that no Christian had come there since she herself arrived and begged him to go away, otherwise the troll that lived there—a troll with nine heads—would swallow him whole.

“Well, if he’s got nine, and nine more on top of that, I’m still not leaving,” said Halvor, and placed himself by the oven.

The princess pleaded so sweetly that he go away, so that the troll wouldn’t eat him, but Halvor said, “Let him come whenever he wishes.”

Then she gave him the troll’s sword and told him to take a sip from the flask so he could wield it properly.

Sure enough, before long the troll came rushing in so hard the wind whistled. It was even bigger and stouter than the other two, and had to walk sideways to get in the door as well. “Phew! I smell Christian blood in here,” it bellowed. At that very moment, Halvor struck off the first head, then the others; but the last head was the toughest of all, and it was the hardest work Halvor had ever done to get it off—even though he thought he was plenty strong.

So then all three princesses gathered together in that castle, as happy as they’d ever been. They were very fond of Halvor—and he of them—and he could have whichever of the three he liked best, though the youngest liked him most of all.

Halvor went around in a strange mood, so quiet and solemn. The princess asked what he was longing for, and whether he didn’t like being with them. Yes, he said, he did, for they had more than enough to live on, and he was happy there. But still he yearned to see his parents again, since they were alive, and he wished to visit them. The sisters agreed he could do that. “You can return safe and sound, if you follow our advice,” said the princesses. Yes, he would do everything they wished.

So they dressed him up until he looked just like a prince, putting a ring on his finger which allowed him to wish himself anywhere—forth and back. But they told him he must not throw that ring away, nor mention their names; if he did, all the splendor would vanish and he would never see them again.

“I wish I were home, and that home was here!” said Halvor. And just as he wished, so it happened—he stood outside his parents’ cottage door before he knew it. It was dusk, and when they saw such a fine, splendid stranger enter, they were quite flustered and began bowing and curtsying.

Halvor asked if he could spend the night there.  
“No, we can’t possibly manage that,” they said. “We haven’t got anything that someone like you could accept. You’d best head up the road to the farmhouse—you can see the chimney from here. They have plenty of everything.”

But Halvor wasn’t hearing it—he insisted on staying. The couple said he really ought to go to the farm, where he’d get food and drink; all they had was a bare cottage without even a chair for him to sit on.

“No,” said Halvor, “I won’t go there until morning. Let me stay here for the night—I can sit by the fireplace.”

They could not refuse, so Halvor settled by the hearth, raking the ashes, just like back when he used to loaf about at home.

They talked of this and that, and told Halvor all sorts of things. He asked whether they had ever had any children.  
“Yes, indeed—they once had a boy called Halvor,” they said, “but they had no idea where he wandered off to; they didn’t even know if he was dead or alive.”

“Couldn’t that be me, then?” asked Halvor.  
“Surely not,” said the old woman, straightening up. “Our Halvor was so lazy he never did anything, and was dressed in nothing but rags—one rag beating the next senseless with how tattered they were. He could never have turned into a fine gentleman like you, sir.”

A little later the old woman went over to the fireplace to poke at the fire. When the glow illuminated Halvor’s face, just the way it used to when he sat there in the ashes long ago, she recognized him.  
“Why, if it isn’t you after all, Halvor!” she exclaimed. Great was the joy of his aged parents—beyond all measure. He had to tell them everything that had happened to him, and his mother was so delighted that she wanted to take him up to the farmhouse that very hour to show him off to the girls who had always been so proud and haughty.

So she walked ahead and Halvor followed. When they arrived, she said Halvor had come home again, and that they should see how splendid he was now—he looked like a prince, she said.  
“Sure he does,” said the girls with a toss of the head. “He’s probably the same ragged tramp he’s always been.” But just then Halvor came in, and they were so flustered they forgot the underdress they had left near the fire, where they’d been sitting to pick over their clothes for fleas. They raced out in just their skirts. When they came back in, they were so ashamed they hardly dared glance at Halvor, toward whom they had always been so proud before.

“Yes, you always thought you were so fine and pretty that no one could match you,” said Halvor, “but you should see the eldest princess I saved. Next to her, you’d look like shepherd girls. The middle one is even more beautiful, and the youngest, who is my sweetheart, outshines both the sun and the moon. Heaven grant they were here, so you could see!” said Halvor.

He hadn’t finished speaking before they all stood there—but Halvor grew uneasy, for he suddenly remembered what the princesses had warned him about. At the farm, they held feasts and made a great fuss over the princesses, but the three would not stay there. “We’ll go down to your parents,” they said to Halvor, “and then travel around a bit.” He went with them.

They came to a large lake just beyond the farm. Close to it there was a lovely green hill. The princesses wanted to sit there and rest a while, saying they liked to look out over the water.

So they sat down, and once they had been there awhile, the youngest princess said, “Perhaps I should stroke your hair for a bit, Halvor.” So Halvor laid his head in her lap, she ran her fingers through his hair, and before long he fell asleep. Then she slipped the magic ring off his finger, put another ring in its place, and said, “Hold onto me, as I hold onto you—and I wish we were all back at Soria Moria Castle!”

When Halvor woke and realized he had lost the princesses, he began to weep and carry on, gripped by such despair that no one could comfort him. Despite all his parents’ coaxing, he couldn’t stay; he said farewell and set off in search of the princesses once again, convinced he’d never see them otherwise—and then his life would no longer be worth living.

He still had three hundred coins left, so he put them in his pocket and started walking. After he’d gone some distance, he met a man leading a fine-looking horse. Halvor wanted to buy that horse and started haggling with its owner.

“Well, I hadn’t exactly planned to sell it,” said the man, “but if we can come to terms—”

Halvor then asked him how much he wanted.  
“I haven’t paid much for it, and it’s not worth too much,” said the man. “It’s a good horse to ride on, but not much good for pulling. Still, it’ll carry your food bag and you as well, if you walk a bit in between.”

At last they settled on a price, and Halvor loaded his bag onto the horse. Then he walked part of the time and rode part of the time. By evening he arrived at a grassy knoll with a big tree, and he sat down there. He let the horse loose to graze and didn’t lie down to sleep but took out his food to eat. At dawn he departed again, for he was in no mood to linger. On he went, and on he rode the whole day through a vast forest, filled with bright green clearings that gleamed so prettily among the trees. He neither knew where he was nor where he was heading, but he took no more time to rest than it took the horse to graze a little, while he himself had a bite to eat whenever they came to a clearing. He walked and he rode, seeming never to reach the forest’s end.

But at dusk on the second day, he saw a light shining among the trees. “Heaven grant there might be people there, so I can get warm and have something to eat,” thought Halvor.

When he got there, he saw it was a small, shabby hut. Through the window, he could see an old man and an old woman inside, both of them as gray-headed as pigeons—and the old woman had a nose so long she was using it like a fire-stoking rod at the hearth.

“Good evening!” said Halvor.  
“Good evening!” said the old woman. “But what business have you here, who come to this place? There haven’t been Christian folk around these parts for over a hundred years.”

So Halvor told her that he was off to Soria Moria Castle, and asked whether she knew the way there.  
“No,” said the old woman. “I don’t, but just now the Moon will be rising; I’ll ask him. He sees everything, so surely he’ll know.”

When the Moon stood bright and clear above the treetops, the old woman went out. “Moon! Moon!” she shouted. “Can you tell me the way to Soria Moria Castle?”

“No,” said the Moon, “I can’t, because the one time I shone there, a cloud lay before me.”

“Wait a little longer,” the old woman said to Halvor. “The West Wind is coming now—he’ll know for sure, for he gusts and blows around every nook and cranny.”

“Ah, now I see you have a horse as well,” she said, coming back in. “Oh, let the poor creature loose into the paddock—don’t let it stand here at the door starving! But say, won’t you trade that horse to me?” said the old woman. “We have an old pair of boots here, and each step in them covers fifteen leagues. You can have them in exchange, and then you’ll reach Soria Moria Castle much faster.”

Halvor agreed at once, and the old woman was so happy with the horse she was nearly dancing. “Now I can ride to church, too!” said she.

Halvor had no peace in him and wanted to leave at once, but the old woman told him there was no rush. “Lie down on the bench and sleep a little—we have no bed for you,” she said. “I’ll keep watch and let you know when the West Wind comes.”

Sure enough, after a while, the West Wind came roaring in, rattling the walls. The old woman went out. “West Wind! West Wind! Can you tell me the way to Soria Moria Castle? This fellow here wants to go there.”

“Yes, I surely do,” said the West Wind. “I’m on my way there right now to dry clothes for the wedding that’s about to take place; if he can keep up with me, I’ll lead him.”

So Halvor went out.  
“Hurry along if you want to follow,” said the West Wind. And away they went, far off in the distance—across hills, ridges, mountains, and shores—and Halvor had all he could do to keep up.

“Well, I’ve no more time to stick with you,” the West Wind said at last. “I must go tear down a patch of pine forest first, before I head to the bleaching fields to dry the clothes. But if you walk along the hillside, you’ll come upon some girls standing and washing clothes—and from there, you won’t have far to go to Soria Moria Castle.”

A little while later, Halvor came to the girls who stood washing clothes. They asked if he had seen anything of the West Wind, who was to come that way and dry the wedding garments.  
“Oh, yes,” said Halvor. “He’s only gone to knock down some pine trees; he’ll be here soon enough.” Then he asked if they could show him the way to Soria Moria Castle. They did, and as he came nearer, he saw the place full of horses and people. But Halvor was so ragged and torn from following the West Wind through brush and thicket that he stayed off to one side, not wishing to appear until the last day, when they were to have the midday feast.

According to custom, on the day the bride was to toast all in the hall—bride, groom, knights, and servants—the server at last came to Halvor. He drank the toast, let the ring (the one the princess had placed on his finger by the lake) drop into the cup, and told the server to offer it to the bride with his compliments.

The moment she received it, the princess rose from the table. “Who among you has more right to one of us,” she asked, “than the man who saved us? Is it the groom who’s sitting by my side, or the one who freed us from captivity?”

Everyone agreed there could be only one answer to that. And when Halvor heard it, he wasted no time throwing off his ragged clothes and dressing as a bridegroom should.

“Yes, he is the true one!” cried the youngest princess when she saw him. Then she sent the other suitor on his way and married Halvor herself.
