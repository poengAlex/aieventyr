THE WIDOW’S SON

Once upon a time there was a very poor Widow who had only one Son. She worked hard to support the Boy until he had finished his lessons with the priest; but then she told him that she could no longer feed him and that he would have to go out into the world and earn his own living.

So the Boy set off. After walking for about a day, he met a stranger. “Where are you going?” asked the Man.  
“I’m out looking for work,” replied the Boy.  
“Would you like to work for me?”  
“Oh yes, just as gladly for you as for anyone else,” said the Boy.  
“Well, you’ll have a good time with me,” said the Man. “All you need to do is keep me company, and you won’t have to do any other tasks.”

So the Boy stayed with him, living well, with plenty to eat and drink, and hardly anything to do. But never once did he see another soul in the Man’s company.

One day, the Man said to him: “I’m going away for eight days. During that time you’ll be here alone, but you must not set foot in any of these four rooms. If you do, I will take your life when I return.”  
“No,” said the Boy, “I certainly won’t do that.”

But after the Man had been gone for three or four days, the Boy simply couldn’t resist the temptation and went into one of the rooms. He looked around but saw nothing except a shelf above the door, where there lay a hawthorn whip. “This is hardly something that needed such a strict warning,” thought the Boy.

When the eight days had passed, the Man came home again. “You haven’t been in any of the rooms, have you?” he asked.  
“No, I haven’t,” said the Boy.  
“Well, I’ll soon find out,” replied the Man, and went straight into the room the Boy had visited. “Indeed, you have been in here after all,” he said, “and now I’m going to take your life.”

The Boy wept and begged for mercy, so he was spared, but he got a good beating. When it was over, however, they were as friendly as ever again.

Some time later, the Man went away once more, this time for fourteen days. Before leaving, he told the Boy not to step into any of the rooms he had not already entered. As for the room he had visited before, he could go there again if he wished. Well, things turned out exactly as before, except that this time the Boy managed to wait eight days before going into one of the new rooms. In that room, too, he saw nothing but a shelf above the door, with a boulder and a water jug on it. “All that fuss over this, too,” thought the Boy.

When the Man came home, he asked if the Boy had been in any of the rooms. “No, absolutely not,” replied the Boy.  
“Well, I’ll soon see,” said the Man. Upon discovering that the Boy had gone inside after all, the Man declared, “This time I won’t spare you—your life is forfeit!”

But again the Boy wept and begged, and once more he got off with a severe thrashing—so severe that he could barely bear it. Yet after he recovered, he lived just as well as before, and he and the Man were good friends again.

Some time after that, the Man prepared for another journey, this time for three weeks. He told the Boy that if he went into the third room, there would be no hope for his life. For fourteen days, the Boy curbed his curiosity, but he couldn’t hold out any longer in the end. He slipped into the room and saw nothing in there except a trapdoor in the floor. He lifted it and looked down through it. There stood a large copper cauldron, bubbling and boiling away—yet he saw no fire underneath. “It would be interesting to see if it’s truly hot,” thought the Boy, and dipped his finger in. When he pulled it out again, it was all covered in gold. The Boy scraped and washed it, but the gold wouldn’t come off, so he wrapped a rag around it. When the Man came home and asked what was wrong with his finger, the Boy claimed he had cut it badly. But the Man tore away the rag and saw what had really happened. First he wanted to kill the Boy, but when the Boy wept and begged as before, the Man merely beat him so hard that he had to lie in bed for three days. Then the Man took a jar from the wall and rubbed him with its ointment, and the Boy was instantly healed.

After a while, the Man went off yet again, and said he wouldn’t be back for a whole month. He told the Boy that if he set foot in the fourth room, he could forget about keeping his life. For two or three weeks, the Boy stayed away, but then he just couldn’t help himself. He had to go in, so in he slipped. There stood a big black Horse in a stall, with a glowing trough in front of its head and a hay basket behind its tail. The Boy thought this arrangement looked odd, so he swapped them, placing the hay basket in front of the Horse’s head.

“Because you have such a kind heart that you think of letting me eat properly,” said the Horse, “I shall save you. If the Troll comes home now and finds you, he’ll kill you. But here’s what you must do: go up to the room directly above us, and take armor from among those that hang on the wall. Whatever you do, don’t take any of the shiny sets. Choose the rustiest one you see. The same goes for sword and saddle—pick out the most worn.”

The Boy did as instructed, though he could barely carry it all. When he got back, the Horse said he must undress completely and bathe in the cauldron that was boiling in the other room. “I’m sure to look awful after this,” the Boy thought, but he did as he was told. Once he had bathed, he became handsome and strong, rosy-cheeked and fair as milk and blood, and much stronger than before.  
“Do you feel any different?” asked the Horse.  
“Yes,” replied the Boy.  
“Try lifting me then,” said the Horse.

So he did, and he swung the sword as if it were nothing, too.  
“All right, now put the saddle on me,” said the Horse, “put on the armor, and be sure to take the hawthorn whip, the boulder, the water flask, and the ointment jar with you. Then we shall ride away.”

As soon as the Boy was on the Horse’s back, off they went so fast that he could hardly tell how they traveled.

They had been riding for a while when the Horse said, “I think I hear a rumble behind us. Look back and see if you can spot anything.”  
“Yes, there are lots and lots of them coming, maybe twenty or so,” said the Boy.  
“That’s the Troll,” said the Horse. “He’s coming with his followers.”

They rode on for a while, until the pursuers began to catch up.  
“Throw the hawthorn whip over your shoulder,” said the Horse, “but make sure to throw it well away from me!”

The Boy did as he was told, and at that moment, a thick, bristly hawthorn forest sprang up behind them.

So the Boy rode on, far, far ahead, while the Troll had to go back to fetch something to chop through the thorny woods.

After a while, the Horse said again, “Look back. Do you see anything now?”  
“Yes, there’s a crowd behind us, like a large mass of people,” said the Boy.  
“It’s the Troll again, and he has more with him this time. Throw the boulder, but throw it well away from me!”

The Boy did so, and immediately a huge rocky cliff rose behind them. Again, the Troll had to return home for something to tunnel through the rock, and meanwhile the Boy traveled farther on.

But soon the Horse asked him to look back again, and now the Boy saw what looked like an entire army—gleaming so brightly it shone. “Yes,” said the Horse, “that’s the Troll. Now he has all his followers with him. Pour the water from the flask behind you—but make sure not to spill any on me!” The Boy did, but one drop splashed onto the Horse’s flank, and suddenly they were standing far out in a vast lake. Still, the Horse managed to swim to shore. As for the Trolls, they lay down to drink it all up so they could get across, but they gulped so much that they burst.

“That’s the last we shall see of them,” said the Horse.

After they had traveled a long, long time, they came to a green clearing in a forest. “Now take off all your armor,” said the Horse, “and put on your threadbare clothes again. Take off my saddle and bridle and turn me loose, then hang everything inside that big hollow linden tree over there. Finally, make yourself a wig out of spruce moss and go up to the royal palace that lies nearby. Ask if they’ll give you some work. Whenever you need me, just come here and rattle the bridle, and I’ll come to you.”

The Boy did as the Horse said, and once he put on the moss wig, he looked so shabby, pale, and ragged that no one could recognize him. He arrived at the royal palace and first asked if he could help in the kitchen, carrying firewood and water for the cook.

But the kitchen maid said, “Why do you wear that ugly wig? Take it off! I won’t have anyone that hideous in here.”  
“I can’t,” the Boy answered. “My head’s not very clean.”  
“Are you saying I should let you handle food looking like that?” said the Cook. “Go down to the stable master; you’re only fit to muck out the barn!”

But when the stable master told him to remove the wig, he got the same answer, and the stable master didn’t want him either. “You can go to the gardener,” he said, “you’re best suited to digging in the ground.”

So the gardener let him stay, but none of the other servants would share a room with him. He had to sleep beneath the stairs leading up to the gazebo, which stood on poles with a high flight of steps. Underneath those steps, he got a bit of moss for a bed and slept as best he could.

Now, it so happened that one morning, just at sunrise, the Boy had taken off his moss wig and was washing himself. He was so handsome that it was a joy to look at him. The Princess saw the handsome garden boy from her window and thought she had never before seen anyone so good-looking. She asked the gardener why the boy slept out under the stairs.  
“Well,” said the gardener, “none of the other servants want to share a sleeping place with him.”  
“Have him come up tonight and sleep by the door in my room,” said the Princess. “After that, perhaps the others will stop acting too proud to let him sleep indoors.”

The gardener passed the message on to the Boy.  
“Do you really think I should?” asked the Boy. “People would say there must be something between me and the Princess.”  
“You surely have reason to be afraid of that,” said the gardener, “as handsome as you are.”  
“Well then, if you command it, I suppose I must,” replied the Boy.

When he went up the stairs that evening, he clomped and stomped so heavily that they told him to walk softly, so the King wouldn’t notice. Once inside, he lay down and started snoring at once. Then the Princess said to her handmaid, “Sneak over and pull off his moss wig.” She tried, but the moment she gave it a tug, he held onto it firmly with both hands and said she couldn’t have it. He then went back to snoring. The Princess signaled her maid again, and this time she managed to yank the wig away. There lay the Boy, as exquisitely fair and rosy as the Princess had seen him at sunrise. From then on, every night he slept in the Princess’s room.

It wasn’t long, though, before the King heard that the garden boy was staying in the Princess’s chamber every night. He became so furious that he almost had the Boy put to death. He didn’t go that far, however, but threw him in the dungeon, and locked up his own daughter in her room so she could never go out—day or night. No matter how much the Princess wept and pleaded for herself and for the Boy, it did no good; the King grew angrier than ever.

Soon afterward, war broke out in the land, and the King had to go to battle against another King who wanted to claim his realm. When the Boy in the dungeon heard this, he asked the jailer to beg the King on his behalf for armor and sword so he could join the war effort. Everyone laughed at the request, but they also thought it might be amusing to give the wretch some old, shabby gear, let him tag along, and watch the show. So he got his battered armor, plus a worn-out horse limping on three legs (it dragged the fourth behind).

They set off to face the enemy, but they hadn’t gone far from the royal palace when the Boy got stuck in a swamp with his poor old horse. There he sat, nodding and calling, “Go on, up! Go on, up!” to the beast. The others found it hilarious, mocking and jeering at him as they rode past. But as soon as they were out of sight, he ran to the hollow linden tree, put on the fine armor, and shook the bridle. At once the black Horse appeared. “If you do your very best, I’ll do mine!” the Horse said. When the Boy arrived at the battle, it had already started, and the King was in dire trouble. But in the blink of an eye, the Boy drove the enemy far away. Both the King and his men marvelled at who this unknown champion might be, but no one got close enough to speak with him. By the time the battle ended, he had vanished again. When they headed back, they found the Boy still stuck in the swamp, nodding and shouting at his three-legged horse. They laughed, pointing and saying, “Well look, there sits that fool still!”

The same thing happened the next day. The Boy sat there, they laughed at him and rode on, then he changed at the linden tree and came to the King’s rescue. No one could guess who that mysterious warrior was.

That evening, as they all rode home, they again saw him stuck in the swamp. They laughed and mocked him even harder; one of them shot an arrow at him and struck him in the leg. The Boy started howling and caused such a commotion that the King actually tossed him his own handkerchief to bind up the wound.

On the third morning, the Boy was slumped in the swamp as usual. “Go on, up! Go on, up!” he called to the broken-down horse.  
“He’ll starve sitting there,” said the King’s men, laughing themselves silly as they passed.

But as soon as they were gone, the Boy dashed to the linden tree, stepped into his armor, shook the bridle—and the Horse appeared. He arrived at the battle just in time. That day, he slew the enemy King, and the war was over then and there.

When the fighting was done, the King spotted his own handkerchief wrapped around the champion’s leg, and thus he recognized him. With great rejoicing, they brought him back to the palace. The Princess, watching from her window, was beyond delighted. “Here comes my beloved at last!” she cried. The Boy then took the ointment jar, treated his own leg and all the wounded soldiers besides, and everyone was instantly healed.

He married the Princess, and on the wedding day—the day the young man himself became King, inheriting half the realm—he went down to the stable to see the Horse. The Horse stood there sullenly, ears drooping, refusing to eat. When the new King asked him what was wrong, the Horse said: “Now that I’ve brought you this far, I no longer wish to live. You must take your sword and cut off my head.”  
“I won’t do that,” said the King. “You can have anything you want and stand here for the rest of your days.”  
“If you don’t do as I say, I’ll find a way to take your life,” said the Horse.

So the King had no choice. With great reluctance, and turning away so he wouldn’t see the blow, he raised his sword and chopped off the Horse’s head. But no sooner had he done so than, right where the Horse had stood, the most splendid Prince appeared.  
“Where in the world did you come from?” asked the King.  
“I was the Horse,” replied the Prince. “Once, I was a King in the land of the very monarch you slew in battle yesterday. He was the one who cast a troll’s spell on me and sold me to the Troll. Because he is now dead, my kingdom is restored to me. You and I will be neighboring Kings—and we shall never wage war against one another.”

And so it was. They remained friends all their lives, and often visited each other.
