34. The Master Thief

Once upon a time there was a tenant farmer who had three sons. He had no inheritance to give them and no task to set them to, and no idea what to do with them either. So he told them they were free to do whatever they wanted and go wherever they pleased; he would gladly follow them on the road, and so he did. He walked with them until they reached a place where three roads met; there they each took a different path, and the father said farewell and returned home. What became of the two eldest, I have never heard, but the youngest kept going, both far and long.

One night, he was crossing a large forest when a terrible storm came upon him. The wind blew and the snow whirled around so fiercely that he could barely keep his eyes open, and before he knew it, he lost his way completely and could find neither road nor path. But as he walked on, he at last saw a light shining far off in the woods. It seemed he ought to make for it, and after a long while, he arrived there. It was a large building, where the fire was still burning bright inside, so he realized they had not yet gone to bed. He went in, and there he found an old woman busily working.

“Good evening, Mother!” said the boy.  
“Good evening!” said the old woman.  
“Goodness me! The weather is so dreadful out there tonight,” said the boy.  
“So it is,” said the old woman.  
“Could I spend the night here? Could I get shelter?” asked the boy.  
“That wouldn’t be safe for you,” said the old woman. “If the people living here come home and find you, they’ll kill both you and me.”  
“What sort of people live here, then?” asked the boy.  
“Oh, they’re robbers and the like,” said the old woman. “They kidnapped me when I was small, and kept me as their housekeeper,” she said.  
“I think I’ll lie down anyway,” said the boy. “Whatever happens, I don’t want to go back out in such foul weather in the middle of the night.”  
“Well, it’ll go worst for you,” said the old woman.

So the boy lay down on a bed that stood there, though he did not dare sleep. Before long, the robbers came, and the old woman told them that a stranger had arrived whom she could not get to leave.  
“Did you see if he had any money?” asked the robbers.  
“Money, that poor fellow?” said the old woman. “He barely had the clothes on his back.”  
The robbers began to whisper among themselves about what to do with him—whether they should kill him or something else. Meanwhile, the boy got up and started talking with them, asking if they needed a servant, because he might quite like to work for them.  
“Well,” they said, “if you really want to, and you’ll do the work we do, you could stay here.”  
“Well, that depends on what sort of work it is,” said the boy, “because when I left home, my father said I could do whatever I liked.”  
“Are you willing to steal, then?” said the robbers.  
“Oh yes,” said the boy; that trade did not seem too hard to learn, he thought.

There was a man not far off who had three oxen. One of them he was going to drive to town and sell, and the robbers had heard about this. So they told the boy that if he managed to steal that ox on the road, without the owner noticing and without hurting him, he would be allowed to stay on as their servant.  
Yes, off the boy went, taking with him a fine shoe with a silver buckle that was lying about. He set it on the road where the man would pass with his ox, and then hid under a bush in the woods. When the man came by, he noticed the shoe right away. “That’s a splendid shoe,” he said. “If I had its mate, I’d take it home, and perhaps I’d make my wife cheerful for once!” For he had a wife so fierce and mean that she often beat him. But he figured he couldn’t do much with a single shoe without its pair, so he went on, leaving it there.

The boy picked up the shoe and ran as fast as he could through the forest, getting ahead of the man, and set the shoe in his path again. When the man came along with his ox, he was upset that he’d let the mate to that shoe remain behind and not taken it with him. “I guess I’d better run back and get the other one,” he said to himself, and tied the ox to the fence. “Then I’ll have a nice pair of shoes for the wife—maybe that will make her cheerful for once.”  
He went off, searching high and low for the other shoe, but could not find it, and at last had to return with only the one. Meanwhile, the boy took the ox and ran off with it. When the man came back and saw that his ox was gone, he began to cry and carry on, afraid his wife would kill him when she found out the ox had disappeared.

Then he thought he would go home, get the second ox, and drive it to town to sell for a good profit, not telling his wife about it at all. And so he did—it was easy enough to slip away without her noticing. The robbers got wind of this too (they had their ways), and they told the boy that if he could steal that ox as well, without the man noticing, and without hurting him, then he would be as good as any of them. The boy did not think that would be difficult.

This time, he took a rope and hanged himself by the arms in the middle of the road. When the man came along with his ox and saw this “apparition” hanging there, it unnerved him. “Oh dear, how troubled you must have been to hang yourself there!” he said. “Well, you can keep hanging there for all I care. I can’t breathe life back into you.” And so he walked on with his ox. The boy climbed down from the tree, took a shortcut, got ahead of the man again, and hung himself from another tree right in his path. “Peculiar—did you really hang yourself there, or am I just seeing things?” said the man. “Well, hang there if you like, whether you’re a ghost or whatever you are,” he said, and kept walking with his ox. The boy jumped down the same way as before, ran ahead through the forest once again, and hung himself in the middle of the man’s path. When the man saw this for the third time, he said to himself, “This is awful! Is it possible that three different people have hanged themselves out here? No, I can’t believe it’s anything but a trick on me. Now I want to find out for sure,” said he. “If the first two are still hanging, then it’s real, but if they aren’t, it’s just some deception.”

He tied up his ox and ran back to check if they really were still there in the trees. While he was off searching among the trunks, the boy jumped down, took the man’s ox, and went on his way. When the man returned and found his ox gone, he was thoroughly miserable. He cried and carried on, but finally calmed down and decided there was nothing for it but to go home and take his third ox without his wife knowing, and sell that one for as much money as possible. So he went home and took the third ox, snuck off so his wife would not notice, and set off again. Of course, the robbers heard about that as well. They told the boy that if he could steal this last ox too, just as he had the others, he would be master over them all.

Yes, the boy set off, sprinting through the forest. When the man came along, the boy began lowing, like the great ox itself, from deeper in the woods. When the man heard it, he felt certain it was the sound of his first big ox, and he thought, “Now I’ll find the other two again for sure!” He tied up his third ox and raced off the road to search in the forest. Meanwhile, the boy took the third ox. When the man came back and found it gone too, well, there was no end to his despair. He wept and lamented out loud and did not dare go home for many days, so afraid was he that his wife would kill him. The robbers were none too pleased either; they had to admit that the boy had bested them all.

One day, they thought they would try to do something he could not manage, so they all went off on a journey together, leaving him alone at home. The first thing he did, once they were well away, was to drive all the oxen out onto the road so they returned to the man from whom he had taken them, who of course was overjoyed. Then he gathered all the robbers’ horses, loaded them with all the best he could find—gold and silver and fine clothes and other splendid things—and told the old woman to greet the robbers for him and thank them. Then he said he was leaving, and they would have a hard time catching him. With that, he rode off.

Long after, he came back to the same road he had traveled when he first met the robbers. On his way home, where his father lived, he put on a uniform—one of the things he had taken from the robbers—that looked like a general’s outfit. He then drove onto the farm like a great man of importance. Afterward, he went inside and asked if he could stay the night there.  
“No,” said the tenant farmer, “I certainly can’t let such a grand gentleman stay here. I hardly have any bedding for myself, and it’s wretched enough as it is.”  
“You’ve always been a hard man,” said the boy. “And you still are, since you won’t even give your own son a place to stay.”  
“Are you my son?” said the man.  
“Don’t you recognize me?” asked the boy.  
Yes, then he recognized him indeed. “But what have you done with yourself, to turn into such a person so quickly?” asked the father.  
“I’ll tell you,” said the boy. “You said I could take up whatever I liked, so I learned from some thieves and robbers, and now I’ve finished my apprenticeship and become a Master Thief.”

Not far from the father’s place lived a district official, who owned a large estate and had so much money he could scarcely keep count of it. He also had a daughter who was beautiful, fair, kind, and gentle. The Master Thief wanted her for his wife, so he told his father to go and ask the official for his daughter’s hand. “If he asks what profession I follow, you can tell him I’m a Master Thief,” said the boy.  
“I think you’re out of your wits,” said his father. “You can’t be in your right mind, wanting something so foolish.”  
But the Master Thief insisted that the father go and ask, no matter what—he had promised, after all.  
“I won’t dare,” said the father. “He’s so rich and has so much money!”  
But the Master Thief said there was no choice—if he wouldn’t go willingly, then he would make him go by force; and following him with a big birch club, he drove the poor father forward until he arrived at the official’s door in tears.

“Well, my good man, what ails you?” asked the district official.  
So the father told him he had three sons who had all moved away one day, and he had said they could go wherever they pleased, and also do whatever they wanted. “And now the youngest has come home and forced me to come here and ask for your daughter’s hand, and say that he is a Master Thief,” said the man, weeping and wailing.  
“Calm yourself, good fellow,” said the official with a laugh. “You can tell him from me that he must prove it first. If he can steal the roast off the spit in my kitchen on Sunday, while everyone there is watching it, he shall have my daughter. Tell him that!”

Yes, the father went home and said so, and the boy thought that would be easy enough to do. He got himself three live hares, put them in a bag, and draped himself in some rags so that he looked wretched and ragged beyond belief—then slipped into the hallway like some scruffy beggar boy on Sunday morning. The district official himself and all who lived in the house were in the kitchen, keeping an eye on the roast.  

All at once, the Master Thief let the first hare out of the bag, and it raced away across the yard outside. “Look at that hare!” they shouted in the kitchen, and wanted to run out to catch it. The official saw it too. “Oh, let it run,” he said. “It’s no use trying to catch a hare on the run.”  

Before long, the Master Thief let out the second hare, and again they saw it from the kitchen, believing it to be the same one. Again they felt an urge to run out and snatch it, but the official said it was pointless. Then, a while later, the Master Thief released the third hare, and it scampered off across the yard. They saw it as well and still thought it had to be the same hare tripping around, so now they all wanted to go and catch it. “That’s the oddest hare, it keeps running in circles,” said the official. “Come on, let’s see if we can catch it.” Out he went, and everyone with him, the hare running ahead and them chasing after, so that it was quite the spectacle.

Meanwhile, the Master Thief grabbed the roast and slipped away, leaving the official with no roast for dinner. However he got himself a midday meal, it certainly wasn’t roast hare, though he came back both sweaty and weary from chasing it.

Around midday, the pastor arrived at the house, and when the official told him the trick the Master Thief had played, the pastor made such fun of him, there was no end to it.  
“I can’t imagine how I could be fooled by such a fellow,” said the pastor.  
“Just you be careful,” said the official. “He might well pay you a visit before you know it.”  
But the pastor wouldn’t hear of it and went on making jokes about how the official had let himself be taken in.

Later in the afternoon, the Master Thief came by to claim the official’s daughter, as promised.  
“You must do another test first,” the official said smoothly. “What you did today was nothing special. Could you play a really good trick on the pastor? He’s in there right now, making fun of me for letting you fool me.”  
Yes, that would be simple enough, said the Master Thief.

He dressed himself as a bird, taking a large white sheet, breaking the wings off a goose, and attaching them to his back. Then he climbed into a big maple tree in the pastor’s garden. When the pastor came home that evening, the boy began to call out, “Herr Lars! Herr Lars!”—for that was the pastor’s name.  
“Who is calling me?” said the pastor.  
“I am an angel, sent by the Lord to proclaim that you shall be taken up alive into heaven, because of your piety,” said the Master Thief. “Next Monday evening, have your bags packed, for then I shall come and fetch you in a sack. You must gather all your gold and silver, and everything else of worldly vanity, in a heap in your main living room.”

So Herr Lars gave thanks and fell to his knees before the “angel,” and that Sunday, he gave a farewell sermon, explaining that an angel of the Lord, in the great maple in his garden, had declared he should be taken up alive into heaven for his godliness. He preached with such passion, old and young alike were moved to tears in church.

On Monday, the Master Thief returned as an angel, and the pastor fell to his knees in gratitude before being bundled into a sack. Once inside, the Master Thief dragged him over both log and stone.  
“Ow! Oh dear!” the pastor moaned inside the sack. “Where are we going?”  
“This is the narrow path that leads to the Kingdom of Heaven,” said the Master Thief, hauling him along until he nearly knocked him senseless.

At last, he tossed him into the goose house at the official’s estate, where the geese hissed and nipped at him till he was nearly dead.  
“Oh dear, oh dear, where am I now?” said the pastor.  
“You are in purgatory,” said the Master Thief, “where you will be cleansed and purified for eternal life.” And off he went, taking all the gold and silver and all the splendid things the pastor had gathered in his main living room.

Early in the morning, when the goose girl came to let out the geese, she heard the pastor groaning inside the sack in the goose house.  
“Good heavens! Who are you, and what ails you so?” she asked.  
“Oh, are you an angel from Heaven? Let me out and let me return to Earth, for this place is worse than Hell. These little devils are pinching me with tongs!” cried the pastor.  
“I’m no angel, Heaven help me,” said the girl, who pulled the pastor out of the sack. “I only watch the official’s geese, and they’re the little devils that have been pinching you.”  
“Oh, it must have been the Master Thief! Oh, my gold and my silver and all my fine clothes!” the pastor wailed, tore at his hair, and ran home so frantically that the goose girl thought he must have lost his senses altogether.

When the official heard what had happened to the pastor—that he had been through purgatory and set upon the narrow path—he nearly laughed himself ill. But when the Master Thief came and wanted the official’s daughter, as he had been promised, the official tried to soothe him and said, “You must do one more trick, so I can see clearly what you are capable of. I have twelve horses in my stable,” he said, “and I’ll put twelve men on them, one on each. If you can steal those horses from under them, then we’ll talk.”  
“That may be possible,” said the Master Thief, “as long as you’ll truly give me your daughter afterward.”  
“Yes, if you manage that, I’ll do my best,” said the official.

So the Master Thief went to a market trader and bought strong liquor for two pocket flasks. He poured a sleeping draught into one of them, leaving the other simply as liquor. Then he hired eleven men to hide behind the official’s barn at night. For good words and payment, he borrowed an old coat and vest from a poor old woman, then took up a walking stick and a bag over his shoulder. As dusk fell, he hobbled off toward the official’s stable. When he arrived, they were watering the horses for the evening and had their hands full.  
“What in the devil’s name do you want?” one of the stable hands said to the old woman.  
“Oh mercy me, it’s so cold!” said the old woman, shivering and complaining. “It’s cold enough out there to freeze someone to death!” And she shook and trembled again. “Dear God, may I please rest here, just inside the stable door?” she asked.  
“Like hell you can! Get lost at once—if the official sees you here, we’ll all be in trouble,” said one.  
“Oh, the poor old soul,” said another, who felt sorry for her. “Surely this poor thing can just sit there. She won’t do any harm.”  

The others did not like it, but while they argued and tended the horses, she slid farther into the stable, until finally she was behind the door and out of sight, and then no one noticed her anymore.

When it grew late, the men got cold, sitting on horseback through the night.  
“Gosh, it’s freezing,” said one, flapping his arms.  
“Yes, I’m shivering,” said another.  
“I wish we had a bit of tobacco,” someone said.  
Someone did have a little chunk, so they shared it—though it wasn’t much for each—and spat and chewed. It helped a little, but in no time, they were as cold as before. “Gosh, it’s freezing!” said one, shaking himself. “Gosh, it’s freezing!” said the old woman, who had started to chatter her teeth so they rattled in her jaw. Then she took out a flask with just liquor inside it, her hand shaking so badly that the liquid sloshed around, and she took a swig, gulping it down audibly.

“What have you got in that flask, old woman?” asked one of the stable hands.  
“Oh, it’s just a little drop of liquor, dear,” she said.  
“Liquor, you say? Let us have a taste! Let us have a taste!” they all shouted, talking over each other.  
“But there is so little,” she whined. “There wouldn’t be enough for you to even get your lips wet.”  
However, they demanded it, and they had to have it—there was no getting around it. So she took out the flask with the sleeping draught and gave it to the first man. Now she no longer shook because she controlled the flask quite well, ensuring each man got what he needed. The twelfth man had barely drunk before the first was already nodding off. So the Master Thief threw off the beggar rags and lifted each of the men off in turn, placing them astride the stable beams, and then he called in his hired eleven men, and away they all rode with the horses to the official.

In the morning, the official went out to look in on his men. They tried to rouse themselves, digging their spurs into the beams so that wood chips flew about, and some fell right off while others stayed perched there like fools. “Oh, I see who’s been here,” said the official. “But you’re a worthless bunch to let the Master Thief steal the horses out from beneath you!” And he gave them a good scolding for not keeping better watch.

Later that day, the Master Thief appeared again, explaining how he had managed it, and now he wanted the official’s daughter as promised. The official gave him a hundred dollars instead and said he must do another, even better trick. “Do you think you can steal the horse from under me while I am out riding?” asked the official.  
“That should be possible,” said the Master Thief, “as long as I truly get your daughter for it.”  
“Well, let’s see what you can manage,” said the official, and told him on which day he would ride out onto the large drill grounds.

The Master Thief got hold of a worn-out mare, fashioned a harness out of nothing but willow twigs and broom handles, bought a battered old cart and a big barrel, and then told a poor old woman that he would give her ten dollars if she would climb into the barrel and keep her mouth over the barrel’s tap hole; he would put his finger through it—he promised nothing bad would happen to her, she would just go for a ride—and if he pulled his finger out more than once, she would get another ten dollars. He then blackened his face, dressed in rags, and put on a wig and a huge goat-hair beard so no one would recognize him. Off he went to the drill grounds where the official had already been riding for some time.

When he arrived, the cart moved so slowly and stiffly that it almost didn’t move at all. It creaked along, a little now and then, then stopped altogether, then moved a bit again, going so miserably that the official could never have guessed it was the Master Thief. He rode right up to the man, asking if he had seen anyone lurking in the nearby forest.  
“No,” said the man, he hadn’t seen a soul.  
“Listen,” said the official, “if you’d ride into the woods and have a good look for anyone lurking about, I’ll lend you my horse, and give you a good tip for helping me.”  
“No, I can’t do that,” said the man, “I’m supposed to deliver this barrel of mead for a wedding, but I lost the tap on the way, and now I have to hold my finger in the hole.”  
“Just ride,” said the official. “I’ll look after your horse and barrel.”

So the man agreed, asking the official to be quick about putting his finger in the barrel’s tap hole when he pulled his own out. The official promised, and the Master Thief leaped onto the horse. But time dragged on, the official got tired of standing there with his finger plugging the hole, and eventually he pulled it out.  
“Now I want another ten dollars!” the old woman in the barrel shouted. Then he understood how it was with the “barrel of mead,” and started walking home. But after a short distance, he was met on the road by someone leading his horse back; the Master Thief, it turned out, had gotten there long ago.

The next day, the Master Thief returned to the official, asking once more for his daughter. The official tried to smooth things over again, gave him two hundred dollars, and said the Master Thief had to complete one last test; if he succeeded, then he would indeed marry her.  
The Master Thief wanted to know what that test was.  
“Do you think you can steal the sheet from our bed and the nightgown off my wife?” the official said.  
“That might not be impossible,” said the Master Thief, “provided I truly get your daughter.”

Late that night, the Master Thief went out to the gallows and cut down a thief who was hanging there. He slung the body over his shoulder and took it with him. Then he fetched a tall ladder and placed it against the window of the official’s bedroom. Up he went and propped the dead man so he rose and fell, just as though someone outside were peering in at the window.

“There’s the Master Thief, dear!” said the official, nudging his wife. “Now I’m going to shoot him,” and he reached for a rifle he had placed by the bed.  
“Oh no, don’t do that,” said his wife. “You invited him to come, after all.”  
“I’m shooting him,” said the official, taking careful aim, for sometimes the head was visible, sometimes not, until at last he had a clear shot. He fired, and the dead body fell with a thud to the ground. The Master Thief hurried down as fast as he could.

“Well, I am the law in these parts,” said the official, “but people will talk if they see a dead man here, and I would hate for that to happen. It’s best if I go out and bury him at once.”  
“That’s up to you, dear,” said his wife.

The official got up, went out through the door, and just at that moment, the Master Thief slipped inside and went straight up to the wife.  
“All done already?” she asked, thinking it was her husband.  
“Oh yes, I tossed him in a hole and covered him with a bit of earth—enough so he’s hidden, for it’s nasty weather outside. I’ll bury him properly later. But let me have the bed sheet to dry myself, for he was all bloody, and I’m covered in it.”  

She gave him the sheet.  
“You’ll have to let me have your nightgown too,” he said. “The sheet alone isn’t enough, I can tell.”
