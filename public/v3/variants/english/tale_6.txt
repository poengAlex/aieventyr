Askeladden Who Entered an Eating Contest with the Troll

Once upon a time there was a farmer who had three sons. He was poor, old, and frail, and his sons refused to do any work. The farm had a large, fine forest, and the father wanted his boys to log some trees and try to pay off part of their debt.

Eventually, he managed to get them going, and the oldest son was to set out and chop first. When he reached the forest and began to fell a tall, old spruce, a huge troll appeared before him. “If you cut down trees in my forest, I shall kill you!” roared the troll. Hearing this, the boy dropped his axe and ran home as fast as he could. Out of breath, he told everyone what had happened, but his father called him a coward and said that when he was young, trolls had never frightened him away from chopping wood.

On the second day, the middle son went out, and exactly the same thing happened. After chopping a few strokes at a spruce, the troll appeared and shouted: “If you cut down trees in my forest, I shall kill you!” The boy barely dared to look at him, dropped his axe, and ran away just like his brother—perhaps even faster. When the boy came home, their father was furious, insisting that trolls had never scared him in his younger days.

On the third day, Askeladden decided to go. “Oh, sure,” said the older brothers, “you, who have never even stepped outside the door, will do so well!” Askeladden said nothing in return, but merely asked for a decent packed meal. His mother had no fat to cook with, so she put a pot on the fire to sizzle something up for him. He took it in his knapsack and headed off.

He had been cutting for a short time when the troll came to him too and said, “If you chop down trees in my forest, I shall kill you!” But Askeladden was quick. He ran into the woods, pulled out the piece of cheese he had, and squeezed it so hard that the whey squirted out. “Be quiet,” he shouted to the troll, “or I’ll squeeze you the way I squeeze water out of this white stone!”

“No, dear friend, spare me,” the troll begged. “I will help you chop.”

On that condition, Askeladden let him be, and the troll was quite the woodcutter. Together, they felled and trimmed many piles of logs that day.

Toward evening, the troll said, “Now you can come home with me. It’s closer to my place than to yours.”

So the boy went along, and when they arrived at the troll’s home, the troll was to start the fire in the fireplace, while the boy was sent for water for the porridge pot. But there were two iron buckets there—so large and heavy that he couldn’t even move them from their spot.

So the boy said, “There’s no point in taking along these thimbles; I’ll fetch the entire well instead!”

“No, good heavens,” said the troll, “I can’t lose my well! You start the fire, and I’ll go for the water!”

When he came back with the water, they cooked up a huge pot of porridge.

“All the same,” said the boy, “if you’re willing, we’ll have an eating contest.”

“Oh yes!” answered the troll, thinking it was a challenge he would surely win.

So they sat down at the table. Unnoticed, Askeladden took out his leather pouch and tied it in front of himself. He ladled more porridge into the pouch than he actually ate. When it was full, he pulled out his pocketknife and slit the pouch quietly. The troll watched him but said nothing.

They kept on eating for quite a while, and at last the troll set down his spoon. “No, I can’t manage any more,” he said.

“You must keep eating!” answered Askeladden. “I’m barely half-full myself. Do the same as I did—cut a hole in your belly, so you can eat as much as you want.”

“But won’t that hurt terribly?” asked the troll.

“Oh, nothing worth talking about,” said Askeladden.

So the troll did as Askeladden said—and thus, as you can imagine, he lost his life. Meanwhile, Askeladden gathered up all the silver and gold in the mountain and took it home with him. Thanks to that, he could always pay off whatever debt the family had.
