THE THREE PRINCESSES IN WHITE LAND

Once upon a time, there was a fisherman who lived close by the castle and caught fish for the King’s table. One day, while he was out fishing, he caught nothing at all. No matter what he did—baiting the hook and fishing, then fishing and baiting again—there wasn’t a single fish on the line. But late in the day, a head popped up from the water and said, “If I can have what your wife is carrying under her belt, you shall have plenty of fish!” The man agreed at once, for he had no idea she was expecting a child. After that, he caught all the fish he could possibly want every day.

When he came home that evening and told his wife how he had managed to catch so many fish, she began to cry and lament, begging God to comfort her over the promise her husband had made—for she was indeed carrying a child. Word of this soon reached the castle, and when the King heard about it, he promised he would take the child and make sure it was saved.

Time passed, and when the day came, the fisherman’s wife gave birth to a baby boy. The King took the child as his own and raised him until he was grown.

One day, the boy asked for permission to go fishing with his father. He longed to do so, he said. The King was reluctant to allow it, but in the end, the boy got his way. He went out with his father, and everything went smoothly all day, until they came ashore in the evening. The son realized he had left his handkerchief in the boat and jumped back in to fetch it. But the moment he set foot in the boat, it started moving off with him, creating a roaring wake all around. Though the boy pulled hard on the oars, it did no good; the boat continued onward all night long, until at last he arrived far, far away on a white shore.

He went ashore, and after walking a while, he met an old man with a long white beard.  
“Where am I?” asked the boy.  
“This is White Land,” answered the old man, who then asked the boy where he came from and what he wanted. The boy told him everything.  
“Well then,” said the old man, “if you keep walking along this shore, you’ll come upon three princesses buried in the ground so that only their heads stick out. The first one you pass—that’s the eldest—will call out to you and ask prettily for your help, and so will the second. You must rush past them as though you neither see nor hear them. But the third you should approach and do whatever she asks, because your fortune will depend on it.”

When the boy came to the first princess, she called out and begged him most sweetly to come over and help her, but he walked on as though he neither saw nor heard her. He did the same with the second. But when he reached the third, he went up to her.  

“If you do as I tell you,” said the princess, “you may have whichever of us three you wish.”  

The boy said he would gladly do so. Then she told him that three trolls had placed all three of them in the ground, although they used to live in that castle over in the woods. She went on to say, “Now you must go into the castle and let the trolls whip you for one night on behalf of each of us. If you can endure that, you will save us.”

“Yes,” answered the boy, “I will try.”

“When you enter,” said the princess, “there will be two lions by the gate, but as long as you walk straight between them, they will do you no harm. Then go directly into a small, dark chamber and lie down. A troll will come and beat you, but you must take the bottle hanging on the wall and rub yourself wherever he strikes, and you will be as good as new. Then grab the sword hanging next to that bottle, and strike the troll dead.”

So he did exactly as the princess told him. He walked straight between the lions as though he didn’t see them, and then on into the small chamber, where he lay down.

On the first night, a troll with three heads and three switches came in and whipped the boy thoroughly. He held on until the troll had finished. Then he took the bottle and rubbed himself with it, took the sword, and killed the troll. When he came out in the morning, the three princesses stood in the ground up to their waists.  

On the second night, the same thing happened, except this time the troll had six heads and six switches and whipped him even harder. But the next morning, the princesses stood in the ground up to their calves.  

On the third night, a troll with nine heads and nine switches appeared and beat him so hard and for so long that he finally fainted. The troll picked him up and threw him against the wall, knocking down the jug so that its contents splashed all over him; he revived at once. Then he wasted no time—he grabbed the sword and killed the troll. By the following morning, the princesses were completely free of the ground. He chose the youngest to be his queen, and lived happily with her for a long while.

Eventually, he longed to visit his parents. The queen didn’t like the idea, but because he was so eager, and simply must go, she said to him, “Promise me one thing: do as your father tells you, but not as your mother tells you.” He promised this. Then she gave him a ring with a special power: whoever wore it could make two wishes for anything they wanted. He wished himself home, and his parents were astonished beyond measure to see how fine and splendid he had become.

After some days at home, his mother wanted him to go up to the castle and show the King what a grand man he was now. His father said, “No, you shouldn’t do that, for then we won’t have the pleasure of having you here at all.” But it was no use—his mother pleaded and begged so long that he finally agreed to go.

When he arrived, he was more splendidly dressed and equipped than the King himself. The King did not like that one bit, and said, “So, you see what my Queen is like, but I cannot see yours. I bet your Queen is not as lovely as mine.”

“If only she were here, then you would see!” said the young King. And instantly, she was there.

But she was very sad and said to him, “Why did you disobey me and not listen to what your father told you? Now I must return home at once, and you have used both of your wishes.” With that, she tied a ring into his hair on which her name was inscribed, and wished herself back home.

The young King was beside himself with sorrow and spent his days and nights thinking only of how he might get back to his Queen. “Perhaps I can find out somewhere how to get to White Land,” he thought, and so he traveled the world.

After a while, he came to a mountain, where he met a man who was lord over all the animals in the forest—whenever he blew a horn he carried, they all came running. The King asked this man about White Land.

“I do not know,” the man answered, “but I shall ask my animals.” He blew the horn, and when they gathered, he asked if any of them knew where White Land lay. None of them knew.

So the man gave him a pair of skis, saying, “When you stand on these, you will travel to my brother, who lives a hundred miles from here. He is lord over all the birds of the air. Ask him. And when you arrive, just turn the skis around so their points face back this way—they’ll return home on their own.”

When the King got there, he turned the skis around as the lord of the beasts had told him, and away they went. The King again inquired after White Land, and the man summoned all the birds. He asked if any of them knew where White Land was. Not one of them knew.

Finally, long after the others, came an old eagle that had been away for ten long years, but even she did not know.

“Well, well,” said the man, “I’ll lend you a pair of skis. When you stand on them, you will reach my brother, who lives a hundred miles from here. He is lord over all the fish in the sea. You can ask him. But don’t forget to turn the skis back again!”

The King thanked him, stepped onto the skis, and when he reached the one who ruled the fish in the sea, he turned them around, and off they went, just as before. Then he asked once more about White Land. The man blew his horn, and all the fish gathered, but none of them knew anything—until there finally came an old, old pike that he had a hard time even blowing in.

When he asked her, she replied, “Oh yes, that place I know well enough, for I have been cook there for ten years. Tomorrow I’m heading back, because the Queen—whose King has disappeared—is supposed to marry another.”

“In that case, let me tell you what to do,” said the man. “Over on that bog stand three brothers who have been there for a hundred years, fighting over a hat, a cloak, and a pair of boots. Whoever has those three things can make himself invisible and wish himself anywhere he likes. Tell them you want to test all three items, and then you shall decide who gets them.”

The King thanked him, went on his way, and did as advised. “What do you stand here quarreling over forever?” he asked the three brothers. “Let me test those things, and I will judge between you.” They were all willing. But no sooner had he gotten the hat, the cloak, and the boots than he said, “When we meet again, you shall hear my judgment!” and with that, he wished himself away.

As he traveled through the air, he encountered the North Wind.  
“Where are you heading?” asked the North Wind.  
“To White Land,” replied the King. And he told the North Wind all that had happened.  
“Well,” said the North Wind, “you can travel faster than I, since I must blow through every corner and gust in every nook. But when you arrive, position yourself on the steps beside the door, and I shall come rushing in, as if to blow the entire castle down. When the prince who is about to marry your queen comes out to see what’s going on, seize him by the neck and throw him out; I will handle the rest and carry him away.”

The King did exactly as the North Wind said. He stood on the steps, and when the North Wind came howling and rumbled against the castle walls so they shook, out came the prince to see what all the fuss was about. The moment he showed himself, the King grabbed him by the scruff of the neck and threw him out, whereupon the North Wind seized him and carried him away.

With that prince gone, the King went inside the castle. At first, the queen did not recognize him because he had grown so thin and pale from wandering in sorrow for so long. But when he showed her the ring, she was overjoyed, and the true wedding celebration took place, so magnificent that people far and wide heard the tale.
