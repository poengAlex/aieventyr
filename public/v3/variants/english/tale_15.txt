The Man’s Daughter and the Old Woman’s Daughter

There was once a man and a woman who had married each other;  
they each had their own daughter. The old woman’s daughter  
was lazy and idle and never wanted to do anything, and the man’s  
daughter was clever and willing; yet she could never please  
the mother, and both the old woman and her daughter wanted  
nothing more than to be rid of her.  

So, one day, they were both to sit by the well and spin. The old woman’s  
daughter was to spin flax, but the man’s daughter got nothing but coarse  
bristles. “You’re always so smart and skilled,” said the old woman’s daughter,  
“but I’m still not afraid to spin in a contest against you.” So they agreed  
that the first one whose thread snapped would have to go into the well.  

Sure enough, the man’s daughter’s thread broke first, so she had to go  
into the well. But when she reached the bottom, she didn’t get hurt, and  
far and wide she saw nothing but a lovely green meadow.  

She walked for a while across the meadow until she came to a brush fence.  
She needed to climb over it. “Oh, please don’t step on me too hard,” said  
the fence, “and then I’ll help you another time!” She made herself light as  
a feather and stepped so carefully that she hardly touched it at all.  

She walked a bit farther, and then she came to a brindled cow that had  
a milk pail on its horns. It was a large, handsome cow with a full, round  
udder. “Oh, please be so kind as to milk me,” said the cow, “for I’m so  
full of milk that I’m about to burst. Drink as much as you want, and pour  
the rest onto my hooves, and then I’ll help you in return.”  

The man’s daughter did as the cow asked; as soon as she grabbed the teats,  
the milk spurted into the pail. She drank until she was no longer thirsty,  
then poured the rest onto the cow’s hooves, and hung the pail on its horns again.  

After walking a bit farther across the meadow, she met a big ram. It had such  
thick, long wool that it was dragging on the ground behind it, and from one  
of its horns hung a big pair of shears. “Oh, please shear me,” said the ram,  
“for I walk around with all this wool and pant in the heat, and I’m about  
to suffocate. Take as much as you want, and wind the rest around my neck,  
and I’ll help you in return.”  

She was immediately willing, and the ram laid its head in her lap and lay  
quite still. She sheared it so gently that it didn’t get the slightest scratch.  
She took as much wool as she wanted, and wound the remainder around  
the ram’s neck.  

A bit farther along, she came to an apple tree, which was so full of apples  
that its branches bent down to the ground. By the trunk stood a little  
stick. “Oh, please pick my apples,” said the tree, “so that my branches  
can straighten up again, for it pains me to stand so crooked. But do it  
carefully so you don’t ruin me; eat as many as you like and place the rest  
by my roots, and I’ll help you in return.”  

She picked all the apples within reach, then took the little stick and gently  
knocked down the others; she ate her fill and then laid the rest neatly  
around the trunk.  

She walked on and on until she came to a large farm, where there lived  
a witch with her daughter. The man’s daughter went inside and asked  
if she could get work there. “Oh, it’s no use,” said the witch. “We’ve had  
many helpers, but none of them amounted to anything.” But the girl begged  
so prettily that the witch finally agreed to hire her.  

Then the witch gave her a sieve and told her to fetch water with it. The  
girl thought it was impossible to carry water in a sieve, but she went  
just the same. When she came to the well, the little birds sang:  
“Spread on clay,  
put in straw!  
Spread on clay,  
put in straw!”  

So she did as the birds said, patching up the sieve with clay and straw,  
and then she could carry water in it well enough. But when she returned  
home with the water and the witch saw the sieve, the witch said,  
“You didn’t figure that out by yourself!”  

Then the witch told her to go to the barn to clean it out and do the milking.  
But when she got there, the shovel was so huge and heavy that she couldn’t  
even lift it. She had no idea what to do, but the birds sang that she should  
take the handle of the pitchfork and toss out a little, then the rest would  
fly out on its own. That’s exactly what she did, and in no time, the barn  
was as clean as if it had been both shoveled and swept.  

Now she was supposed to milk the cows, but they were so restless—kicking  
and stomping—that she couldn’t get near them to milk. But then the little  
birds outside sang:  
“Tiny stream,  
little sip,  
sprinkle a bit  
for all small birds!”  

So she sprinkled a small trickle of milk for the birds, and all the cows  
quieted down and let themselves be milked, and they neither kicked nor  
stomped; they didn’t even lift a hoof.  

When the witch saw the girl returning with the milk, she said,  
“You didn’t figure that out by yourself! But now you can take that black  
wool outside and wash it until it’s white.” 

The girl didn’t know how on earth she was supposed to do that, since  
she’d never seen black wool turn white, but still she said nothing. She  
simply took the wool and went out to the well. Then the birds sang  
that she should put the wool into the big tub that stood there, and  
soon it would be white.  

“Ugh, ugh!” said the witch when the girl brought the wool inside.  
“You’re capable of everything; if you stay here any longer, you’ll be  
the death of me. You’d best be on your way.”  

Then the witch set out three boxes: one red, one green, and one blue,  
and told the girl she could choose whichever she wanted and keep it  
as her pay. The girl didn’t know which to take, but the little birds sang:  
“Don’t take green,  
don’t take red;  
take the box that’s blue,  
with three crosses on its lid!”  

So she chose the blue one, just as the birds had sung. “Shame on you!”  
called the witch. “You’ll pay for that!”  

When the man’s daughter was about to leave, the witch flung a glowing  
iron bar straight at her. But she jumped behind the door and hid, so  
it didn’t strike her—for the little birds had told her what to do. She  
hurried on as fast as she could, but when she got to the apple tree,  
she heard a heavy rumble on the road behind her. It was the witch  
and her daughter, chasing after her. The girl was so afraid she didn’t  
know where to run.  

“Come to me,” said the apple tree, “and I’ll help you. Hide under  
my branches, so they don’t catch you, or they’ll take the box from you  
and tear you to pieces.”  

So she did. Soon the witch and her daughter arrived.  
“Have you seen a girl come by, Tree?” the witch asked.  
“Oh yes,” said the tree, “she ran by a while ago, but now she’s so far  
away, you’ll never catch her.”  

So the witch turned around and went back home.  

The girl went on a bit farther, but when she reached the ram, she heard  
that same heavy rumble once again, so she was as frightened and uneasy  
as ever—knowing well that the witch must have changed her mind.  

“Come here, and I’ll help you,” said the ram. “Hide under my wool so  
they don’t see you; otherwise, they’ll take the box from you and tear you  
to bits.”  

Sure enough, the witch soon came rushing over.  
“Have you seen a girl pass by?” she demanded of the ram.  
“Oh yes,” said the ram, “I saw one go by just a while ago, but she ran so  
fast you’ll never catch her.”  

So the witch turned around and went home.  

When the girl reached the cow, she heard the road thundering behind her.  
“Come to me,” said the cow, “and I’ll help you. Hide under my udder,  
or else the witch will catch you and take the box away from you  
and tear you to bits.”  

Not long afterward, the witch appeared again. “Have you seen a girl pass  
by here?” she asked the cow.  
“Yes,” said the cow, “I saw one just a while ago, but she’s long gone now.  
You’ll never catch her, for she ran too fast.”  

So the witch turned around and went home again.  

The girl walked on and on, far into the distance, and when she was nearly  
at the brush fence, she heard that rumbling once more. She was so frightened  
she didn’t know what to do, for the witch must have doubled back yet again.  

“Come here, and I’ll help you,” said the brush fence. “Crawl under my  
branches so they don’t see you; otherwise, they’ll take the box away  
from you and tear you to pieces.”  

She quickly crept under the branches of the brush fence.  
“Have you seen a girl pass by?” the witch asked the fence.  
“No, I haven’t seen a single girl,” replied the fence. It was so angry it  
bristled, then spread itself so wide that there was no way the witch  
could climb over. She had no choice but to go back home.  

When the man’s daughter made it back safely, both the old woman and  
her daughter became even more jealous of her than before, because now  
she was more beautiful, and so nicely dressed that it was a pleasure  
to see her. They didn’t allow her inside the house; they drove her to the  
pigsty, where she had to stay. So she washed it spotlessly clean, then  
opened the box she had received for her wages to see what was inside.  

No sooner did she open it than it overflowed with gold, silver, and  
wonderful things, so that the walls and ceiling sparkled with them.  
Indeed, the pigsty ended up looking far finer than the most splendid  
royal palace. When the stepmother and her daughter found out, they  
were beside themselves. They began asking what the work had been like.  
“Oh,” said the man’s daughter, “you can surely guess what it was like  
from the reward I got. They were such kind folk and such a wonderful  
mistress to serve, there isn’t another like them.”  

Well then, the old woman’s daughter was determined to set off so she  
could also get herself a box of gold. They sat down to spin again, but  
this time it was the old woman’s daughter who had to spin coarse bristles,  
and the man’s daughter who got to spin the flax—and whoever broke  
her thread first had to go into the well. It didn’t take long before the  
old woman’s daughter’s thread broke, of course, and into the well she went.  

It went just as before—she fell to the bottom, but she wasn’t hurt. There she  
found a lovely green meadow. After she had walked a bit, she came to the  
brush fence.  

“Please don’t tread so heavily on me, and I’ll help you in return,” it said.  
“Oh, what do I care about a bunch of sticks?” she said, making herself  
as heavy as she could and stomping on the fence until it creaked and cracked.  

Before long, she came to the cow, which stood bursting with milk.  
“Please be kind and milk me,” said the cow, “and I’ll help you in return.  
Drink whatever you want, but pour what’s left onto my hooves.”  

Yes, she did…in a way. She took the milk, drank until she couldn’t drink  
any more, but nothing remained to pour on the cow’s hooves; she just  
tossed the pail over the hill and walked away.  

After walking a little farther, she reached the ram that was dragging all  
its wool on the ground behind it.  

“Oh, please shear me,” said the ram, “and I’ll help you in return. Take  
as much wool as you want, but wrap the rest around my neck.”  

She did shear it, but so carelessly that she cut big holes in its skin, and  
she kept all the wool for herself.  

After a while, she came to the apple tree, which stood hunched over  
with its apples.  

“Please pick my apples,” said the tree, “so my branches can straighten  
up again—it’s such a strain to stand so bent over. Be careful not to  
ruin me; eat as many as you like, and lay the rest nicely by my roots,  
and I’ll help you in return.”  

She picked the ones she could reach and knocked down the others with  
the stick, but she went at it so roughly that she broke and tore off  
whole branches, stuffed herself with apples until she couldn’t eat  
another bite, and dumped the rest under the tree.  

Before long, she arrived at the farm where the witch lived. She asked  
for work. The witch said she didn’t want any housemaids, either they  
were no good at anything or they were far too clever and tricked her  
out of everything she had. The old woman’s daughter wouldn’t budge,  
though, and said she still wanted the job. So the witch agreed to hire her,  
if she could do the work.  

The first task was to fetch water in the sieve. The girl went to the well  
and tried to fill the sieve, but just as fast as she poured water in, it  
ran right out again. The birds sang:  
“Spread on clay,  
put in straw!  
Spread on clay,  
put in straw!”  

She cared nothing for the birds’ song; instead, she pelted them with clay  
so that they flew far away. She ended up carrying the empty sieve home  
and was severely scolded by the witch.  

Next, she was supposed to clean out the barn and milk the cows, but she  
thought that was far beneath her. She did go to the barn, but she couldn’t  
manage the giant shovel. The birds told her the same thing they’d told  
the other girl—that she should toss out a little, and the rest would  
follow—but she hurled the pitchfork at the birds instead.  

When she went to milk the cows, they were so restless that they kicked  
and stomped. Every time she got a little milk in the pail, they knocked  
it over. The birds sang:  
“Tiny stream,  
little sip,  
sprinkle a bit  
for all small birds!”  

But she beat and hit the cows and threw whatever she could at the birds,  
making such a sight of herself that she neither cleaned nor milked. When  
she came back in, she got both blows and bawling out from the witch. Then  
she was told to wash the black wool until it was white, but that was no  
easier for her.  

The witch thought this was all too much and put out the three boxes:  
red, green, and blue—telling the old woman’s daughter that she was no  
help at all and would have to leave. Still, the witch said she could pick  
one box as payment. The birds sang:  
“Don’t take green,  
don’t take red;  
take the box that’s blue,  
with three crosses on its lid!”  

She took no heed of the birds but chose the red one, because it glowed  
the brightest. Then she set off for home without anyone chasing her.  

She and her mother were overjoyed when she got home. They went straight  
into the main room and placed the box there, certain it was filled with  
gold and silver. They imagined the walls and ceiling would be gilded, too.  
But when they opened it, out came only snakes and toads! And whenever  
the old woman’s daughter opened her mouth, out came all manner of  
snakes and toads and every creeping creature imaginable, making it  
impossible to stay in the house with her.  

Such was the reward she received for serving the witch.
