Rich Per the Merchant

Once upon a time there was a man called Rich Per the Merchant, because he had traveled around selling wares and gathered a lot of money so that he had become a wealthy man. This Rich Per had a daughter, and he set her so high that all the suitors who came for her were turned away, for he thought none of them was good enough for her.

When this happened to everyone, eventually no one came, and as the years went by, Per became afraid that she might never marry.

“I really wonder,” he said to his wife, “why no one comes to propose to our daughter, who is so wealthy. It would be quite peculiar if no one should want her, because she has money and will inherit more. I believe I’ll travel to the astrologers and ask them whom she’s supposed to have; for not a living soul is coming here anymore.”

“How can the astrologers say anything about that, though?” asked his wife.

“Oh, they can read everything in the stars,” said Rich Per.

He took a lot of money to the astrologers and asked if they would look at the stars and tell him what man his daughter was destined to marry. The astrologers studied the stars, but said they could not see it. However, Per insisted that they look more carefully and absolutely tell him; he would pay them well for it. The astrologers then looked even more carefully and said that his daughter was to marry the miller’s child, who had just been born in the mill that lay right below Rich Per’s estate. Per gave the astrologers one hundred silver coins and traveled home with the news. He thought it was quite absurd that his daughter should marry one who had only just been born, and one of such low standing at that. He told this to his wife, adding, “I wonder if they might sell me that boy, so we can be rid of him.”

“Yes, I think they would,” said his wife. “They are poor people, after all.”

Per then went down to the mill and asked the boy’s mother if she would sell him her son; she would get plenty of money for it. No, she absolutely would not. “I can’t see why you won’t,” said Rich Per the Merchant. “You live in nothing but poverty, and I can’t imagine this boy will ease that.” But she was so fond of her son that she refused to part with him. When the miller came in, Per made the same request to him, promising to give him six hundred silver coins for the boy, so they could buy themselves a farm and no longer have to grind grain for other people, or go hungry when there was no water to power the mill. The miller liked the sound of that; he talked with his wife, and so Rich Per got the boy. The mother wept and carried on, but Per consoled her by saying the child would be well cared for. He made them promise not to inquire after him, because he intended to send him far away, to learn foreign languages.

When Rich Per the Merchant got home with the boy, he had a small chest made, so finely crafted that it was a delight to see. He sealed it with tar, laid the miller’s boy inside, locked it, and set it afloat in the river, which carried it away. “Now I’m rid of him,” thought Rich Per.

But after the chest had sailed far down the river, it drifted into a water chute at another mill site and through the chute down onto the mill’s water wheel, stopping the mill from turning. The man who owned that mill went down to check what was stopping it, found the chest, and took it back upstairs.

When he came home to his wife at lunchtime, he said, “I really wonder what could be inside this chest—it floated onto my mill wheel through the chute and stopped the mill today.”  
“We’ll soon find out,” said his wife. “The key is still in the lock—just open it.”  

When they unlocked it, there lay the most beautiful child anyone could wish to see, and they were so happy that they decided to keep him. They themselves had no children, and they were old enough that they no longer expected to have any.

After some time had passed, Rich Per the Merchant again started to wonder why no suitors came for his daughter, who was so rich and had so much money. But no one came, so he traveled again to the astrologers, offering them a large sum of money if they could tell him whom his daughter would marry.

“We have already told you that she will marry that miller’s boy down there,” said the astrologers.

“Yes, that may be,” said Rich Per, “but something has happened to him, so he’s gone for good. If you could only tell me who my daughter will have for a husband, I’d gladly give you two hundred silver coins.”

The astrologers studied the stars again, but then they grew angry and said, “She shall have that same miller’s boy you sent down the river and tried to do away with, for he is still alive and is staying in the mill that lies thus-and-so far below.”

Rich Per the Merchant gave them two hundred silver coins for the prophecy and thought about whether he could still rid himself of that miller’s boy.

The first thing Per did when he got home was to head to the mill. By now the boy was big enough that he had gone through his church confirmation, and he helped around the mill. He had grown into a handsome lad.

“Couldn’t you let me have that boy?” Rich Per asked the miller.  
“No, I can’t,” the miller replied. “I’ve raised him as my own, and he’s behaved well so that I can now rely on his help here at the mill. I’m getting old and weak myself.”  
“Yes, we’re both in the same situation,” said Rich Per, “and that is why I’d like someone I can train for trade. If you let me have him, I’ll give you six hundred silver coins so you can buy a farm and live in peace in your old age.”  

When the miller heard that, he let Rich Per the Merchant take the boy.

The two of them traveled far and wide with trade goods, until they came to an inn at the edge of a great forest. From there, Per sent the boy home with a letter for his wife—it wasn’t far if you went straight through the forest—and told him to say that she must do what was written in the letter as soon as possible. But in the letter, it said that she must immediately light a large bonfire and throw the miller’s boy onto it, and if she did not, she herself would be burned alive.

The boy set out across the forest with the letter. Towards evening, he arrived at a farm deep in the woods. He went inside, but found no people there. He noticed a bed made up in one room and stretched himself across it. He had placed the letter under his hatband and laid his hat over his face.

When the robbers who lived on that farm—there were twelve of them—came home and saw the boy sleeping on the bed, they wondered who he might be. One of them took the letter, opened it, and read it.  
“Hmm,” he said. “This is from Per the Merchant, trying to pull a trick; but now let’s play him a trick of our own, because it would be cruel if that old scoundrel got to finish off such a fine, handsome boy.”  

So the robbers wrote another letter to Per the Merchant’s wife, tucked it under the hatband while the boy slept, and in that letter they stated that she must immediately hold a wedding for their daughter and the miller’s boy, give them horses and livestock and equipment, and set them up properly on the farm he owned under the ridge. If it was not done by the time Per got home, she would be punished.

The next day the robbers let the boy go. When he arrived and gave the letter to Per’s wife, he said he was to greet her from Per the Merchant and say she should do exactly what was in the letter right away.

“You must have served him well,” said Per’s wife to the miller’s boy, “since he can write so differently now. When you two left, he was so enraged at you that he didn’t know how to do away with you.”  

She immediately prepared for a wedding and sent him and Per’s daughter to the farm under the ridge with horses, livestock, and every kind of tool.

Not long after, Per the Merchant came home again, and the first thing he asked was if she had done what he had commanded in the letter.

“Yes, I did think it was strange, but I didn’t dare do otherwise,” she said. Then Per asked where their daughter was.  
“You can surely guess where she is,” said his wife. “She’s with him, on the farm under the ridge, just as it said in the letter.”  

When Per heard how matters stood and saw the letter, he became so furious he almost exploded. At once, he rushed up to the farm to see the young couple.

“It’s all well and good, my boy, that you have my daughter,” he said to the miller’s boy, “but if you mean to keep her, you must travel to the Dragon of Dybenfart and bring me three feathers from his tail, for the one who has them can get whatever he wishes.”

“Where will I find him, then?” asked his new son-in-law.  
“That’s your problem; I don’t know,” said Per the Merchant.

The boy set out bravely on his way. After he had walked for some time, he came to a royal palace. “I’ll go in and ask for directions,” he thought, “because such people will know more about the world than others, and perhaps I can find out where to go.”  

The King asked him where he came from and what his business was.  
“I’m on my way to the Dragon of Dybenfart to get three feathers from his tail, if only I can find him,” said the boy.  
“That will take some luck,” said the King, “for I’ve never heard of anyone who has returned from him. But if you do find him, would you also ask on my behalf why I can’t get fresh water in my well? I’ve dug it over and over again, but I can never get clean water in it.”

“Yes, I’ll do that,” said the boy. He ate and slept well at the palace, and was given both food for his journey and money when he left.

Toward evening, he reached another royal palace. When he entered the kitchen, the King himself came out and asked where he was from and what his errand was.

“I’m on my way to the Dragon of Dybenfart to get three feathers from his tail,” said the boy.  
“That will take some luck,” said the King, “for I’ve not heard of anyone returning from him. But if you do manage to reach him, would you ask on my behalf where my daughter is, who disappeared years ago? I’ve looked and asked in all the churches, but no one knows anything.”  
“I’ll do so,” said the boy. He was well hosted at that palace, too. When he went on his way, they gave him food and money.

As evening approached once again, he came to yet another royal palace. The Queen came out to the kitchen and asked where he was from and what his errand was.

“I’m going to the Dragon of Dybenfart, to get three feathers from his tail,” said the boy.  
“That truly will take some luck,” said the Queen, “for I’ve never heard of anyone returning from him. But if you find him, could you ask on my behalf where I can find my golden keys, which I have lost?”  
“I certainly will,” said the boy.

After he had walked a while, he came upon a wide, deep river. As he stood wondering how he might get across it or if he should walk along it until he found a bridge, an old, hunched man appeared and asked where he was heading.

“I’m trying to find the Dragon of Dybenfart,” said the boy, “if only someone could tell me where to look.”  
“I can tell you that,” said the old man, “for I ferry across everyone who is on their way to him. He lives just up ahead; once you get on the hill, you’ll see his castle. And if you manage to speak with him, would you also ask on my behalf how long I must go back and forth ferrying people over?”  
“I will,” said the boy.

The old man carried him on his back across the river, and when the boy reached the top of the hill, he spotted the castle and went inside. The princess was there alone.

“Oh dear, can Christian folk even come here?” she said. “No one has come here since I arrived, and it’s best you leave at once, for when the Dragon comes home, he’ll smell you and swallow you on the spot—making me miserable as well.”

“No,” said the boy. “I won’t leave before I get three feathers from his tail.”  

“That you’ll never get,” said the princess. But the boy refused to leave; he wanted to wait for the Dragon and at least try for those feathers and also get answers to his questions.

“Well, since you’re so determined, we’ll see if I can help you,” said the princess. “Try to lift that sword hanging on the wall.”  

But the boy couldn’t even budge it.

“Then have a sip from this flask,” said the princess.

After he had sat a while, he tried again, and this time he barely managed to move the sword.  

“You’ll have to take another sip,” said the princess, “and then tell me your errand.”

He took another sip and then explained that one King had asked him to find out why he couldn’t get clean water in his well; another King asked about the whereabouts of his daughter, who’s been missing for years; yet another Queen wanted to know where she could find her lost golden keys; and, lastly, the ferryman down by the river needed to know how long he must continue ferrying people across.

When he now took hold of the sword, he could lift it, and after another sip he could even swing it.

“To make sure the Dragon doesn’t do away with you immediately, you must hide under the bed when evening comes,” said the princess, “for he’ll be home soon, and you must lie there so still that he doesn’t notice you. When we go to lie down, I’ll start to ask him these questions one by one; listen carefully and remember his answers. Stay under the bed until everything has quieted down and the Dragon has gone back to sleep. Then crawl out silently and take the sword with you. When he gets up, you must quickly chop off his head with a single strike, and at once pluck the three feathers from his tail, or else, if he has any chance, he’ll tear them out himself so no one else can benefit from them.”

A little while after the boy had crawled under the bed, the Dragon came home.  

“It smells like a Christian man’s bones in here!” said the Dragon.  
“Oh yes, a raven flew over with a man’s bone in its beak and landed on the roof,” said the princess. “That must be what you smell.”  
“Ah, I see,” said the Dragon.

Then the princess served dinner, and after they ate, they lay down. But soon, the princess slept restlessly, and all at once she shrieked, “Ow!”  

“What’s wrong with you?” asked the Dragon.  
“Oh, I’m so restless,” she said, “and I had such a strange dream.”  
“What did you dream?” asked the Dragon.  
“I dreamt that a King came here, asking how he could get clean water in his well,” she said.  
“Ah, he should know that himself,” replied the Dragon. “If he digs up the well and removes the old rotten log at the bottom, he’ll have clean water again. Now lie still, and don’t dream anymore.”

After a while, the princess tossed and turned again, and then she shrieked, “Ow!” a second time.  

“What is it now?” said the Dragon.  
“I’m so restless, and I had such a peculiar dream,” said the princess.  
“Well, you do dream a lot,” said the Dragon. “What did you dream this time?”  
“I dreamt that a King came here, asking where his daughter was, the one who vanished so many years ago,” said the princess.  

“That is you,” said the Dragon. “But he will never lay eyes on you again. Now don’t go disturbing my sleep anymore, or I’ll break your ribs.”  

The princess hadn’t lain still for long before she became uneasy once again, and suddenly she shrieked for the third time, “Ow!”  

“Oh, is it the same thing again? What’s wrong this time?” the Dragon asked, now so agitated and sleepy that he was close to flying into a rage.  
“You mustn’t be angry with me,” said the princess, “I can’t help it—I had another strange dream.”  
“Even the devil can’t match this dreaming! What did you dream now?” said the Dragon.  
“I dreamt a Queen was asking you where she could find her golden keys, which she has lost,” said the princess.  

“Oh, she should look among the bushes where she lay that time—she knows when—then she’ll find them,” said the Dragon. “But let me sleep now. No more dreams!”

They settled down to sleep once more, but before long the princess grew restless yet again and cried out, “Ow!”  

“You won’t get any better until I snap your neck,” said the Dragon, now so furious that sparks flew from him. “What’s bothering you again?”  
“Please don’t be angry at me,” said the princess. “I can’t help it—I had such a peculiar dream.”  
“All this dreaming is too much,” said the Dragon. “What was it this time?”  
“I dreamt that the ferryman by the crossing down below asked how long he must keep ferrying,” said the princess.  

“That foolish beast can end it soon enough,” said the Dragon. “All he has to do is toss the next person who wants to cross right into the middle of the river and say, ‘Now you ferry people until someone replaces you!’ But let me be done with these dreams now, or there’ll be a different dance in store.”

After that, the princess let him sleep in peace. As soon as it was quiet and the boy heard the Dragon snoring, he crept out. Before daybreak, the Dragon got up, but he had scarcely set both feet on the floor when the boy chopped off his head in one blow and quickly plucked out the three feathers. Great was their joy! The boy and the princess gathered as much silver and gold and other valuables as they could carry.  

When they reached the river, they kept the ferryman so busy carrying all the riches across that he forgot to ask what the Dragon had said. By the time everything was over on the other side, along with the boy and the princess, the ferryman exclaimed, “Oh, that’s right—did you ask the Dragon what I told you to?”  

“Yes, I did,” said the boy. “He said that the next time someone wants to get across, you should toss them into the middle of the river and tell them, ‘Now you ferry people until someone replaces you!’ Then you’ll be free.”  

“Ah, curse you!” said the ferryman. “Had you told me that sooner, I would have made you let me go!”

When they reached the first royal palace, the Queen asked if he had remembered to ask the Dragon about her golden keys.

“Yes,” said the boy, whispering so that only she could hear, “he said you should look among the bushes where you lay that time—you know when.”  
“Shh, say nothing,” said the Queen, and she gave the boy one hundred silver coins.

When he arrived at the second royal palace, the King asked if he had asked the Dragon the question he’d entrusted him with.

“Yes,” said the boy, “I asked, and here is your daughter.”  
The King was overjoyed and would have gladly given the miller’s boy the princess and half the kingdom. But since the boy was already married, the King gave him two hundred silver coins, plus horses and a carriage and as much gold and silver as he could carry.

At the third royal palace, the King came out and asked if he’d inquired about the matter he had asked him to.

“Yes,” said the boy, “the Dragon told me you must dig out the well and remove the old rotten log in the bottom, then you’ll have fresh water.”  

So the King gave him three hundred silver coins.

From there he traveled straight home. He was so loaded with gold and silver and so fine in his appearance that everything about him shone. Now he was far wealthier than Per the Merchant. When Per got the feathers, he had nothing more to say against the marriage. But when he saw all that wealth, he asked if there was really so much in the Dragon’s lair.

“Yes,” said the boy, “there’s even more than I could carry—there’s so much it would fill many wagons. If you want to go there, I guarantee there’s enough for you, too.”

Indeed, Rich Per wanted to go. The boy told him the way so clearly that he wouldn’t need to ask once, but added, “It’s best to leave your horses on this side of the river, because that old ferryman will certainly carry you across.”

So Per left, taking a large supply of food and many horses with him. But he left the horses behind at the river, as the boy advised. The ferryman took him on his back, and when they were partway across, he tossed him into the middle of the river and said, “Now stay here and ferry people until someone replaces you!”

And if no one has freed him, Rich Per the Merchant is still there ferrying people across to this very day.
